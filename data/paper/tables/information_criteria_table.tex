\caption{AIC, BIC and Bayes factor of each model each run discovered and of each seed model, all fitted to the domain's pooled data: the three runs' final data for subjective randomness ($n = 23{,}104$ trials from 359 participants) and number sequence generalization ($n = 22{,}680$ responses), and the campaign's pooled data for reference games ($n = 70{,}841$ trials). AIC $= -2\log\hat{L} + 2k$ and BIC $= -2\log\hat{L} + k\ln n$, with $\log\hat{L}$ the maximized log-likelihood (L-BFGS from several starts). $\log \mathrm{BF}$ is the model's log marginal likelihood (the likelihood integrated against its priors) minus that of the domain's best seed, which therefore scores 0. Arrows mark the better direction (lower AIC and BIC, higher $\log \mathrm{BF}$); the best value in each domain is in bold. The discovered subjective-randomness models have person-level parameters: their $\hat{L}$ integrates them out (importance sampling around a Laplace approximation, a lower bound in expectation) and $k$ counts only the population-level parameters (14 to 17); counting all 1{,}450 to 2{,}171 person-level parameters as free instead gives each a higher BIC than every seed. Marginal likelihoods are estimated by importance sampling with a multivariate-$t$ proposal fitted to posterior draws (centered on the posterior mode for number sequence generalization), nested for the person-level parameters; the discovered subjective-randomness models' estimates are poorly sampled and so are, with high probability, lower bounds up to a few nats; the estimates for two discovered number-sequence models have small effective sample sizes (14 and 28) but agree with their Laplace approximations to within about 1 nat.}
