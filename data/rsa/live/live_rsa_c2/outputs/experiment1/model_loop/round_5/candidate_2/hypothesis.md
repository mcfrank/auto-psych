When interpreting referring expressions or anticipating referents on unprompted trials, listeners reason about what the speaker is likely to talk about: speakers prefer to describe referents that possess high communicative codability, meaning they can be reliably distinguished from context competitors using available vocabulary. Listeners assign prior probability to candidate referents in proportion to the speaker's maximal communicative discriminability, penalizing subsumed intermediate items whose descriptions remain ambiguous and elevating uniquely nameable targets and baselines. Pragmatic listeners invert this codability-grounded speaker at depth 2, explaining why intermediate objects are strongly disfavored on unprompted trials while uniquely distinguishing features elevate competitor choices.
