When formulating referring expressions in visual reference games, speakers evaluate candidate utterances against perceptual distractor interference: words that are shared with visually complex, feature-rich distractors carry higher communicative cost because feature-dense distractors act as visual attractors that capture listener attention. Pragmatic listeners invert this distractor-aware speaker at depth 2, expecting speakers to avoid words that point toward salient competitors, naturally favoring simpler referents in cross-cutting displays and penalizing intermediate referents whose features point toward visually prominent maximal exemplars.
