Listeners interpret utterances through pragmatic preemption: they recognize that speakers prioritize unambiguous descriptors that uniquely pick out an intended referent, so the presence of an unambiguous distinguishing feature preempts the speaker from using an ambiguous shared descriptor. When hearing a shared descriptor, listeners infer that the speaker intended a referent lacking a unique distinguishing label rather than a competitor for which a uniquely identifying descriptor was available. Across displays with redundant features and large feature gaps, this preemption mechanism prevents the artificial dilution of pragmatic inferences while maintaining calibrated expectations for candidate referents.
