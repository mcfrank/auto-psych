In communicative reference, foundational literal interpretation evaluates candidate referents through proportional feature coverage: the applicability of a single-word descriptor scales with the fraction of the referent's total visual features that the word accounts for, treating minimal-feature objects as prototypical exemplars while penalizing multi-featured objects in proportion to their descriptive dilution. A pragmatic listener inverts a communicative speaker who anticipates this coverage-weighted interpretation, which sharply elevates minimal-feature target selection on redundant-feature trials and curtails intermediate competitor choices across display sizes. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with the incumbent model on ambiguous displays containing large feature discrepancies—such as a shared descriptor matching a two-feature target competing against a four-feature object—where proportional feature coverage decisively favors the two-feature target over the four-feature competitor.
