In reference games, uniquely distinguishing referring expressions impose cognitive specificity and verification costs on speakers, whereas widely shared features are salient and low-cost to produce. Speakers weigh communicative utility against this specificity cost, frequently choosing a shared or ubiquitous word even when describing a feature-rich object rather than paying the cost to articulate its unique feature. Pragmatic listeners invert this cost-sensitive speaker, expecting that hearing a shared word does not rule out feature-rich items, which attenuates over-confident scalar implicatures and elevates feature-rich choices under ambiguous or ubiquitous expressions.
