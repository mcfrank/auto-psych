Listeners expect speakers to refer to objects with high referential codability—objects that possess distinguishing descriptive features that make them viable communicative targets in the scene context. Before hearing any informative word, listeners evaluate each referent by its communicative affordance, defined as the total discriminative capacity of the available expressions that describe it, strongly favoring feature-rich and unique entities over featureless or duplicate ones. When an utterance is heard, listeners invert an informative speaker using this codability prior, naturally tempering scalar exclusion on two-object displays, explaining the preference for complex referents under ubiquitous words, and suppressing choices of intermediate feature-count competitors.
