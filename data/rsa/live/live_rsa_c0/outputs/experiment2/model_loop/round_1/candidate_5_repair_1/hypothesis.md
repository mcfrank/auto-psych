Listeners interpret referring expressions under capacity-limited executive inhibitory control while reasoning about speakers who incur cognitive specificity costs for producing uniquely distinguishing expressions. When an utterance is referentially ambiguous, resolving competing candidates depletes executive inhibitory control, causing distractor suppression to degrade with candidate load; simultaneously, speakers frequently avoid the effort of producing highly specific alternative words for complex referents, preferring broader shared expressions. Pragmatic listeners invert this cost-sensitive speaker, recognizing that ambiguous expressions do not rule out feature-rich competitors whose unique distinguishing features were costly to specify, directly moderating excessive scalar implicatures on two-object displays and elevating competitor choices.
