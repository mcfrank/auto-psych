Listeners interpret referring expressions through contextual contrastive selectivity: they engage in pragmatic scalar inference only to the extent that an utterance isolates a contrast set against non-matching distractors in the visual context, while treating contextually unselective expressions that describe all scene objects as non-discriminating mentions resolved by perceptual feature complexity. As display size and feature overlaps increase, selectivity naturally scales with the proportion of objects ruled out, preserving robust pragmatic exclusion in large distinctive scenes while remaining sensitive to partial ambiguities. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with standard pragmatic models on two-object ambiguous displays where the heard word applies to both objects, because standard models predict over-confident scalar exclusion favoring the minimal target, whereas contrastive selectivity recognizes the utterance as non-discriminating and suspends scalar exclusion, lowering target choices to match human behavior.
