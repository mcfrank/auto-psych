Listeners interpret referring expressions by reasoning about speakers whose lexical production is governed by lateral inhibition among competing communicative alternatives. When describing an object with multiple features, highly informative distinguishing words laterally suppress broader shared words within the speaker's lexicon, making speakers disproportionately unlikely to produce a shared word when intending a feature-rich referent with distinguishing options. Pragmatic listeners invert this competitive speaker, inferring that hearing a shared word provides strong evidence for referents that lack distinguishing alternatives, which naturally sharpens scalar implicatures against complex competitors in multi-object scenes while calibrating referent selection under ambiguity.
