Listeners interpret referring expressions by reasoning about contrastive preemption: when an intended referent possesses distinguishing alternative features that could have ruled out competing candidates sharing the heard word, the speaker's failure to produce those distinguishing alternatives strongly preempts that referent. Conversely, for a minimal pragmatic target lacking distinguishing alternatives, the shared word was a communicative necessity rather than a gratuitous ambiguity. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with standard models on four-object displays where a word matches both a two-feature target and an all-feature competitor: while standard models predict near-indifference due to the competitor's high feature count and typicality, contrastive preemption recognizes that the competitor had multiple distinguishing alternatives that would have ruled out the target, elevating target choices to match human behavior.
