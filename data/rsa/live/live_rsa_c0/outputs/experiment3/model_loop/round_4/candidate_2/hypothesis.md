Listeners interpret referring expressions by evaluating candidate referents within a contrastive feature hierarchy, grounded in Clark's Principle of Contrast: when a spoken word applies to multiple candidate objects in a visual scene, listeners penalize subsumed intermediate candidates whose visual features are strictly nested within a more complete competitor without possessing any distinctive features of their own. Because intermediate candidates are strictly dominated by the minimal pragmatic target on communicative parsimony and by maximal competitors on visual completeness, speakers avoid intending subsumed compromise referents. Pragmatic listeners invert this subsumption-sensitive speaker under capacity-limited executive attention, resolving the over-prediction of intermediate candidates in multi-match displays while preserving choices of minimal pragmatic targets and maximal exemplars.
