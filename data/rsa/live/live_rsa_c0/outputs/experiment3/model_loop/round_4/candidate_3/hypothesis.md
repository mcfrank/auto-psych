Listeners interpret referring expressions through boundary polarization: when a referring expression matches multiple candidate referents that vary in feature complexity, evaluative attention is anchored on the range extrema—the minimal pragmatic target and the maximal category prototype—while candidates located in the interior of the feature range suffer cognitive suppression due to lack of boundary contrast. Pragmatic listeners invert an informative speaker who avoids producing referring expressions for referents located in this suppressed interior of the candidate feature range, leading them to strongly avoid intermediate-feature competitors and polarize choices between the minimal target and the maximal prototype. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with standard models on displays where three candidates match the heard utterance with strictly distinct feature counts (such as a three-object display with one, two, and three features hearing the shared word), where standard models predict intermediate candidates to receive substantial choice probability between the minimal and maximal candidates, whereas boundary polarization strongly suppresses the intermediate competitor to match human behavior.
