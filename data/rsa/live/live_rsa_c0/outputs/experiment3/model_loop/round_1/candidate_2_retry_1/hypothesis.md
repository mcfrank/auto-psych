Speakers in reference games are regret-averse communicators who penalize referring expressions in proportion to counterfactual communicative regret—the shortfall in listener recovery probability compared to the most distinguishing alternative expression available for the intended referent. Pragmatic listeners invert this regret-averse speaker under capacity-limited executive attention, inferring that hearing an ambiguous word provides strong evidence for referents that lacked superior alternatives, while strongly precluding complex competitors that possessed uniquely distinguishing words. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with standard models on four-object displays where an utterance describes both a multi-feature target and an all-feature competitor: because the all-feature competitor had unique alternative words that would have guaranteed recovery, producing the shared word incurs substantial communicative regret, elevating target choices to match human behavior while moderating scalar exclusion when alternatives are equally ambiguous.
