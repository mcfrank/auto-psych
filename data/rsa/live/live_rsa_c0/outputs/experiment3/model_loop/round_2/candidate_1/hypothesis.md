Listeners interpret referring expressions with an a priori expectation that speakers refer to objects possessing salient visual features, grounded in psychological feature search asymmetry: visual attention and discourse reference are captured by the presence of distinctive visual features, not the mere absence of shared ones. Before hearing an informative word and during pragmatic referent resolution, listeners form an expectation over objects based on feature-presence asymmetry, prioritizing objects that instantiate unique visual singletons and contrastive features that pop out against the scene background. This feature-presence salience prior naturally elevates choices of feature-rich objects on uninformative mumble trials, while giving distinctive targets with unique alternative features a decisive prior advantage that resolves scalar under-prediction under ambiguous referring expressions.
