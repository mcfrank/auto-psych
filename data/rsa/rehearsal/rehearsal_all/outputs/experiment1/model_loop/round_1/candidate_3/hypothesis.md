Speakers evaluate candidate referring expressions against the best available description for the intended referent, actively suppressing ambiguous words when a more uniquely identifying alternative is available (lexical preemption). When a speaker intends a referent that cannot be uniquely named, no superior alternative exists to preempt the shared expression, allowing that word to be produced without penalty. Pragmatic listeners invert this preemption-aware speaker, inferring that hearing an ambiguous word implies the speaker intended a referent that could not be uniquely named rather than one with an unselected distinctive feature.
