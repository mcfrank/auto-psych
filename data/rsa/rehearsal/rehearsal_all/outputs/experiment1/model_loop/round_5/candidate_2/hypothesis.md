Communicative speakers evaluate referring expressions by the alternative nameability of competing display items, avoiding descriptions shared with competitors that lack distinctive labels of their own. When a distractor cannot be uniquely named, it relies entirely on shared descriptions and heavily competes for the heard word; in contrast, distractors that possess uniquely identifying alternatives are preempted from using the shared expression. Pragmatic listeners invert this competitor-nameability-aware speaker to resolve referential ambiguity, expecting ambiguous descriptions to identify referents whose competitors could not be uniquely named.
