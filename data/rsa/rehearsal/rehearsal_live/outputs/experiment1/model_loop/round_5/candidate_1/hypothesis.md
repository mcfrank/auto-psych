Listeners interpret referring expressions through contrastive inference, expecting speakers to produce descriptive features specifically to resolve minimal contrast pairs against contextually similar competitors that lack the asserted property. On displays where a candidate referent shares all background features with an adjacent competitor lacking the uttered word (such as a circle-and-hat item contrasted with a hat distractor), this mechanism predicts strong choice of the contrast partner, disagreeing sharply with the twin-contrast solitary oddity model which predicts a uniform split on displays without duplicate twins. On uninformative prior trials without an asserted word, listeners have no contrastive focus and distribute their choices uniformly.
