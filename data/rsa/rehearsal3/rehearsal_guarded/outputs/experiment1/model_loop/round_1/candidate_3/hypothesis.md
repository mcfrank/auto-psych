When selecting referring expressions, speakers balance communicative informativeness against lexical accessibility, experiencing lower cognitive production cost for high-prevalence features that appear frequently in the visual scene compared to rare, hyper-specific features. Candidate words receive an accessibility advantage proportional to their extension across context objects. Pragmatic listeners invert this accessibility-sensitive speaker, correctly discounting negative implicatures because a speaker may choose a common, accessible word even when a more specific description was logically available.
