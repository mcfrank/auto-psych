Listeners interpret referring expressions by reasoning about visual salience grounded in feature surprisal, expecting speakers to refer to objects whose visual features are rare and informative in the context. Rather than treating all objects as equally likely a priori, listeners compute an attentional prior over candidate referents by accumulating the Shannon self-information of each object's features relative to their scene frequencies, filtering out ubiquitous background features while highlighting contextually rare attributes. On uninformative trials without a distinguishing word, listeners choose the object possessing the highest total feature surprisal, and when hearing a descriptive word, they integrate this surprisal prior with pragmatic reasoning at depth 2.
