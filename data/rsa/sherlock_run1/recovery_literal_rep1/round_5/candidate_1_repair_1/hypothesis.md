Listeners interpret referring expressions by evaluating candidate referents under the principle of lexical contrast: when an utterance applies to multiple candidate objects, listeners penalize candidates in proportion to the contextual specificity of their unmentioned alternative features. Because an informative speaker would have preferentially used a distinctive alternative label to single out an intended referent, hearing a shared label directs the listener toward the candidate that lacks distinguishing alternative names. On symmetric displays where competing candidates have the same total feature count but differ in whether their alternative features are scene-unique or shared (such as in Sikos et al., 2021), this mechanism predicts a strong preference for the referent lacking a unique label, directly contradicting the 50/50 prediction of naive speaker models.
