Listeners interpret referring expressions by assuming speakers tailor their need for discriminative specificity to an object's contextual prominence: speakers readily use shared, general labels when referring to prominent focal objects (highlighted by color, feature complexity, or affective framing), but demand highly discriminating features when referring to background objects. This prominence-dependent specificity demand allows ambiguous words to point toward salient objects without eliminating pragmatic contrast for non-salient alternatives. The listener inverts this prominence-sensitive production process, combining linguistic evidence with prior expectations over visual and evaluative prominence.
