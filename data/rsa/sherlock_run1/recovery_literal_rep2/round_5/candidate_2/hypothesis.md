Speakers incur a cognitive production cost when retrieving and producing uniquely distinguishing features, preferring to describe referents using shared, accessible descriptors unless the communicative benefit overcomes this cost. When interpreting referring expressions, listeners reason inversely about this cost-sensitive speaker: upon hearing a shared feature, listeners do not strongly exclude referents that possess unuttered distinguishing features because the speaker had a cost incentive to avoid producing them. Under a softmax decision policy, this balances informativeness against production effort, explaining systematic conservatism in human referent choice.
