People interpret referential expressions through direct mutual exclusivity rather than recursive Theory-of-Mind speaker simulation, penalizing candidate objects that possess additional competing features. On complex implicature displays where two objects share the heard word and each possess one alternative feature (such as target-complex displays in Mayn & Demberg), this mechanism predicts an exact fifty-fifty choice because both objects are equally complex, disagreeing sharply with the current best model's asymmetric preference driven by second-order recursive speaker simulation. On simple implicature displays, it naturally favors the object with fewer features because that object has no competing labels.
