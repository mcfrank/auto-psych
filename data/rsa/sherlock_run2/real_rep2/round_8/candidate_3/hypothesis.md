Speakers choose referring expressions to minimize the expected perceptual error between the intended referent and the referent chosen by a literal listener, penalizing words that risk confusing the target with visually distant distractors. When an ambiguous referring expression is heard, listeners invert this error-minimizing speaker, expecting descriptions whose potential misidentifications are perceptually close to the target. This mechanism disagrees most sharply with the current best model on displays featuring duplicate twin objects and an isolated singleton when a shared feature is named: while the best model's perceptual isolation prior boosts the singleton to over 50% choice probability, expected error minimization predicts listeners will prefer the twins (over 70% combined probability), because a speaker intending a twin incurs only half the expected perceptual error (confusing one twin with another causes zero feature discrepancy) compared to a speaker intending the singleton (confusing the singleton with a twin causes a severe feature discrepancy).
