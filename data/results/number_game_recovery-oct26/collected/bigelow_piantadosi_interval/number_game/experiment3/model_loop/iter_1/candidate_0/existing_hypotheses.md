# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## inflated_size_familiar_rule_prior  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3425.1

Refinement of inflated_concept_size_principle: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100 (evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger- or weaker-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), and in the size principle they still perceive every concept as larger than it really is by an inferred constant number of members. The one change, taken from rule_size_prior_anchored_evidence, is that even before seeing any examples they find small, exotic rules ("powers of 4", "powers of 8", "cubes") less plausible than large, familiar ones ("even numbers", "ends in 4", "multiples of 8"), with each rule's prior belief rising as an inferred power of how many numbers it contains. This preference is the same whatever the number of examples, while the perceived-size bias matters more the more examples are shown. So after sets where a tiny rule sits inside a larger one (8, 64; 64, 4; 2, 8; 16, 32, 2, 8), people generalize to the larger rule's members more than either source model predicts, at every set size; an exponent of zero recovers inflated_concept_size_principle.

## volatile_changepoint_inflated_size  — rank 1, 0.6 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3425.7

Refinement of volatile_concept_changepoint: people still read the examples one at a time, updating a Bayesian belief over weighted rules and ranges while allowing that the program may switch concept between outputs (with everything else as in that model), but in the size principle they do not take a concept's size at face value — they perceive every concept as larger than it is by an inferred constant number of members. The one change, taken from inflated_concept_size_principle, is this perceived-size inflation, which shrinks the evidential advantage of tiny rules like "powers of 2" over larger consistent ones like "even numbers", so after all-power-of-2 sets people generalize to the remaining powers of 2 less sharply relative to other even numbers than the volatile-concept model predicts; zero inflation recovers it.

## volatile_range_width_inflation  — rank 2, 8.5 ± 3.6 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3433.5

Refinement of volatile_changepoint_inflated_size (statistically tied with the best model): people still read the examples one at a time, updating a Bayesian belief over 42 weighted rules (with a rule-size prior) and all ranges of 1-100 while allowing that the program may switch concept between outputs (with a stronger- or weaker-weighted first example, a decision gain and a shifted criterion), and they still perceive arithmetic rules as larger than they really are by an inferred number of members. The one change is how people discount the evidence for narrow magnitude ranges: instead of applying their own weaker (or stronger) evidence weight to ranges, they perceive every range as wider than it is by its own inferred number of extra members, and rules and ranges share one evidence weight. A uniform weight scales the advantage of a narrow range at every width, whereas added width wipes it out among narrow ranges and keeps it for wide ones. So a tight range just around clustered examples (52, 57, 55) loses most of its edge over a moderately wider one, and generalization reaches nearby numbers (48, 62), while very wide ranges are still rejected. With equal inflations for rules and ranges, the model becomes volatile_changepoint_inflated_size with the range evidence weight tied to the rule weight.

## conservative_partial_belief_updating  — rank 3, 17.4 ± 6.1 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3442.5

People read the examples one at a time and revise their belief about the program's concept after each one, but conservatively: after the first example (judged by the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules with a size-dependent prior and all ranges of 1-100, evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger- or weaker-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), each later example moves their belief only an inferred fraction of the way from what they believed before toward what full Bayesian updating on that example would give. Because the old belief is kept as a share of the new one, broad concepts that fit the early examples (e.g. "even numbers" or a wide range after 16) retain belief even after later examples single out a tiny rule ("powers of 2" after 16, 8, 64), so people generalize to the remaining powers of 2 less sharply relative to other even numbers, and the earlier examples shape belief more than the later ones; an update fraction of one recovers full Bayesian updating.

## inflated_concept_size_principle  — rank 4, 18.9 ± 8.2 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3444.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100, evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but they do not take a concept's size at face value in the size principle: they perceive every concept as larger than it is by an inferred constant number of members, so tiny concepts such as "powers of 8" (8, 64) or "powers of 4" (4, 16, 64) lose most of their advantage over moderately larger ones while the contrast between moderate and broad concepts is largely kept. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives on sets that fit a tiny rule nested inside a larger one (8, 64; 64, 4; 16, 8; 16, 32, 2, 8): the best model generalizes tightly to the tiny rule's other members (16 after 64, 4), whereas this model predicts broader generalization to the larger rule (multiples of 8, ends in 4, even numbers) and fewer yeses to the tiny rule's own members.

## volatile_concept_changepoint  — rank 5, 20.6 ± 6.7 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3445.7

People read the examples one at a time and update their belief about the program's concept after each one, but they believe the program might switch to a different concept between outputs: before each new example they allow, with an inferred switch probability, that the concept has been re-drawn from their prior. This belief in a volatile generator makes recent examples count more than earlier ones in a graded, Bayes-consistent way — a concept supported only by the last few examples keeps substantial belief even when an earlier example contradicts it — while everything else (weighted rules and ranges, size principle with tempered evidence, first-example weighting, rule-size prior, decision gain and criterion) is as in the best model; a switch probability of zero recovers it.

## log_compressed_range_sampling  — rank 6, 21.7 ± 6.0 nats behind the best (3.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3446.8

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (weighted rules and all numeric ranges of 1-100, size principle with evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger-weighted first example, a rule-size prior, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but they think about magnitude ranges on a compressed, logarithmic-like mental number line: they believe a range's examples are spread evenly in that compressed space, so the "size" of a range is its compressed width (by an inferred degree of compression) rather than its count of numbers. Because high numbers are crowded together on such a line, a range like 80-100 counts as small and examples there support a tight range, while among small numbers the same linear spread counts as wide, so generalization from small examples reaches proportionally further (e.g. 2, 4 generalizes to 7 or 10) and generalization from large clustered examples stays narrow in absolute terms; no compression recovers the best model.

## rule_size_prior_anchored_evidence  — rank 7, 23.3 ± 5.8 nats behind the best (4.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3448.4

Refinement of first_example_evidence_weighting: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100 (evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first example weighed as a stronger or weaker draw than later ones, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but before seeing any examples they do not find tiny, exotic rules ("powers of 4", "powers of 8", "cubes") as plausible as larger, familiar ones ("even numbers", "ends in 4", "multiples of 8"). The one change is that each rule's prior belief rises or falls as an inferred power of how many numbers it contains, which counteracts the size principle for small rules independently of set size, so after 64, 4 people generalize to 14, 24, 44 ("ends in 4", "even") rather than committing to 16 ("powers of 4") and after power-of-2 sets they leak to other even numbers more than the incumbent predicts; an exponent of zero recovers the incumbent.

## volatile_log_compressed_ranges  — rank 8, 25.3 ± 7.6 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3450.4

Refinement of log_compressed_range_sampling: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules (with a rule-size prior) and all ranges of 1-100 (evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger- or weaker-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), and they still think of magnitude ranges on a compressed, logarithmic-like mental number line, so a range's examples are believed spread evenly in compressed space and ranges among high numbers count as small while ranges among low numbers count as wide. The one change, taken from volatile_concept_changepoint, is that they read the examples one at a time and believe the program may switch concept between outputs (with an inferred switch probability, re-drawing the concept from their prior), so recent examples count more than earlier ones and a concept supported only by the last examples keeps belief; zero switch probability recovers log_compressed_range_sampling and zero compression approximately recovers volatile_concept_changepoint. The critique shows people generalize to the remaining powers of 2 less sharply than the incumbent predicts; on a compressed line powers of 2 are evenly spaced, so after power-of-2 sets a compressed magnitude range spanning the examples competes with "powers of 2" and pulls yeses toward nearby non-power even (and odd) numbers.

## prototype_property_profile_match  — rank 9, 291.6 ± 45.9 nats behind the best (6.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3716.7

People do not infer which concept generated the examples; instead they abstract a prototype of the example set — a profile of how strongly the set as a whole has each familiar number property (each arithmetic rule such as "powers of 2" or "ends in 4", and each numeric range), given by the fraction of the examples that have it, with rare, distinctive properties more salient than common ones. A target is endorsed in proportion to how much of this salience-weighted prototype it matches, so the salience of a small property is fixed rather than growing with each extra example (no size principle compounding over examples), and properties shared by only some of the examples still contribute partial support.
