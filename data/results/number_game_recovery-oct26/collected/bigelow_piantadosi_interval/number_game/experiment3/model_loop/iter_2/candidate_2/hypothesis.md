People's prior belief over arithmetic rules is not a list of separately learned rule familiarities; it comes from a simple rule grammar: they first choose a kind of rule (powers of a number, multiples of a number, ends in a digit, contains a digit, odd/even, primes, squares, cubes), with some kinds more familiar than others, and then fill in its number or digit with no preference among the options beyond how many numbers the resulting rule contains. Everything else is Bayesian concept learning as in the best model (all ranges of 1-100, perceived concept sizes inflated by a constant, tempered evidence, a re-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), so rules of the same kind and size ("ends in 3" and "ends in 7", "multiples of 7" and "multiples of 8" apart from size) are equally plausible before any examples, and a kind with many variants ("multiples of n") gives each variant less prior belief than a one-of-a-kind rule ("primes").
