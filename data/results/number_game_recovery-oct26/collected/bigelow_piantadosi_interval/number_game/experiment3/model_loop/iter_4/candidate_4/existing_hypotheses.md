# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## uneven_rule_familiarity_ends  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3378.1

Refinement of serial_position_ends_inflated_size: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 rules and all ranges of 1-100, with inflated perceived concept sizes, evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first and last examples re-weighted, a rule prior that rises as a power of rule size, and a decision gain with a shifted criterion. The one change is to how familiar each rule seems before any examples: on top of the size trend, each rule has an idiosyncratic familiarity drawn from a wide spread, instead of the incumbent's weights that are pulled toward one another. So people can treat a few rules as strongly familiar ("even numbers", "multiples of 10", "powers of 2") and many others as barely entertained, far more unevenly than the incumbent allows.

## serial_position_ends_inflated_size  — rank 1, 39.4 ± 21.9 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3417.4

Refinement of inflated_size_familiar_rule_prior. People do the same Bayesian concept learning as that model: 42 rules, with a familiarity prior that rises with rule size, and all ranges of 1-100. Perceived concept sizes are inflated by a constant, evidence is tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first example counts as a stronger or weaker draw, and answers pass through a decision gain and a shifted criterion. The one change adds a recency effect alongside that primacy effect: in sets of two or more, the last example shown is still fresh in memory when the target is judged, so it too counts as a stronger (or weaker) draw from the concept, by its own inferred multiplier. Concepts that contain both the first and the last example therefore gain belief over those that fit only the middle examples, and a multiplier of one recovers the incumbent.

## conservative_updating_recency_boost  — rank 2, 41.2 ± 22.7 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3419.3

Refinement of conservative_updating_inflated_size (statistically tied with the best model). People still read the examples one at a time and update conservatively: after the first example (Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules with a size-dependent prior and all ranges of 1-100, perceived concept sizes inflated by a constant, one shared evidence weight, a re-weighted first example, a decision gain and a shifted criterion), each later example moves belief only an inferred fraction of the way toward its full Bayesian update. Belief is therefore shaped most by the first example (anchoring and conservatism) and the last one (recency), and least by the middle ones; a multiplier of one recovers conservative_updating_inflated_size.

## conservative_updating_inflated_size  — rank 3, 42.2 ± 22.7 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3420.3

Refinement of conservative_partial_belief_updating: people still read the examples one at a time and revise their belief conservatively. After the first example (Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules with a size-dependent prior and all ranges of 1-100, with tempered evidence, a re-weighted first example, a decision gain and a shifted criterion), each later example moves their belief only an inferred fraction of the way from their previous belief toward the full Bayesian update on that example. ## decade_partitioned_number_line  — rank 4, 42.6 ± 22.7 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3420.7

People judge how spread out a set of examples is on a mental number line that is partitioned into decades: every decade boundary (between 10 and 11, 20 and 21, ...) that a stretch of numbers crosses adds an inferred amount of extra psychological distance, so a magnitude range that stays inside one decade feels tighter than an equally wide range that straddles a boundary. Apart from this, they infer the program's concept as the best model does. The size principle therefore favours within-decade ranges around the examples, and people say yes more often to nearby targets in the examples' own decade than to equally near targets just across a decade boundary. With no extra distance, this reduces to the best model.

## adaptive_breadth_criterion  — rank 5, 45.7 ± 24.0 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3423.8

People infer the program's concept exactly as in inflated_size_familiar_rule_prior (Bayesian weighing of familiar arithmetic rules and numeric ranges), but their yes/no criterion is not fixed: they calibrate it to how broad the concept they have inferred is, i.e. to what share of the numbers 1-100 they expect the program to produce. When the examples point to a broad concept ("even numbers", a wide range) they demand a different degree of belief before saying yes than when they point to a narrow one ("powers of 2", a tight cluster), so the same belief that a target belongs earns more or fewer yeses depending on the expected breadth of generalization for that set; no calibration recovers the fixed criterion of the source model.

## surprise_weighted_sequential_evidence  — rank 8, 48.9 ± 22.4 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3427.0

People read the examples one at a time, and how much each new example teaches them depends on how surprising it is given the guess they have formed so far: an example their current belief predicted well is treated as old news and weighted less (or, under confirmation bias, more), while an unexpected example draws more (or less) attention and moves their belief more, by an inferred surprise sensitivity. Everything else is the same Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules and numeric ranges (rule-size prior, perceived concept sizes inflated by a constant, tempered evidence, a re-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), so the same numbers shown in a different order, or a set whose later examples merely confirm the first, generalize differently from what order-blind accumulation predicts; zero sensitivity recovers order-blind learning.

## queried_target_self_evidence  — rank 9, 50.6 ± 23.2 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3428.7

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (42 rules with a size-dependent prior and all ranges of 1-100, perceived concept sizes inflated by a constant, evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a re-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but with one distortion: when asked about a number they cannot help imagining it as one of the program's outputs, so the queried target itself counts as an extra piece of evidence about the concept, with an inferred weight (positive is a confirmation bias, negative a discounting of the probe). ## volatile_range_width_inflation  — rank 10, 55.5 ± 22.5 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3433.5

Refinement of volatile_changepoint_inflated_size (statistically tied with the best model): people still read the examples one at a time, updating a Bayesian belief over 42 weighted rules (with a rule-size prior) and all ranges of 1-100 while allowing that the program may switch concept between outputs (with a stronger- or weaker-weighted first example, a decision gain and a shifted criterion), and they still perceive arithmetic rules as larger than they really are by an inferred number of members. The one change is how people discount the evidence for narrow magnitude ranges: instead of applying their own weaker (or stronger) evidence weight to ranges, they perceive every range as wider than it is by its own inferred number of extra members, and rules and ranges share one evidence weight. A uniform weight scales the advantage of a narrow range at every width, whereas added width wipes it out among narrow ranges and keeps it for wide ones. So a tight range just around clustered examples (52, 57, 55) loses most of its edge over a moderately wider one, and generalization reaches nearby numbers (48, 62), while very wide ranges are still rejected. With equal inflations for rules and ranges, the model becomes volatile_changepoint_inflated_size with the range evidence weight tied to the rule weight.

## no_repeat_example_sampling  — rank 11, 64.1 ± 25.3 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3442.2

Refinement of inflated_size_familiar_rule_prior. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100: rules get a prior that rises as a power of their size, perceived concept sizes are inflated by a constant, evidence is tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first example is re-weighted, and belief passes through a decision gain and a shifted criterion. The one change is to the size principle. People notice that the program never repeats a number within a set, so they treat each example as drawn only from the concept's members not yet shown. Each further example is therefore stronger evidence for a small concept the examples are close to using up ("powers of 4" after 4, 16, 64, or a tight range around 52, 55, 57), while broad concepts are barely affected. As a result, the advantage of small concepts grows faster than in proportion to the number of examples. This adds no free parameter, and sampling with replacement recovers the incumbent.

## volatile_concept_changepoint  — rank 15, 67.6 ± 25.2 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3445.7

People read the examples one at a time and update their belief about the program's concept after each one, but they believe the program might switch to a different concept between outputs: before each new example they allow, with an inferred switch probability, that the concept has been re-drawn from their prior. This belief in a volatile generator makes recent examples count more than earlier ones in a graded, Bayes-consistent way — a concept supported only by the last few examples keeps substantial belief even when an earlier example contradicts it — while everything else (weighted rules and ranges, size principle with tempered evidence, first-example weighting, rule-size prior, decision gain and criterion) is as in the best model; a switch probability of zero recovers it.

## log_compressed_range_sampling  — rank 16, 68.7 ± 25.0 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3446.8

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (weighted rules and all numeric ranges of 1-100, size principle with evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger-weighted first example, a rule-size prior, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but they think about magnitude ranges on a compressed, logarithmic-like mental number line: they believe a range's examples are spread evenly in that compressed space, so the "size" of a range is its compressed width (by an inferred degree of compression) rather than its count of numbers. Because high numbers are crowded together on such a line, a range like 80-100 counts as small and examples there support a tight range, while among small numbers the same linear spread counts as wide, so generalization from small examples reaches proportionally further (e.g. 2, 4 generalizes to 7 or 10) and generalization from large clustered examples stays narrow in absolute terms; no compression recovers the best model.

## surface_numeral_rule_fallback  — rank 19, 127.5 ± 26.9 nats behind the best (4.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3505.6

People do not always work out the arithmetic structure of the examples: on an inferred fraction of judgments they reason only with properties they can read straight off the written numerals and their magnitude (odd or even, the final digit, which digits appear, multiples of 5 or 10, numeric ranges), and only on the rest do they also consider computed rules such as powers, squares, cubes, primes and multiples of 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11 or 12. Either way they do Bayesian concept learning within the concepts they have in mind, with a prior that depends only on how many numbers a rule contains. So however many power-of-two examples are shown, the judgments made without computing still generalize to other even numbers or numbers sharing a final digit (14, 24, 44 after 64, 4; 62 after 2, 8) rather than to the remaining powers, a leak that more examples cannot remove.

## prototype_property_profile_match  — rank 20, 338.6 ± 39.3 nats behind the best (8.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3716.7

People do not infer which concept generated the examples; instead they abstract a prototype of the example set — a profile of how strongly the set as a whole has each familiar number property (each arithmetic rule such as "powers of 2" or "ends in 4", and each numeric range), given by the fraction of the examples that have it, with rare, distinctive properties more salient than common ones. A target is endorsed in proportion to how much of this salience-weighted prototype it matches, so the salience of a small property is fixed rather than growing with each extra example (no size principle compounding over examples), and properties shared by only some of the examples still contribute partial support.
