# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## rule_size_prior_anchored_evidence  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -1945.4

Refinement of first_example_evidence_weighting: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100 (evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first example weighed as a stronger or weaker draw than later ones, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but before seeing any examples they do not find tiny, exotic rules ("powers of 4", "powers of 8", "cubes") as plausible as larger, familiar ones ("even numbers", "ends in 4", "multiples of 8"). The one change is that each rule's prior belief rises or falls as an inferred power of how many numbers it contains, which counteracts the size principle for small rules independently of set size, so after 64, 4 people generalize to 14, 24, 44 ("ends in 4", "even") rather than committing to 16 ("powers of 4") and after power-of-2 sets they leak to other even numbers more than the incumbent predicts; an exponent of zero recovers the incumbent.

## inflated_concept_size_principle  — rank 1, 0.9 ± 4.3 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1946.3

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (weighted rules and all ranges of 1-100, evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, a stronger-weighted first example, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but they do not take a concept's size at face value in the size principle: they perceive every concept as larger than it is by an inferred constant number of members, so tiny concepts such as "powers of 8" (8, 64) or "powers of 4" (4, 16, 64) lose most of their advantage over moderately larger ones while the contrast between moderate and broad concepts is largely kept. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives on sets that fit a tiny rule nested inside a larger one (8, 64; 64, 4; 16, 8; 16, 32, 2, 8): the best model generalizes tightly to the tiny rule's other members (16 after 64, 4), whereas this model predicts broader generalization to the larger rule (multiples of 8, ends in 4, even numbers) and fewer yeses to the tiny rule's own members.

## rule_size_inflation_anchored  — rank 2, 4.0 ± 4.8 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1949.4

Refinement of first_example_evidence_weighting: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules and numeric ranges (evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, the first example weighed as an extra-strong or weak draw, a decision gain and shifted criterion), but they do not judge a mathematical rule's size by exactly counting its members — they treat every rule as if it had an inferred number of extra members beyond those it actually has in 1-100. The one change is this subjective size inflation of rules, which shrinks the size-principle advantage of tiny rules ("powers of 2", "powers of 4") over broader ones ("even numbers", "ends in 4", "multiples of 8") — and shrinks it more the more examples are shown — so after 64, 4 or 2, 8 people generalize to other even numbers or same-final-digit numbers more readily than to the remaining powers; zero extra members recovers the refined model.

## first_example_evidence_weighting  — rank 3, 21.7 ± 6.7 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1967.1

Refinement of first_example_anchored_concepts: people still let the first example they see anchor their Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules and numeric ranges (with evidence tempered separately for rules and ranges, and a decision gain and shifted criterion), but the anchoring works through evidence rather than a flat bonus: the first example is weighed as a stronger (or weaker) draw from the concept than later examples, by an inferred multiplier on its size-principle evidence. The one change replaces the incumbent's equal boost to every concept containing the first example with this extra evidential weight, so the first example pulls belief most strongly toward small, specific concepts that contain it (e.g. "powers of 2" when 16 is shown first) and only weakly toward broad ones like "even numbers" or wide ranges, while concepts that must write it off as noise are penalised more.

## exemplar_rule_member_similarity  — rank 10, 29.0 ± 8.5 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1974.4

People store each familiar arithmetic category ("powers of 2", "multiples of 10", "ends in 3", ...) as a list of remembered member numbers, and judge both the examples and the target against a category by exemplar similarity to its nearest stored member on the number line, not by all-or-none membership; the similarity falls off geometrically with distance at an inferred rate. Categories whose stored members closely match all the examples (diluted by how many members the category stores) are retrieved most strongly, alongside plain numeric ranges, and the target is endorsed by its similarity to the members of the retrieved categories — so near-misses of a rule (30 or 66 after 16, 8, 64) get graded partial yeses rather than none.

## parity_restricted_rules  — rank 11, 29.7 ± 8.5 nats behind the best (3.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1975.1

People do the same Bayesian concept learning over weighted mathematical rules and numeric ranges as the best models (size principle with tempered evidence, a decision gain and shifted criterion turning belief into a yes/no answer), but their space of rules also contains each rule restricted to odd or to even numbers — "odd multiples of 3", "even squares", "odd numbers containing a 7" — which take an inferred share of their parent rule's prior belief. Because such a parity-restricted rule is half the size, the size principle favours it whenever all the examples share a parity, so after 63, 27, 99, 33 people endorse other odd multiples of 3 but reject even ones like 18 or 24.

## criterion_shifted_tempered_decision  — rank 12, 30.3 ± 8.1 nats behind the best (3.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1975.7

Refinement of tempered_evidence_decision_gain: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 42 weighted rules and all intervals of 1-100 (size principle, 10% noisy examples, examples' evidence tempered by an inferred weight) and pass their belief through the same inferred decision gain on its log-odds, but their yes/no criterion is not centred on even odds. ## round_landmark_interval_prior  — rank 13, 31.8 ± 8.0 nats behind the best (4.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1977.2

People do the same Bayesian concept learning over weighted mathematical rules and numeric ranges as the best model (size principle with tempered evidence, a decision gain and criterion turning belief into a yes/no answer), but the ranges they entertain are not all equally natural: ranges whose boundaries fall on round decade landmarks (such as 1-50, 20-29, 61-100 or "the 80s") get extra prior belief, by an inferred factor, over ranges with arbitrary edges like 23-87. ## stochastic_example_forgetting  — rank 14, 32.6 ± 8.2 nats behind the best (4.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1978.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (weighted rules and all intervals of 1-100, size principle with tempered evidence, and a decision gain and criterion turning belief into a yes/no answer), but their memory of the examples is unreliable: by the time they judge a target, each example has been retained only with an inferred probability, and they infer the concept from whichever examples they still hold. Their belief is therefore an average over the possible remembered subsets of the set, so larger sets are sometimes judged as if from fewer examples (broader generalization) and an odd-one-out example is sometimes forgotten, letting the tight rule the other examples share (e.g. "ends in 2" after 92, 82, 27, 42) survive without being explained away as noise.

## range_bounded_rule_concepts  — rank 15, 33.1 ± 8.0 nats behind the best (4.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1978.5

## probability_threshold_decision  — rank 17, 56.6 ± 11.4 nats behind the best (5.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2002.0

People infer the program's concept exactly as the simplest current model does (Bayesian weighing of weighted mathematical rules and numeric ranges, with tempered evidence), but they answer by comparing their degree of belief that the target belongs against a fixed certainty threshold on the plain probability scale, with comparison noise of constant size in probability units. Because the noise is constant in probability rather than in log-odds, all weakly believed targets (belief 1% or 15%) get nearly the same small floor of yeses and all strongly believed targets (85% or 99%) nearly the same ceiling, while answers change steeply only for beliefs near the threshold — unlike a gain and criterion on log-odds, which keep distinguishing 1% from 15%.

## strong_weak_sampling_mixture  — rank 19, 77.0 ± 15.5 nats behind the best (5.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2022.4

Refinement of split_rule_range_tempering: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 42 weighted rules and all intervals of 1-100 (10% noisy examples), judge the evidence separately for rules and for ranges, and answer through the same decision gain and shifted criterion. The one change is how the size principle is weakened: instead of discounting every example's evidence by a power, people believe each example was either drawn from the concept itself or picked at random from 1-100 and merely found to fit it (a mixture of strong and weak sampling, with an inferred strong-sampling share for rules and another for ranges). The size penalty therefore saturates — tiny concepts are still sharply distinguished from each other, but a large concept consistent with the examples (e.g. "multiples of 8" after 8, 64) is barely penalised relative to a tiny one, so generalization to larger consistent rules and wide ranges stays strong.

## conjunctive_category_similarity  — rank 21, 750.4 ± 44.7 nats behind the best (16.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2695.8

People judge a target by its similarity to each example separately, where two numbers are similar to the degree that they fall together in the same familiar number categories (mathematical rules or numeric ranges), with small, specific shared categories counting more than broad ones by an inferred similarity gradient. These per-example similarities combine conjunctively (multiplicatively): the target must resemble every example, so a single example it is dissimilar to sharply lowers the chance of a "yes", and the combined similarity is turned into a yes/no answer through a decision gain and criterion.
