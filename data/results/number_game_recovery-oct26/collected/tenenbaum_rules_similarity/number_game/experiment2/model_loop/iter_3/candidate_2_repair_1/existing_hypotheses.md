# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## upper_edge_marking_ranges  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2060.3

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts` (with its prime concept corrected to exclude 1, as in the incumbent): people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, but they assume the program's examples mark where its range stops at the top — the largest example shown is taken as evidence that the range ends there. The one change, grafted in one-sided form from `endpoint_marking_interval_sampling`: a range whose upper end coincides with the largest example gets an inferred evidence bonus, while its lower end is unconstrained, so people generalize downward from the examples more readily than upward (after 16 they accept 15 or 13 more than 17 or 19; after 83,77 they reject 84).

## upper_edge_discounted_range_size  — rank 1, 0.2 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (0.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2060.5

Refinement of `upper_edge_marking_ranges`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and they take the largest example as marking where the program's range stops at the top. The one change, grafted from `range_discounted_size_principle`: people apply the size principle at full strength only to arithmetic rules and only partly to numeric ranges, judging a range's fit to the examples as if it were wider than it is (by an inferred degree), so it is the marked top edge — not the size principle — that stops range generalization going upward, while downward from the examples "yes" spreads further across their neighbourhood than strong sampling predicts.

## prime_concept_excludes_one  — rank 2, 0.4 ± 1.8 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2060.6

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules (kind-level rule prior, Erlang width prior with a decade-boundary preference, size principle with 10% random examples, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their concept of "prime numbers" is the mathematical one, which excludes 1. The one change is removing 1 from the prime concept: when 1 is among the examples (e.g. 31,3,1) people cannot explain the set as primes and fall back on "odd numbers" (yes to 9 or 25, no special yes to 2), and after a set of primes (e.g. 67,31,17,61) they answer "no" to 1 rather than accepting it as a prime.

## two_cluster_range_concepts  — rank 3, 0.9 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2061.2

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1, kind-level rule prior) and numeric ranges of 1-100 (width prior favouring moderate, decade-bounded ranges, the top of a range marked by the largest example, size principle with 10% random examples), but they can also think of the program's numbers as lying in two separate clusters — two equally wide stretches of the number line with a gap between them ("numbers near 10 and numbers near 80") — a kind of concept with its own inferred prior share and a width prior shared with ranges. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives in the gap between widely separated examples (e.g. 45 after 10,80, or 35 after 5,65): the best model must cover such examples with one wide range or a rule and so says "yes" across the gap, whereas this model says "yes" near each example and "no" in the empty middle.

## range_discounted_size_principle  — rank 4, 1.0 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2061.3

Refinement of `prime_concept_excludes_one`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, but they apply the size principle at full strength only to arithmetic rules and only partly to numeric ranges. The one change: when judging how well a range explains the examples, people treat the range as if it were wider than it is — its effective size lies between its true width and the whole 1-100 scale, by an inferred degree — so more examples tighten range-based generalization less than rule-based generalization, and clustered examples are explained by ranges (with "yes" spreading further across the examples' neighbourhood) more readily than strong sampling predicts.

## pedagogical_teacher_sampling  — rank 5, 1.1 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2061.4

People assume the examples were chosen by a helpful teacher rather than drawn at random from the program's concept: a teacher who wants them to guess the concept picks the numbers that point most clearly to it, i.e. numbers that few other plausible concepts would also produce. Reasoning about this choice (pedagogical sampling), people take an example that is distinctive — unlikely under the concepts they find plausible in general — as stronger evidence for the concepts containing it than a commonplace example, with an inferred strength of this teacher assumption (zero gives the ordinary random-sampling learner), and say "yes" with the resulting posterior probability that the concept contains the target.

## odd_composite_concept  — rank 7, 1.8 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2062.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic rules with a kind-level prior, every range of 1-100, range-restricted rules, size principle with 10% random examples, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their space of concepts also includes a rule with exceptions, "odd numbers that are not prime" (9, 15, 21, 25, 27, 33, ...), as a kind of rule of its own with an inferred prior share. When the examples are all odd composites (e.g. 9,15,25 or 63,27,99,33), this smaller concept wins under the size principle, so people say "yes" to other odd non-primes but "no" to odd primes such as 7, 11 or 53, which "odd numbers" would accept.

## log_odds_yes_bias_concept_learning  — rank 8, 1.9 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2062.2

People infer the program's concept by plain Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules and numeric ranges (rules of one kind equally likely, a width prior favouring moderately wide and decade-bounded ranges, the size principle, primes excluding 1), but their answer is not a direct readout of their belief: they carry a constant response bias toward "yes" or toward "no" that shifts their confidence by the same amount on the log-odds scale on every trial. The bias therefore moves answers most for targets the learner is unsure about (posterior near one half) and hardly at all for targets that are clearly in or clearly out of the concept.

## named_sets_distinct_familiarity  — rank 9, 6.5 ± 3.6 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2066.8

Refinement of `prime_concept_excludes_one`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic rules with a kind-level prior, every range of 1-100 with an Erlang width prior and decade-boundary preference, range-restricted rules, size principle with 10% random examples, primes excluding 1, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but the familiar named sets — primes, even numbers, odd numbers, squares and cubes — are not equally likely a priori. ## range_restricted_rule_concepts  — rank 10, 49.0 ± 15.0 nats behind the best (3.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2109.2

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (42 arithmetic rules and every interval of 1-100, a kind-level rule prior, an Erlang width prior favouring decade-bounded ranges, size principle with 10% random examples, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their hypothesis space also contains range-restricted rules — conjunctions such as "even numbers in the 60s-70s" or "multiples of 4 below 20" — which receive an inferred share of prior belief and, being smaller, are strongly favoured by the size principle when the examples cluster. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives on rule-consistent targets far from the examples' span (e.g. 12 or 30 after 66,78; 64 or 100 after 4,16,12): the best model says "yes" to every member of the rule wherever it lies, while this model predicts that "yes" to rule members falls off outside the region the examples occupy.

## family_pooled_rule_prior  — rank 11, 50.2 ± 15.6 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2110.5

Refinement of `decade_bounded_interval_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over 42 arithmetic rules and every interval of 1-100 (rule share, size principle with 10% random examples, Erlang prior over interval width, a preference for decade-bounded ranges, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their prior over rules is set by the kind of rule, not rule by rule. The one change: people hold a belief about how likely each kind of rule is (powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit, and special sets such as primes, evens, odds, squares, cubes) and treat all rules of one kind as equally likely, so "multiples of 7" is as plausible a priori as "multiples of 4", instead of the incumbent's separate free weight for each of the 42 rules.

## target_as_weak_evidence  — rank 12, 50.4 ± 15.7 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2110.6

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules and ranges of 1-100 (as in `family_pooled_rule_prior`: kind-level rule prior, size principle with 10% random examples, Erlang width prior, a preference for decade-bounded ranges), but being asked about a number is itself taken as weak evidence: people partly treat the queried target as if the program had produced it, adding it to the examples with an inferred fraction of an example's weight before judging whether the concept contains it. This suggestion (positive-test) bias inflates "yes" most for targets that fit a small, plausible concept alongside the examples, and least for targets that only a broad or implausible concept would contain.

## random_forgetting_range_restricted_rules  — rank 13, 50.6 ± 14.9 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2110.8

Refinement of `random_example_forgetting`: people do Bayesian concept learning over 42 arithmetic rules and every range of 1-100 (kind-level rule prior, size principle with 10% random examples, Erlang width prior with a preference for decade-bounded ranges, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target) from only the examples they still hold in memory, each shown example being independently forgotten with an inferred probability. The one change, grafted from `range_restricted_rule_concepts`: the concepts people entertain also include range-restricted rules ("even numbers in the 60s", "multiples of 4 below 20") with their own inferred share of prior belief, so the remembered examples can pick out a rule confined to the region they occupy — forgetting loosens generalization on large sets while the conjunctions confine "yes" to rule members near the remembered examples, and the two together predict how far rule-based "yes" extends beyond the examples' span.

## random_example_forgetting  — rank 14, 51.5 ± 15.4 nats behind the best (3.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2111.8

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules and ranges of 1-100 (as in the best model: kind-level rule prior, size principle, a width prior and a preference for decade-bounded ranges), but their working memory for the examples is unreliable: each example shown is independently lost from memory with an inferred probability (at least one is always retained), and people generalize only from the examples they still hold. So their judgment is an average of what the ideal learner would conclude from each remembered subset of the examples — larger sets are more often reduced to fewer examples, which weakens the size principle's tightening and lets broader concepts and single-example generalizations survive, irrespective of the order the examples were shown in.

## scale_end_avoiding_ranges  — rank 15, 52.5 ± 15.4 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2112.7

People do Bayesian concept learning over 42 arithmetic rules and every interval of 1-100 exactly as in `family_pooled_rule_prior` (rule-kind prior shares, size principle with 10% random examples, Erlang prior over interval width, a preference for decade-bounded ranges, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but they believe the program's range is unlikely to run all the way to the ends of the 1-100 scale. Each end of a candidate range that sits at 1 or at 100 changes its prior by an inferred factor, so people generalize less toward the extremes of the scale — fewer "yes" answers to numbers in the 90s above high examples (and near 1 below low ones) than a learner who treats the scale's ends like any other number.

## weber_blurred_ranges_kind_pooled_rules  — rank 16, 52.9 ± 15.7 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2113.2

Refinement of `weber_noisy_range_membership`: people do Bayesian concept learning over 42 arithmetic rules and every range of 1-100 (size principle, a prior favouring moderately wide ranges), and they check whether a target falls inside a range on a noisy mental number line whose noise grows with magnitude (Weber's law), so range edges are blurred, more among large numbers, while rule membership is checked exactly. The one change, grafted from `family_pooled_rule_prior`: people's prior over rules is set by the kind of rule (powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit, special sets), with all rules of one kind equally likely, instead of a separate free weight for each of the 42 rules — so the blurred-edge account stops being handicapped by an overfitted rule prior, and range generalization ends in a magnitude-scaled soft edge rather than at decade boundaries.

## noise_rate_inferring_restricted_rules  — rank 17, 56.1 ± 15.3 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2116.4

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, plain ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules ("even numbers in the 60s-70s"), with the size principle and "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target. The one change is how they treat noisy examples: instead of assuming that exactly 10% of the program's examples are random, people are unsure how many of the examples are random noise and infer the noise rate jointly with the concept for each set, from an inferred prior belief over noise levels. So a set with one stray example (such as 8,24,48,63) can still be read as a tight rule plus one random number, while a set that fits a tight concept exactly drives the belief in noise down and sharpens generalization.

## win_stay_lose_shift_concept  — rank 19, 101.9 ± 16.0 nats behind the best (6.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2162.2

People do not weigh all the examples jointly; they hold a single running guess about the concept and revise it one example at a time, in the order shown (win-stay, lose-shift). After the first example they adopt one concept drawn in proportion to its posterior plausibility (arithmetic rules with a kind-level prior, ranges with a width prior favouring decade-bounded ranges, size principle); each later example that their current guess contains leaves the guess unchanged, and only an example the guess fails to contain makes them draw a new guess from the posterior given all the examples seen so far. So a broad concept adopted early (e.g. "even numbers" or a wide range) survives later examples that would have narrowed an ideal learner's belief, and the same examples in a different order lead to different generalization.

## decade_block_range_concepts  — rank 20, 258.0 ± 33.5 nats behind the best (7.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2318.3

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit, primes, even, odd, squares, cubes; rules of one kind equally likely a priori) and numeric ranges, with the size principle, but they only ever think of ranges as whole blocks of decades ("the 80s and 90s", "1 to 30", "41 to 60") — never as a range with arbitrary ends hugging the examples. So range-based generalization is all-or-none within a decade: a target in the same decade block as the examples is accepted as readily as one right next to them, and acceptance drops abruptly at decade boundaries, with no gradient tied to the examples' exact span.

## common_feature_contrast_similarity  — rank 21, 783.0 ± 40.6 nats behind the best (19.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2843.3

People judge a target by its similarity to the example set as a whole, not by inducing a concept: the psychological similarity is the share of the properties that all the examples have in common (parity, multiples, final digit, contained digit, powers, primes, squares, cubes, decade) that the target also has, each property counted by how distinctive it is (rarer properties count more), together with how close the target is in magnitude to the nearest example on a compressed (logarithmic) distance scale. The probability of "yes" rises smoothly with this similarity, so a target matching the set's distinctive shared properties and lying near an example is accepted, and acceptance falls off gradually — never all-or-none — as it loses shared properties or moves away.

## soft_min_exemplar_generalization  — rank 22, 896.9 ± 40.3 nats behind the best (22.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2957.2

People do not infer one concept from the examples jointly; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its similarity to each stored exemplar, where the similarity of a target to one exemplar is Shepard's consequential-region generalization (how likely a familiar number concept — an arithmetic rule, or a range with a preference for moderate, decade-bounded widths — that contains that exemplar also contains the target). The similarities are then combined conjunctively, as a soft minimum dominated by the least similar exemplar (the target must resemble every example, with an inferred degree of strictness), so a target is accepted only if it fits with all of the examples, but adding examples never tightens generalization by the size principle beyond what each exemplar alone implies.

## uncertain_sampling_assumption  — no comparison row

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges of 1-100 (moderate, decade-bounded ranges preferred, the largest example marking a range's top), but they are unsure how the examples were chosen: with an inferred probability they assume the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling, so a small concept that fits is strongly favoured), and otherwise that the examples are merely numbers the program happens to accept (weak sampling, so every concept containing them stays as plausible as it was a priori). Their "yes" averages over both readings, so broad concepts such as "even numbers" or wide ranges keep a floor of support however many examples fit a narrower one, and generalization tightens with more examples less than an ideal strong-sampling learner predicts — for rules and ranges alike.

## extreme_examples_attended_sampling  — no comparison row

People attend to the extreme examples: they treat the smallest and the largest example shown as samples drawn from the program's concept, but the examples lying between them are encoded only as confirming that the concept includes them, so they count as weaker evidence under the size principle by an inferred degree (zero: mere consistency; one: the ideal learner). Otherwise people do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1, kind-level prior) and ranges of 1-100 (Erlang width prior, decade-boundary preference, the largest example marking the top of the range), so adding examples inside the span of the first two tightens generalization less than an ideal learner predicts, while examples that extend the span tighten it fully.
