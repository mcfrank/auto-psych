## Hypotheses

- **parity_representative_sequence_edges**: Refinement of `sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with a last-shown largest example marking a range's top and a first-shown smallest example (in sets of several) marking its bottom. The one change, taken from `parity_representativeness_bias`: people judge how likely the examples are under a concept partly by how representative they look of it, so a concept holding both odd and even numbers (a range, multiples of 3, squares) is expected to produce a parity-mixed sample, and each further example that repeats a parity shared by the whole set costs it an inferred extra penalty, while parity-pure concepts (even numbers, odd numbers, multiples of 4) pay nothing. So after 22,46,68 people say "yes" to even numbers outside the span (8 or 90) more, and to odd numbers inside it less, than the incumbent, while the order-read edges still stop generalization above a last-shown maximum and below a first-shown minimum.
- **parity_representative_stray_example**: Refinement of `position_graded_stray_example`: people do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with a last-shown largest example marking a range's top and a first-shown smallest marking its bottom, and they suspect (with an inferred probability and recency gradient) that one example in a set of several is a random stray, learning from the others alone. The one change, grafted from `parity_representativeness_bias`: people judge how likely the examples they keep are under a concept by how representative they look, so a concept holding both odd and even numbers is expected to produce a parity-mixed sample and each further kept example repeating a parity shared by all of them costs it an inferred penalty, while parity-pure concepts pay nothing. So an example of the odd-one-out parity (27 in 92,82,27,42) is read as the stray more readily, and people then say "yes" to even numbers outside the span and "no" to that stray example more than either parent model predicts.
- **sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top**: Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_edge`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe the program may deliberately end its demonstration on its range's top number, so a last-shown largest example blocks generalization above it. The one change is where the bottom edge is read from. It comes from the opening of the sequence, not its end: when several examples are shown and the smallest comes first, people believe (with separately inferred odds) that the program deliberately started at its range's bottom, and ranges whose bottom is that example get generative credit (larger for wider ranges). So after 20,35,60 people say "yes" less to 15 than after 35,20,60, which addresses the critique that a first-shown minimum restricts downward generalization.
- **compressed_rule_extension**: People think of an arithmetic property such as "even numbers" or "multiples of 5" by its description rather than by listing its members, so they underestimate how many numbers it contains. When they weigh how well a concept explains the examples (the size principle), they treat a rule as smaller than it really is by an inferred degree, but they judge a numeric range at its true size, because its extent is visible on the number line. Otherwise they reason as in Bayesian concept learning over rules, ranges and range-restricted rules, with the last-shown largest example marking a range's top. So broad properties like parity keep more support against tight ranges, and people generalize by the shared property beyond the examples' span (yes to same-parity numbers far away, no to opposite-parity neighbours just outside) more than an ideal learner would.
- **parity_representativeness_bias**: People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic rules, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with the size principle and the last-shown largest example marking a range's top), but they judge how likely the examples are under a concept partly by how representative they look: a concept that contains both odd and even numbers (a range, multiples of 3, squares) is expected to produce a parity-mixed sample, so a set whose examples all share a parity seems less likely under it than chance implies, with each further same-parity example costing an inferred extra penalty. Parity-pure concepts (even numbers, odd numbers, multiples of 4) pay nothing, so after examples like 22,46,68 people say "yes" to even numbers outside the examples' span (e.g. 8 or 90) more, and to odd numbers inside it less, than the ideal learner does.
- **position_graded_stray_example**: Refinement of `sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top` by way of `stray_closing_example`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with a last-shown largest example marking a range's top and a first-shown smallest marking its bottom, and they suspect that, with an inferred probability, one example in a set of several is a random number mixed into the demonstration rather than a member of the program's concept, and learn from the others alone. The one change from `stray_closing_example`: any example, not only the last, may be the stray, and how suspect each position is follows an inferred recency gradient (later examples more suspect when it is positive, all positions alike at zero). So an example that breaks the others' pattern is often rejected wherever it was shown (e.g. "no" to 72 after 18,8,72,98 or to 27 after 92,82,27,42), not only when it closes the sequence.
- **sequence_only_edge_marking**: Refinement of `sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and read the range's edges from the order of the demonstration: a sequence of several examples ending on its largest marks the range's top, and one opening on its smallest marks its bottom (with separately inferred odds). The one change, taken from `multi_example_sequence_top_marking`: a lone example is not a sequence and marks no edge, so it is treated as an ordinary random draw rather than as the range's top. So after a single example (e.g. 60) people say "yes" to 62 about as readily as to 58, while after 20,35,60 generalization still stops at 60 and drops below 20.
- **stray_closing_example**: People read the examples in order and suspect that the program's closing example may be a stray: with an inferred probability, they believe the last number shown (in a set of two or more) was a random number tacked onto the demonstration rather than a member of the program's concept. Otherwise they reason as in the best model (Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, ranges and range-restricted rules, a last-shown largest example marking a range's top, a first-shown smallest marking its bottom), and under the stray reading they learn from the earlier examples alone. So the last-shown example is itself rejected more often than earlier ones (e.g. "no" to 100 after 31,19,49,100), and a closing example that breaks the earlier examples' pattern loosens generalization less than an ideal learner predicts.
