# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3098.0

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_edge`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe the program may deliberately end its demonstration on its range's top number, so a last-shown largest example blocks generalization above it. The one change is where the bottom edge is read from. It comes from the opening of the sequence, not its end: when several examples are shown and the smallest comes first, people believe (with separately inferred odds) that the program deliberately started at its range's bottom, and ranges whose bottom is that example get generative credit (larger for wider ranges). So after 20,35,60 people say "yes" less to 15 than after 35,20,60, which addresses the critique that a first-shown minimum restricts downward generalization.

## compressed_rule_extension  — rank 1, 0.1 ± 2.5 nats behind the best (0.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3098.2

People think of an arithmetic property such as "even numbers" or "multiples of 5" by its description rather than by listing its members, so they underestimate how many numbers it contains. When they weigh how well a concept explains the examples (the size principle), they treat a rule as smaller than it really is by an inferred degree, but they judge a numeric range at its true size, because its extent is visible on the number line. Otherwise they reason as in Bayesian concept learning over rules, ranges and range-restricted rules, with the last-shown largest example marking a range's top. So broad properties like parity keep more support against tight ranges, and people generalize by the shared property beyond the examples' span (yes to same-parity numbers far away, no to opposite-parity neighbours just outside) more than an ideal learner would.

## parity_representativeness_bias  — rank 2, 0.3 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (0.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3098.4

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic rules, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with the size principle and the last-shown largest example marking a range's top), but they judge how likely the examples are under a concept partly by how representative they look: a concept that contains both odd and even numbers (a range, multiples of 3, squares) is expected to produce a parity-mixed sample, so a set whose examples all share a parity seems less likely under it than chance implies, with each further same-parity example costing an inferred extra penalty. Parity-pure concepts (even numbers, odd numbers, multiples of 4) pay nothing, so after examples like 22,46,68 people say "yes" to even numbers outside the examples' span (e.g. 8 or 90) more, and to odd numbers inside it less, than the ideal learner does.

## last_example_marks_range_top  — rank 3, 1.1 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.1

Refinement of `top_marking_range_sampling`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that, with some inferred odds, the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random — but they believe the program ends its demonstration on that top number. The one change: the marked top is read from the order of presentation, so a range ending at the largest example gets the generative top-marking credit (larger for wider ranges) only when the largest example is the last one shown, and then at full strength whatever the set size, while sets whose largest example comes earlier get no edge credit — after 20,35,60 people say "yes" to 45 but not 62, whereas after 60,35,20 they extend above 60 as readily as below 20.

## parity_kind_last_shown_top_marking  — rank 4, 1.3 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.3

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with the program believed to end its demonstration on its range's top number. The one change: "even numbers" and "odd numbers" are a kind of rule in their own right, with their own inferred prior share, rather than sharing one "special sets" share equally with primes, squares and cubes. So when all the examples share a parity (e.g. 22,36,48), parity stays a live explanation next to the ranges, and people say "yes" to same-parity numbers outside the examples' span (e.g. 80) more than to opposite-parity ones (81), which addresses the critique that the incumbent under-uses parity beyond the range.

## examples_bracket_range_ends  — rank 5, 1.4 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.5

People believe the program often demonstrates a range by showing both of its ends: with some inferred odds, the smallest and the largest example are the range's bottom and top numbers (whatever order they are shown in) and any other examples are random draws. Otherwise they reason as in the best model's Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic rules perceived as smaller than they are, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, a last-shown largest example marking a range's top). So the range spanning exactly the examples is favoured far more than chance sampling implies, most of all for widely separated pairs. After 22,46 people say "yes" to odd numbers between the examples (e.g. 33) more, and "no" to neighbours just outside (21 or 47) more, than the best model predicts.

## multi_example_sequence_top_marking  — rank 6, 1.5 ± 1.7 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.5

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that, with inferred odds, the program deliberately ends its demonstration on its range's top number. The one change: people read this marking from the sequence only when there is a sequence. When several examples are shown and the largest comes last, they credit ranges ending there, as the incumbent does. A lone example is not taken to mark anything, so it is treated as an ordinary random draw. So after a single example (e.g. 60) "yes" spreads about as far above it as below it (62 as readily as 58). After 20,35,60, generalization still stops at 60 while spreading below 20.

## parity_restricted_range_concepts  — rank 7, 1.6 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.6

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_top`. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and they read a last-shown largest example as marking the top of the range. The one change: "even (or odd) numbers within a range" is a family of concepts in its own right, with its own inferred prior plausibility. It is not a minor range-restricted rule whose plausibility is diluted by the weight of the parity rule among all rules. So when every example shares a parity (e.g. 66,78 or 83,77), people readily read the set as "even/odd numbers around here" and say "no" to opposite-parity numbers near and beyond the examples. Other range-restricted rules ("multiples of 7 in the 20s") keep a separate share.

## opening_example_marks_range_edge  — rank 8, 1.9 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3099.9

Refinement of `compressed_rule_extension`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (perceived as smaller than they are), ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with a last-shown largest example marking a range's top. The one change is that people also read the opening of a sequence. When several examples are shown and the first one is the smallest, or the largest, they believe (with inferred odds) that the program deliberately started on that edge of its range and drew the rest at random. Ranges with that end at the first example then gain credit, more for wider ranges. So after 20,35,60 people say "no" to 17 more readily, and after 60,35,20 to 63, than after the same numbers in an order that does not open on an extreme.

## last_shown_top_discounted_ranges  — rank 9, 2.1 ± 2.4 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3100.1

Refinement of `upper_edge_discounted_range_size`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, applying the size principle fully to rules but only partly to ranges (judging a range as if it were wider than it is). The one change, taken from `last_example_marks_range_top`, is how they read the top edge. They do not give a fixed bonus to any range ending at the largest example. Instead, they believe the program may deliberately end its demonstration on its range's top number, so a range ending at the largest example gets generative credit (larger for wider ranges) only when that example is shown last. Because ranges are only weakly penalised for size, "yes" still extends a little above a last-shown maximum (addressing the critique that the order-based model over-sharpens that edge). Sets whose largest example comes earlier get no edge credit and generalize upward freely.

## uncertain_sampling_sequence_top  — rank 10, 2.3 ± 2.4 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3100.3

Refinement of `uncertain_sampling_top_marking`: people do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and ranges of 1-100, unsure whether the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling) or are merely numbers it accepts (weak sampling), so broad concepts such as "even numbers" keep a floor of support beyond the examples' span (the critique's parity gap), and they believe the program, with inferred odds, deliberately shows its range's top number. The one change, taken from `multi_example_sequence_top_marking`: the marked top is read from the order of presentation, so only a sequence of several examples that ends on its largest example blocks generalization above it (after 20,35,60 "yes" to 45 but not 62), while sets whose largest example comes earlier, and single examples, get no edge credit and generalize upward as freely as downward.

## limited_capacity_example_memory  — rank 11, 2.6 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3100.6

People can hold only a limited number of examples in working memory while judging: when a set has more examples than their capacity (which varies from trial to trial, between two and four, with inferred probabilities), they keep the last example shown plus a random selection of the earlier ones and forget the rest. They then reason exactly as in the best model's Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic rules perceived as smaller than they are, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, a last-shown largest example marking a range's top) from only the examples they still hold, so sets of three or four examples tighten generalization less than an ideal learner predicts and a forgotten example is itself sometimes rejected, while one- and two-example sets are judged in full.

## digit_sum_concepts  — rank 12, 2.6 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3100.7

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic rules, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with the size principle and the last-shown largest example marking a range's top), but their space of candidate concepts also includes digit-sum concepts — "numbers whose digits add up to the same total" (e.g. 18, 27, 36, 45 … 90 for a total of nine; 15, 24, 33, 42, 51, 60 for six) — as a kind of rule of its own with an inferred prior share. So examples that share a digit sum (36,45,54,81 or 15,51) lead people to say "yes" to other numbers with that digit sum (90 or 27; 24 or 60) but not to rule-mates that break it (99 after 36,45,54,81).

## span_anchored_width_expectation  — rank 13, 3.1 ± 3.2 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.2

People do Bayesian concept learning as in the best model (arithmetic rules with primes excluding 1 perceived as smaller than they are, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, the size principle, the last-shown largest example marking a range's top), but their expectation of how wide the program's range is gets anchored on how widely the examples are spread, so the spread is counted twice: once as evidence and again in the prior. The one distortion: the typical range width people expect grows (or shrinks) with the examples' span by an inferred power, so widely spread sets lead people to expect, and generalize to, ranges reaching further past the outermost examples than the ideal learner would, and tightly clustered or single examples to ranges hugging them.

## top_marking_range_sampling  — rank 14, 3.3 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.3

Refinement of `upper_edge_marking_ranges`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe the program shows where its range stops at the top — but they reason about this generatively rather than granting a fixed bonus. The one change: people assume that, with some inferred odds, the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random, so the largest example is explained by marking instead of by chance; a range ending at the largest example is therefore credited in proportion to how unlikely a random draw would have been to hit that number — far more for wide ranges than narrow ones, and less as more examples are shown — so single examples and sparse sets license especially strong downward generalization (after 60, "yes" to 40 but not 62), while dense sets fall back toward ordinary size-principle ranges.

## last_example_marks_range_edge  — rank 15, 3.3 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.4

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that, with some inferred odds, the program ends its demonstration on a boundary of its range and draws the other examples at random. The one change: the last example marks whichever boundary it is, not only the top — a sequence ending on its largest example marks the range's top (as in the incumbent), while a sequence ending on its smallest example marks the range's bottom with the same generative credit (larger for wider ranges), so after 60,35,20 people say "yes" to 45 but less to 15, the mirror image of 20,35,60, and only sets ending on a middle example get no edge credit.

## breadth_adaptive_yes_criterion  — rank 16, 3.4 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.4

People infer the program's concept by plain Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges, but the criterion they use to say "yes" is not fixed: it adapts to how many of the numbers 1-100 they believe the program produces. When the examples suggest a broad concept (e.g. "even numbers", a wide range) they shift how sure they must be that a target belongs before answering "yes" by an inferred amount, relative to sets that suggest a narrow concept (e.g. powers of 4), so the same membership belief leads to different answers depending on the expected breadth of the concept.

## upper_edge_marking_ranges  — rank 17, 3.4 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.4

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts` (with its prime concept corrected to exclude 1, as in the incumbent): people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, but they assume the program's examples mark where its range stops at the top — the largest example shown is taken as evidence that the range ends there. The one change, grafted in one-sided form from `endpoint_marking_interval_sampling`: a range whose upper end coincides with the largest example gets an inferred evidence bonus, while its lower end is unconstrained, so people generalize downward from the examples more readily than upward (after 16 they accept 15 or 13 more than 17 or 19; after 83,77 they reject 84).

## prime_concept_excludes_one  — rank 18, 3.6 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.6

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules (kind-level rule prior, Erlang width prior with a decade-boundary preference, size principle with 10% random examples, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their concept of "prime numbers" is the mathematical one, which excludes 1. The one change is removing 1 from the prime concept: when 1 is among the examples (e.g. 31,3,1) people cannot explain the set as primes and fall back on "odd numbers" (yes to 9 or 25, no special yes to 2), and after a set of primes (e.g. 67,31,17,61) they answer "no" to 1 rather than accepting it as a prime.

## spread_sampled_ranges  — rank 19, 4.1 ± 2.5 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.2

People learn the program's concept by Bayesian inference over arithmetic rules and numeric ranges, as in the best model, but with an inferred probability they believe a program that produces a range spreads its examples evenly across it (one example from each equal stretch of the range) instead of scattering them at random. A set of several evenly spaced examples is then far likelier under the range they span than under a rule or a random draw, while a range reaching well past the outermost examples, or a set with clumped examples, gets no such boost. The sharpest disagreement with the best model is on widely and evenly spaced sets of three or four examples (e.g. 20,35,68,92): this model says "yes" more readily to any number inside their span, whatever its parity or divisibility, and its range-based "yes" stops close to the outermost examples.

## upper_edge_discounted_range_size  — rank 20, 4.2 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.2

Refinement of `upper_edge_marking_ranges`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and they take the largest example as marking where the program's range stops at the top. The one change, grafted from `range_discounted_size_principle`: people apply the size principle at full strength only to arithmetic rules and only partly to numeric ranges, judging a range's fit to the examples as if it were wider than it is (by an inferred degree), so it is the marked top edge — not the size principle — that stops range generalization going upward, while downward from the examples "yes" spreads further across their neighbourhood than strong sampling predicts.

## uncertain_sampling_assumption  — rank 21, 4.5 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.6

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges of 1-100 (moderate, decade-bounded ranges preferred, the largest example marking a range's top), but they are unsure how the examples were chosen: with an inferred probability they assume the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling, so a small concept that fits is strongly favoured), and otherwise that the examples are merely numbers the program happens to accept (weak sampling, so every concept containing them stays as plausible as it was a priori). Their "yes" averages over both readings, so broad concepts such as "even numbers" or wide ranges keep a floor of support however many examples fit a narrower one, and generalization tightens with more examples less than an ideal strong-sampling learner predicts — for rules and ranges alike.

## uncertain_sampling_top_marking  — rank 22, 4.9 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.9

Refinement of `uncertain_sampling_assumption`. People still do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and ranges of 1-100, and they are still unsure whether the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling) or are just numbers it happens to accept (weak sampling). The one change, taken from `top_marking_range_sampling`, is how the largest example marks the top of a range: instead of a fixed bonus, people think that with some inferred odds the program deliberately showed the top of its range and drew the other examples at random. A range ending at the largest example is then credited by how unlikely a chance draw would have been to hit that number under each sampling reading. Under strong sampling this credit is large for wide ranges and shrinks as more examples are shown; under weak sampling it is flat. So downward generalization from sparse sets is strong, and upward generalization is blocked, under both readings.

## top_marking_uncertain_sampling  — rank 23, 5.1 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3103.1

Refinement of `top_marking_range_sampling`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that with inferred odds the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random — but, the one change grafted from `uncertain_sampling_assumption`, they are unsure whether the remaining examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling, size principle) or are merely numbers it happens to accept (weak sampling), and average over both readings with an inferred probability. ## information_weighted_prototype_matching  — rank 24, 1027.1 ± 49.4 nats behind the best (20.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4125.1

People do not infer a rule; they abstract a single prototype from the examples — the examples' average magnitude and how far they spread, plus the proportion of examples that have each familiar number property (even, multiple of 3, 4, 5 or 10, prime, square, cube, power of two, final digit) — and say "yes" to a number in proportion to how closely it matches that prototype, where distance in magnitude is judged relative to the examples' spread and missing a property most examples share costs more the rarer that property is among numbers. Because the comparison is to the central tendency rather than to the stored examples, an example that is atypical of the rest (such as 100 after 31,19,49) is itself often rejected, and generalization spreads symmetrically around the examples' centre.

## stray_closing_example  — no comparison row

People read the examples in order and suspect that the program's closing example may be a stray: with an inferred probability, they believe the last number shown (in a set of two or more) was a random number tacked onto the demonstration rather than a member of the program's concept. Otherwise they reason as in the best model (Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, ranges and range-restricted rules, a last-shown largest example marking a range's top, a first-shown smallest marking its bottom), and under the stray reading they learn from the earlier examples alone. So the last-shown example is itself rejected more often than earlier ones (e.g. "no" to 100 after 31,19,49,100), and a closing example that breaks the earlier examples' pattern loosens generalization less than an ideal learner predicts.

## shared_final_digit_similarity  — no comparison row

People judge a number as similar to an example when it ends in the same digit, and they entertain "numbers that look like one of the examples by their last digit" as a candidate concept of its own: when the examples end in different digits (e.g. 23,47), the numbers ending in any of those digits (every number ending in 3 or 7) are a possible concept, weighed by its size and an inferred prior share alongside the best model's arithmetic rules, ranges (with the last-shown largest example marking the top and a first-shown smallest the bottom) and range-restricted rules. So people say "yes" to numbers far outside the examples' span that share an example's last digit (13 or 17 after 23,47) more than to neighbours that share none (15 or 18), even though no ends-in-d rule fits all the examples.

## concept_confusion_decision_noise  — no comparison row

People infer the program's concept by plain Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges, but how consistently they act on their belief depends on how sure they are which concept the program has. When the examples point clearly to one concept (e.g. 2,4,8,16,32), people answer decisively, close to all-or-none from their membership belief. When many concepts remain plausible (e.g. a single example like 60), their answers become noisier and drift toward 50/50, with how strongly confusion about the concept degrades answer consistency inferred from the data.

## final_digit_pair_concepts  — no comparison row

Refinement of `sequence_opens_bottom_ends_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with a last-shown largest example marking a range's top and a first-shown smallest example (in sets of several) marking its bottom. The one change: people treat a number's ones digit as a feature that may take either of two values, so their concepts also include "numbers ending in d or e" (e.g. "ending in 3 or 7"), a kind of rule with its own inferred prior share. So after 23,47 people say "yes" to 3, 7, 13 or 17 outside the examples' span more than to neighbours such as 5 or 12, which addresses the critique that targets sharing a ones digit with some example are accepted more than the best model predicts.
