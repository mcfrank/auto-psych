# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## last_example_marks_range_top  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3099.1

Refinement of `top_marking_range_sampling`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that, with some inferred odds, the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random — but they believe the program ends its demonstration on that top number. The one change: the marked top is read from the order of presentation, so a range ending at the largest example gets the generative top-marking credit (larger for wider ranges) only when the largest example is the last one shown, and then at full strength whatever the set size, while sets whose largest example comes earlier get no edge credit — after 20,35,60 people say "yes" to 45 but not 62, whereas after 60,35,20 they extend above 60 as readily as below 20.

## top_marking_range_sampling  — rank 1, 2.2 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.3

Refinement of `upper_edge_marking_ranges`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe the program shows where its range stops at the top — but they reason about this generatively rather than granting a fixed bonus. The one change: people assume that, with some inferred odds, the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random, so the largest example is explained by marking instead of by chance; a range ending at the largest example is therefore credited in proportion to how unlikely a random draw would have been to hit that number — far more for wide ranges than narrow ones, and less as more examples are shown — so single examples and sparse sets license especially strong downward generalization (after 60, "yes" to 40 but not 62), while dense sets fall back toward ordinary size-principle ranges.

## upper_edge_marking_ranges  — rank 2, 2.3 ± 2.4 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.4

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts` (with its prime concept corrected to exclude 1, as in the incumbent): people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, but they assume the program's examples mark where its range stops at the top — the largest example shown is taken as evidence that the range ends there. The one change, grafted in one-sided form from `endpoint_marking_interval_sampling`: a range whose upper end coincides with the largest example gets an inferred evidence bonus, while its lower end is unconstrained, so people generalize downward from the examples more readily than upward (after 16 they accept 15 or 13 more than 17 or 19; after 83,77 they reject 84).

## prime_concept_excludes_one  — rank 3, 2.5 ± 2.6 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3101.6

Refinement of `range_restricted_rule_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules (kind-level rule prior, Erlang width prior with a decade-boundary preference, size principle with 10% random examples, "yes" = posterior probability the concept contains the target), but their concept of "prime numbers" is the mathematical one, which excludes 1. The one change is removing 1 from the prime concept: when 1 is among the examples (e.g. 31,3,1) people cannot explain the set as primes and fall back on "odd numbers" (yes to 9 or 25, no special yes to 2), and after a set of primes (e.g. 67,31,17,61) they answer "no" to 1 rather than accepting it as a prime.

## upper_edge_discounted_range_size  — rank 4, 3.1 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.2

Refinement of `upper_edge_marking_ranges`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and they take the largest example as marking where the program's range stops at the top. The one change, grafted from `range_discounted_size_principle`: people apply the size principle at full strength only to arithmetic rules and only partly to numeric ranges, judging a range's fit to the examples as if it were wider than it is (by an inferred degree), so it is the marked top edge — not the size principle — that stops range generalization going upward, while downward from the examples "yes" spreads further across their neighbourhood than strong sampling predicts.

## uncertain_sampling_assumption  — rank 5, 3.5 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.6

People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges of 1-100 (moderate, decade-bounded ranges preferred, the largest example marking a range's top), but they are unsure how the examples were chosen: with an inferred probability they assume the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling, so a small concept that fits is strongly favoured), and otherwise that the examples are merely numbers the program happens to accept (weak sampling, so every concept containing them stays as plausible as it was a priori). Their "yes" averages over both readings, so broad concepts such as "even numbers" or wide ranges keep a floor of support however many examples fit a narrower one, and generalization tightens with more examples less than an ideal strong-sampling learner predicts — for rules and ranges alike.

## uncertain_sampling_top_marking  — rank 6, 3.8 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.9

Refinement of `uncertain_sampling_assumption`. People still do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and ranges of 1-100, and they are still unsure whether the examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling) or are just numbers it happens to accept (weak sampling). The one change, taken from `top_marking_range_sampling`, is how the largest example marks the top of a range: instead of a fixed bonus, people think that with some inferred odds the program deliberately showed the top of its range and drew the other examples at random. A range ending at the largest example is then credited by how unlikely a chance draw would have been to hit that number under each sampling reading. Under strong sampling this credit is large for wide ranges and shrinks as more examples are shown; under weak sampling it is flat. So downward generalization from sparse sets is strong, and upward generalization is blocked, under both readings.

## top_marking_uncertain_sampling  — rank 7, 4.0 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3103.1

Refinement of `top_marking_range_sampling`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that with inferred odds the program deliberately shows its range's top number and draws the other examples at random — but, the one change grafted from `uncertain_sampling_assumption`, they are unsure whether the remaining examples were drawn from the program's concept (strong sampling, size principle) or are merely numbers it happens to accept (weak sampling), and average over both readings with an inferred probability. So the marked top still blocks upward generalization, while broad concepts ("even numbers", wide ranges ending at the largest example) keep a floor of support however many examples fit a narrower one, and "yes" tightens with more examples less than the incumbent predicts.

## breadth_adaptive_yes_criterion  — no comparison row

People infer the program's concept by plain Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1) and numeric ranges, but the criterion they use to say "yes" is not fixed: it adapts to how many of the numbers 1-100 they believe the program produces. When the examples suggest a broad concept (e.g. "even numbers", a wide range) they shift how sure they must be that a target belongs before answering "yes" by an inferred amount, relative to sets that suggest a narrow concept (e.g. powers of 4), so the same membership belief leads to different answers depending on the expected breadth of the concept.

## digit_sum_concepts  — no comparison row

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic rules, ranges of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, with the size principle and the last-shown largest example marking a range's top), but their space of candidate concepts also includes digit-sum concepts — "numbers whose digits add up to the same total" (e.g. 18, 27, 36, 45 … 90 for a total of nine; 15, 24, 33, 42, 51, 60 for six) — as a kind of rule of its own with an inferred prior share. So examples that share a digit sum (36,45,54,81 or 15,51) lead people to say "yes" to other numbers with that digit sum (90 or 27; 24 or 60) but not to rule-mates that break it (99 after 36,45,54,81).

## information_weighted_prototype_matching  — no comparison row

People do not infer a rule; they abstract a single prototype from the examples — the examples' average magnitude and how far they spread, plus the proportion of examples that have each familiar number property (even, multiple of 3, 4, 5 or 10, prime, square, cube, power of two, final digit) — and say "yes" to a number in proportion to how closely it matches that prototype, where distance in magnitude is judged relative to the examples' spread and missing a property most examples share costs more the rarer that property is among numbers. Because the comparison is to the central tendency rather than to the stored examples, an example that is atypical of the rest (such as 100 after 31,19,49) is itself often rejected, and generalization spreads symmetrically around the examples' centre.

## last_example_marks_range_edge  — no comparison row

Refinement of `last_example_marks_range_top`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (primes excluding 1), every range of 1-100 and range-restricted rules, and believe that, with some inferred odds, the program ends its demonstration on a boundary of its range and draws the other examples at random. The one change: the last example marks whichever boundary it is, not only the top — a sequence ending on its largest example marks the range's top (as in the incumbent), while a sequence ending on its smallest example marks the range's bottom with the same generative credit (larger for wider ranges), so after 60,35,20 people say "yes" to 45 but less to 15, the mirror image of 20,35,60, and only sets ending on a middle example get no edge credit.
