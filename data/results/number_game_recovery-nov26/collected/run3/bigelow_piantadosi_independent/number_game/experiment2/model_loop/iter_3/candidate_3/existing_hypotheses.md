# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## prime_over_odd_reading  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2200.5

Refinement of `hundred_chart_row_ranges` (everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change is that "odd numbers" and "prime numbers" are rival readings of the same kind of evidence (numbers with no factor of 2), and people find "prime" the more meaningful reading and "odd" the emptier, merely "not even" one: a single fitted preference moves prior plausibility away from "odd numbers" and onto "prime numbers" by the same amount. So after all-prime sets (2, 47; 53, 2; 67, 31, 17, 61) people say yes to other primes and no to odd composites, and after all-odd non-prime sets (69, 9, 39, 21) they extend "odd" less than the incumbent predicts and fall back on ranges or narrower rules.

## marked_odd_parity_concepts  — rank 3, 14.0 ± 3.7 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2214.5

"Odd" is a marked, negatively defined category: people think of an odd number as one that is "not even" rather than by a positive property, so "odd numbers" is harder to entertain as the program's rule than "even numbers", even though both are equally simple and equally large. As a result, after all-odd examples (e.g. 19, 73 or 7, 31) people often reject other odd numbers (63, 81, 87, 21) and fall back on a range or a narrower rule. After all-even examples they readily extend "even". Everything else in how they infer the rule is unchanged: primitive-built rules, the size principle, hundred-chart ranges and near-all-or-none commitment.

## sieve_prime_concepts  — rank 5, 41.3 ± 16.0 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2241.8

People recognise "prime numbers" not by knowing the true primes but by a quick divisibility sieve: a number counts as prime-like if it is not even, does not end in 0 or 5, and is not in the 3 times table (2, 3 and 5 themselves included). This sieve concept is a familiar, readily entertained rule with its own fitted prior bonus, layered on the best model's concept search (primitive costs, final-digit discount, power/square costs, factor-graded multiples, hundred-chart row ranges, size principle, near-all-or-none commitment), so after all-prime sets such as 53, 47, 41 or 19, 73 people say yes to other primes and no to odd multiples of 3 or 5. The sharpest disagreement with the best model (and with true-prime salience) lives at odd composites that survive the sieve, 49, 77 and 91: after a prime set people should say yes to them about as often as to real primes.

## first_example_overridden_concepts  — rank 11, 143.8 ± 17.6 nats behind the best (8.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2344.3

People revise their guess about the concept as each example arrives, so the later examples carry the final say: the first example shown is the one most readily dismissed as a stray that the program did not really mean, while later examples are trusted to belong to the concept. The concept they settle on (built from primitives with simplicity costs, a final-digit discount, extra costs for powers and squares, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment) may therefore ignore a first example that breaks the pattern the later ones share, and reordering the same examples (e.g. 8,16 versus 16,8) changes which concept wins.

Refinement of `final_digit_multiplicative_concepts`. People still build number concepts from mental primitives: a fitted cost per extra primitive, a fitted prior discount for salient "ends in d" rules, Erlang-weighted intervals, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment to the best-explaining concept. The one change splits the single cost on repeated-multiplication concepts in two. ## repeat_averse_concept_learning  — rank 13, 149.1 ± 18.8 nats behind the best (7.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2349.6

People infer the hidden concept as a Bayesian learner would: they build number rules from simple primitives and weigh them, and numeric ranges, by the size principle. The one distortion is that they treat the program as producing distinct numbers, as if it sampled without replacement, so a number already shown as an example seems less likely to come up again. As a result they sometimes say "no" to a target that is itself one of the examples, even though it certainly fits the concept, while their answers to every other target are unchanged.

## place_value_disjunctive_contains  — rank 15, 286.3 ± 27.4 nats behind the best (10.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2486.8

Refinement of `multiplicative_not_exponential_concepts`: people still build number concepts from mental primitives (a fitted cost per extra primitive, a fitted extra cost on powers, squares and cubes, Erlang-weighted intervals, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment to the best-explaining concept). The one change is how "contains the digit d" is represented: people read numerals by place value (tens digit, ones digit), so "contains d" is not an elementary property but a disjunction, "the ones digit is d OR the tens digit is d", and it costs one more primitive than "ends in d" or "multiples of m", priced by the same per-primitive cost with no new free parameter. So when every example contains a digit (e.g. 92, 82, 27, 42), people seldom commit to "contains 2" and say no to targets that contain the digit without sharing an example's ending (23, 29, 12), as the critique reports.

## diagnostic_attention_exemplar_similarity  — rank 16, 520.4 ± 36.0 nats behind the best (14.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2720.9

People do not infer a hidden concept: they hold the examples in memory as exemplars and say yes to a target the more similar it is, on average, to those stored examples. Similarity is judged over familiar number properties (parity, primality, squares, cubes, powers of 2, multiples, final digit, decade, digits contained) and magnitude. Attention shifts to whatever the examples conspicuously share, with more attention the rarer the shared property is and, for magnitude, the more tightly the examples cluster. So after an all-prime set, a non-prime target mismatches every example on a heavily attended dimension and is rejected, while other primes are accepted.

## spacing_scaled_distance_gradient  — rank 17, 766.4 ± 45.0 nats behind the best (17.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2967.0

People judge a target by its numerical distance to the nearest example: willingness to say yes falls off exponentially (a Shepard generalization gradient) as the target moves away from the closest example on the number line. The width of that gradient is not fixed but scales with how widely spaced the examples are, so tightly clustered examples (e.g. 60, 62, 64) produce a narrow gradient that rejects numbers a few steps away, while widely spaced examples (e.g. 16, 8, 64) produce a broad gradient that accepts numbers far from any example.
