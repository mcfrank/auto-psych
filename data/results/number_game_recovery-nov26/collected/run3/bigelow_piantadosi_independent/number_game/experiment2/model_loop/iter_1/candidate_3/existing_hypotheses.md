# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## factor_graded_multiples_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2304.2

Refinement of `costly_squares_final_digit_concepts`. Everything is kept: concepts built from mental primitives with a fitted cost per extra primitive, a discount for "ends in d" rules, separate extra costs for powers/cubes and for perfect squares, Erlang-weighted ranges, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. The one change is that "multiples of m" rules are not equally available for every m. People check divisibility by recalling a times table, and larger tables are less fluent, so the prior cost of a multiples rule grows with the logarithm of its factor, at a fitted rate. People therefore readily read sets as multiples of 3, 4 or 5 (e.g. 20, 32, 92 as multiples of 4; 90, 100, 5 as multiples of 5). After 14, 98, 91 or 8, 64 they seldom commit to "multiples of 7" or "multiples of 8" and say no to other multiples (7, 35, 63; 24, 40, 56).

## forward_counting_final_digit_concepts  — rank 1, 12.3 ± 12.7 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2316.5

Refinement of the pruned `final_digit_multiplicative_concepts`: people still build number concepts from mental primitives (a fitted cost per extra primitive, a fitted extra cost on powers, squares and cubes, a fitted prior discount on salient "ends in d" rules, Erlang-weighted ranges, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change, taken from `forward_counting_range_sampling`, is how people think a range-type program produces its numbers: they picture it counting upward from where the range starts, so the examples are read as early, low members of the range, with the strength of this forward-counting assumption fitted. So when a set is explained by a range, people say yes more to numbers a little above the examples than to numbers equally far below them, while their rule-based answers (parity, multiples, final digits) are unchanged.

## first_example_overridden_concepts  — rank 2, 40.1 ± 11.8 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2344.3

People revise their guess about the concept as each example arrives, so the later examples carry the final say: the first example shown is the one most readily dismissed as a stray that the program did not really mean, while later examples are trusted to belong to the concept. The concept they settle on (built from primitives with simplicity costs, a final-digit discount, extra costs for powers and squares, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment) may therefore ignore a first example that breaks the pattern the later ones share, and reordering the same examples (e.g. 8,16 versus 16,8) changes which concept wins.

## costly_squares_final_digit_concepts  — rank 3, 42.9 ± 10.7 nats behind the best (4.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2347.1

Refinement of `final_digit_multiplicative_concepts`. People still build number concepts from mental primitives: a fitted cost per extra primitive, a fitted prior discount for salient "ends in d" rules, Erlang-weighted intervals, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment to the best-explaining concept. The one change splits the single cost on repeated-multiplication concepts in two. "Perfect squares" (a number multiplied by itself) pays its own fitted extra cost, separate from the cost shared by powers of a base (and their exception variants) and cubes. The claim is that self-multiplication is the hardest of these to conceive, harder than repeatedly multiplying by a fixed base. So after square sets such as 100, 1, 16 or 4, 64, 25, 81, people rarely commit to "squares". Instead they fall back on other rules, ranges or scattered guesses. Meanwhile, powers of 2 or 3 stay more available than a single cost on all of these concepts allows.

## repeat_averse_concept_learning  — rank 4, 45.4 ± 10.7 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2349.6

People infer the hidden concept as a Bayesian learner would: they build number rules from simple primitives and weigh them, and numeric ranges, by the size principle. The one distortion is that they treat the program as producing distinct numbers, as if it sampled without replacement, so a number already shown as an example seems less likely to come up again. As a result they sometimes say "no" to a target that is itself one of the examples, even though it certainly fits the concept, while their answers to every other target are unchanged.

## small_number_expectation_concepts  — rank 5, 59.5 ± 11.4 nats behind the best (5.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2363.8

Refinement of `costly_squares_final_digit_concepts`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as in that model (primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, a final-digit discount, extra costs on powers/cubes and on squares, Erlang-weighted intervals, the size principle, near-all-or-none commitment). The one change is a small-number expectation: people believe a computer program is more likely to produce small numbers than large ones, so their readiness to say "yes" falls steadily with the target's magnitude at a fitted rate. For the same concept belief, a target in the 80s or 90s gets fewer yeses than one in the teens or 20s.

## place_value_disjunctive_contains  — rank 6, 182.6 ± 26.7 nats behind the best (6.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2486.8

Refinement of `multiplicative_not_exponential_concepts`: people still build number concepts from mental primitives (a fitted cost per extra primitive, a fitted extra cost on powers, squares and cubes, Erlang-weighted intervals, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment to the best-explaining concept). The one change is how "contains the digit d" is represented: people read numerals by place value (tens digit, ones digit), so "contains d" is not an elementary property but a disjunction, "the ones digit is d OR the tens digit is d", and it costs one more primitive than "ends in d" or "multiples of m", priced by the same per-primitive cost with no new free parameter. So when every example contains a digit (e.g. 92, 82, 27, 42), people seldom commit to "contains 2" and say no to targets that contain the digit without sharing an example's ending (23, 29, 12), as the critique reports.
