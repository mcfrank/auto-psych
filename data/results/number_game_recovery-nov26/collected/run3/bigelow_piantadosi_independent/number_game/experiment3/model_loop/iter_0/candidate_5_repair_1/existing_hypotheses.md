# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## repdigit_pattern_chart_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3586.7

Refinement of the incumbent `prime_over_odd_reading`, with everything in it kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, the final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, the single preference that moves plausibility from "odd" to "prime", Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. The one change adds a concept the incumbent lacks. People notice when a numeral's two digits are the same (11, 22, ..., 99), so "repeated-digit numbers" is a candidate rule, priced like other two-primitive rules plus its own fitted prior bonus. After sets holding two or more repdigits (e.g. 33, 77 or 44, 11, 88), people say yes to other repdigits and no to numbers that merely lie near the examples, which closes the critique's report that the incumbent under-predicts yes to repdigit targets after repdigit sets.

## prime_salient_hundred_chart  — rank 1, 134.7 ± 29.6 nats behind the best (4.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3721.5

Refinement of `hundred_chart_row_ranges` (everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change, taken from `prime_salient_concepts`, is that "prime numbers" is a familiar school-math category kept ready to hand, so it gets a fitted prior bonus over other compound rules. So after all-prime sets such as 2, 47 or 67, 31, 17, 61, people commit to "primes": they say yes to other primes more than to odd composite numbers, which the current best model gets backwards.

## prime_salient_marked_odd  — rank 2, 135.5 ± 28.5 nats behind the best (4.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3722.2

Refinement of `prime_salient_hundred_chart` (everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, a fitted prior bonus for the familiar category "prime numbers", the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change, taken from `marked_odd_parity_concepts`, is that "odd" is a marked, negatively defined category ("not even"), so the rule "odd numbers" carries a fitted extra prior cost that "even numbers" does not. As a result, after all-prime sets of odd primes (53, 47, 41; 67, 31, 17, 61) "odd" competes less with "primes", and people say yes to other primes but no to odd composites (9, 21, 63, 81) more sharply than prime salience alone predicts, while after all-even sets they still extend "even" readily.

## prime_over_odd_reading  — rank 3, 136.9 ± 25.4 nats behind the best (5.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3723.7

Refinement of `hundred_chart_row_ranges` (everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change is that "odd numbers" and "prime numbers" are rival readings of the same kind of evidence (numbers with no factor of 2), and people find "prime" the more meaningful reading and "odd" the emptier, merely "not even" one: a single fitted preference moves prior plausibility away from "odd numbers" and onto "prime numbers" by the same amount. So after all-prime sets (2, 47; 53, 2; 67, 31, 17, 61) people say yes to other primes and no to odd composites, and after all-odd non-prime sets (69, 9, 39, 21) they extend "odd" less than the incumbent predicts and fall back on ranges or narrower rules.

## irregular_threes_times_table  — rank 4, 145.3 ± 25.9 nats behind the best (5.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3732.1

Refinement of the incumbent `prime_over_odd_reading`. Everything in it is kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, the final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, the single preference that moves plausibility from "odd" to "prime", Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. The one change is that the 3 times table has no visible pattern in the written numeral (multiples of 2, 5 or 10 can be read off the last digit, multiples of 3 cannot), so people seldom notice that a set is all "multiples of 3", and that one rule carries its own fitted extra prior cost. So after sets of multiples of 3 such as 69, 9, 39, 21 or 63, 27, 99, 33, people extend "odd", a final digit or a range instead, and say yes to other multiples of 3 hardly more often than to non-multiples, where the incumbent over-generalises "multiples of 3".

## hidden_divisibility_by_three  — rank 5, 160.0 ± 28.9 nats behind the best (5.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3746.7

Refinement of `prime_salient_marked_odd` (everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, a prime bonus, a marked-odd cost, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change is that divisibility by 3 cannot be read off a numeral's last digit the way divisibility by 2, 5 or 10 can; it needs a digit-sum check. So every rule whose membership test contains that check ("multiples of 3", "of 6", "of 9", "of 12") carries one shared, fitted extra prior cost. As a result, after odd multiples of 3 such as 69, 9, 39, 21 or 63, 27, 99, 33, people seldom commit to "multiples of 3". They extend "odd", a final digit or a range instead, and say yes to odd non-multiples of 3 (19, 29, 49, 23, 37) about as readily as to other multiples of 3.

## single_example_guessing  — no comparison row

A single example is too little evidence to settle on a rule, so on a fitted share of single-example trials people stop trying to work out the program and simply guess yes or no at even odds. With two or more examples they answer from the concept they inferred, using the best model's inference unchanged. So after a lone example (e.g. 16 or 81), numbers far from it get noticeably more yeses than the inferred concept implies, and the falloff from near to far targets is flatter than it is after sets of several examples.

## digit_reversal_pair_concepts  — no comparison row

People notice when two numerals are written with the same two digits in swapped order (16 and 61, 47 and 74, 38 and 83). So "a number and its digit-reversal" is a concept they can entertain for the hidden program. It is a tiny two-member rule, priced like other two-part rules plus its own fitted prior cost or bonus, added to the current best model's concept search, which is otherwise unchanged: primitive-built rules, final-digit discount, power and square costs, graded times tables, prime over odd, repdigits, hundred-chart ranges, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. So after a set such as 47, 2, 74, or a lone example such as 61, people say yes to the reversed numeral (74, 16) more than to its numerical neighbours, which the current best model cannot predict.

## minerva_category_trace_echo  — no comparison row

People do not infer a concept; they hold in long-term memory traces of familiar number categories (parity, primes, squares, cubes, powers, times tables, final digits, contained digits, repeated digits, and stretches of the number line), and the examples act as a memory probe that activates each stored trace in proportion to a steep power of its overlap with the examples (shared members over members in either, as in MINERVA 2 echo retrieval). They say yes to the target in proportion to how strongly the resulting echo — the activation-weighted blend of the retrieved traces — contains it, so broad categories are only weakly retrieved by a few examples and, unlike the size principle, the sharpness of retrieval does not grow with the number of examples shown.

## set_size_graded_evidence_weight  — no comparison row

Refinement of the incumbent `repdigit_pattern_chart_concepts`. Everything in it is kept: primitive-built rules with per-primitive costs, the final-digit discount, costs on powers and squares, graded multiples, prime over odd, the repdigit rule, hundred-chart ranges, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. The one change is how people pool evidence across examples. Agreeing examples are more convincing together than one at a time, so the weight each example carries grows with the set's size at a fitted rate. From a single example people therefore commit only weakly to a tight range or rule and generalise broadly; they do not answer by a steep nearness gradient around it. Once three or four examples agree, they commit more sharply than an ideal learner would.

## prime_bonus_odd_cost_decoupled  — no comparison row

Refinement of the incumbent `repdigit_pattern_chart_concepts`, with everything kept: primitive-built rules with a per-primitive cost, the final-digit discount, costs on powers/cubes and squares, times-table-graded multiples, the repeated-digit rule with its own bonus, Erlang ranges with a bonus for whole hundred-chart rows, the size principle and near-all-or-none commitment. The one change is about "prime" versus "odd". The incumbent treats them as one trade-off: whatever plausibility "prime numbers" gains, "odd numbers" loses. Here they are two separate facts about people, taken from `prime_salient_marked_odd`. "Prime" is a familiar category with its own fitted bonus, and "odd" is a marked "not even" category with its own fitted cost of a different size. So after all-prime sets people still commit to "primes", but after odd sets with no primes in them (63, 27, 99, 33) they extend "odd" to odd non-examples (23, 31, 37, 43) more than the incumbent's coupled trade-off allows.
