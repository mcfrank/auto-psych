# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## round_landmark_veto  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3485.4

Round numbers (10, 20, ..., 100) are landmarks on the mental number line rather than ordinary outputs, so people check a round target against the examples before accepting it: if none of the examples is itself a round number, on a fitted share of such trials they doubt that the program produces round numbers and answer no, whatever concept they inferred. Concept inference itself is unchanged from the current best model, so the claim shows up only after sets with no multiple of 10 (e.g. 8, 64 or 32, 4, 16), where people say no to 10, 20, 30 or 70 more often than the concept they inferred (e.g. "even numbers") implies.

## accumulating_round_skip_evidence  — rank 1, 3.4 ± 1.1 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3488.8

Refinement of the incumbent `set_size_evidence_privileged_fives`, with all of its concept inference kept (primitive-built rules with per-primitive costs, final-digit discount, power and square costs, graded times tables, prime bonus and odd cost, repdigits, privileged multiples of 5, hundred-chart ranges, the size principle, set-size-graded evidence and near-all-or-none commitment). The one change is that people notice, example by example, that the program seems to skip round numbers (10, 20, ..., 100): each example that is not round is a separate chance to notice this, and once they have noticed it they reject a round target even if it fits the rule they inferred. So rejection of round targets grows with the number of non-round examples: it is weak after a lone 16 and strong after 32, 4, 16, 8, unlike a fixed round-number veto. Round targets after sets with any round example are judged by the inferred concept alone.

## round_number_unrepresentativeness  — rank 3, 4.8 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3490.2

People infer the hidden program as an ideal concept learner does (simple, familiar rules and ranges weighed by how well they explain the examples), but they add one representativeness bias. Round numbers (multiples of 10) are a salient class of their own, so when none of the examples is round, a round target looks unlike anything the program has produced, and people say yes to it less often than the concept they inferred implies. When some example is round, round targets are judged by the inferred concept alone.

## decade_block_ranges  — rank 6, 20.6 ± 6.6 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3506.0

People divide the number line into spoken decades ("the thirties" = 30 to 39), not into the rows of a hundred chart (31 to 40). When they fall back on a range around the examples, the natural blocks they prefer are whole decades, which start at a round number and stop just before the next one. So after examples such as 34, 37 people say yes to 30 more readily than to 40, and in general a round number just above the examples' decade gets fewer yeses than the current best model (whose favoured blocks end on round numbers) predicts. Everything else in how they infer the program is as in the current best model.

## example_spacing_sparsity_bias  — rank 7, 56.1 ± 9.9 nats behind the best (5.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3541.6

People read how far apart the examples are as a clue to how sparse the hidden program's output is: widely spaced examples (e.g. 3, 48, 99) suggest a program that produces only a few scattered numbers, while closely spaced examples suggest a program that produces many. So the wider the average gap between neighbouring examples, the less ready people are to say yes to any new number, beyond what the rule or range they infer from the examples implies. That inference is otherwise unchanged from the current best model.

## weber_scaled_range_widths  — rank 17, 125.1 ± 18.6 nats behind the best (6.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3610.5

People represent numbers on a compressed mental number line, so the same numerical gap feels smaller among large numbers than among small ones (Weber's law). When they consider that the program produces a stretch of the number line, the range width they find natural is therefore proportional to the magnitude of the examples: after examples in the 70s-90s they readily extend to numbers ten or twenty away, while after examples below 20 they expect a tight range and reject numbers only a few steps away. How they infer rules (parity, primes, times tables, final digits, repdigits, powers) is unchanged from the current best model.

## minerva_category_trace_echo  — rank 23, 896.6 ± 46.6 nats behind the best (19.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4382.1

People do not infer a concept; they hold in long-term memory traces of familiar number categories (parity, primes, squares, cubes, powers, times tables, final digits, contained digits, repeated digits, and stretches of the number line), and the examples act as a memory probe that activates each stored trace in proportion to a steep power of its overlap with the examples (shared members over members in either, as in MINERVA 2 echo retrieval). They say yes to the target in proportion to how strongly the resulting echo — the activation-weighted blend of the retrieved traces — contains it, so broad categories are only weakly retrieved by a few examples and, unlike the size principle, the sharpness of retrieval does not grow with the number of examples shown.

## first_example_anchored_trust  — no comparison row

People build their guess about the program from the first example they see and then check each later example against that guess, so the first example anchors the concept: it is less readily set aside as a stray number than any later example, at a fitted rate. Concept inference is otherwise that of the current best model, with the round-number veto kept. So a rule or range that fits the first example but misses a later one survives better than one that fits the later examples but misses the first, and a target that shares the first example's rule or region gets more yeses than one that shares only a later example's.

## log_ratio_nearest_example_similarity  — no comparison row

People judge a target by its similarity to the nearest example on a logarithmically compressed mental number line: the farther the target lies from the closest example in log-magnitude (ratio) terms, the less likely they are to say yes, falling off smoothly at a fitted rate. Because the scale is compressed, a gap of 10 counts as small among numbers in the 80s but as large among numbers below 20, so people generalise widely around large examples and tightly around small ones, with no rule inference at all.

## forward_continuation_response_bias  — no comparison row

At the answer stage people treat the examples as what the program has produced so far, and they assume it runs upward. So, beyond what the concept they inferred implies, they lean toward yes for a target above the largest example and toward no for a target below the smallest. The strength of this lean is fitted, and targets inside the examples' span are unaffected. Concept inference is the current best model's, unchanged (including its round-number veto). So after 32, 4, 16, a target such as 40 or 60 gets more yeses than 2 or 3, even where the inferred concept rates them equally.
