People take the smallest and largest example shown as the limits of a possible program, so "numbers from the lowest to the highest example" comes to mind as a magnitude explanation much more readily than any other range. Otherwise they learn the concept in the usual Bayesian way: equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a range, and magnitude ranges, the size principle, an uncertain stray rate, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers. So when examples that share a rule sit close together (37, 57 or 4, 16, 12), people often fill in the rule's non-members between them (44, 7, 13) but do not stretch that magnitude reading past the outermost examples, whereas the min-to-max range of a widely spread set is too large to compete with the rule.
