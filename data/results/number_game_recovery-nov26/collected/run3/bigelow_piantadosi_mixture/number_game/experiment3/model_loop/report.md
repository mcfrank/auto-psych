## Hypotheses

- **approximate_near_miss_rules**: Besides exact mathematical rules, people entertain approximate versions of sparse rules: "numbers at or just next to a power of 2 / square / multiple of 10", so a set of near-misses of a small rule (1, 3, 15, 31 are each one off a power of 2) is explained by the approximate rule, and people then endorse the rule's exact members and its other near-misses (2, 4, 16, 17). These approximate rules hold a small stated share of the belief in rules (about 3%), split equally among them; otherwise learning is the incumbent's Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible rules, rules held within a range, and magnitude ranges with a salient min-to-max range, the size principle, uncertain reliability with familiar strays, and a rule-versus-range expectation that varies across judgements.
- **example_count_scaled_hull**: Refinement of `variable_min_max_bounded_range`. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, uncertain stray rate, strays sometimes familiar numbers), with a rule-versus-range expectation that varies from one judgement to the next, and the range from the smallest to the largest example comes to mind more readily than any other range. The one change is how readily it comes to mind: each example after the first that falls between those limits adds another bit of evidence that "numbers from the lowest to the highest example" is the program, so the extra salience of that range grows in proportion to the number of examples minus one, with no new parameter. With two examples people therefore fill in the span between them less often than the incumbent predicts, and with four clustered examples they fill it in more often.
- **variable_min_max_bounded_range**: Refinement of `min_max_bounded_range`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, uncertain stray rate, strays sometimes familiar numbers), and they treat the smallest and largest example as the natural limits of a range, so "numbers from the lowest to the highest example" comes to mind far more readily than any other range. The one change, taken from `variable_rule_range_expectation`, is that how strongly people expect the program to follow a rule rather than produce a magnitude range varies from one judgement to the next around an inferred average. So for clustered examples that share a rule (37, 57 or 4, 16, 12), people on some judgements adopt the min-to-max range and fill in the rule's non-members between the examples, never beyond them, and on other judgements they keep the rule.
- **variable_rule_range_expectation**: Refinement of `range_limited_rule_familiar_strays`. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers. The one change is that how strongly people expect the program to follow a mathematical rule rather than produce a magnitude range is not fixed: it varies from one judgement to the next around an inferred average, with an inferred spread. So on some judgements a set that fits a rule is read as a range and the person endorses non-members inside the examples' span, and on others the same set is read as a rule. This blurs the commitment to rule or range most where the two are close competitors, such as clustered examples that share only a broad rule like "odd", and least where one explanation wins decisively.
- **tightness_cued_min_max_range**: Refinement of `variable_min_max_bounded_range`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, uncertain stray rate, strays sometimes familiar numbers), the range from the smallest to the largest example comes to mind far more readily than any other range, and their expectation of a rule rather than a range varies from one judgement to the next. The one change, taken from `cluster_cued_range_salience`, is that the average of that expectation depends on how the examples look: the more tightly the examples cluster on the number line, the more people expect a "numbers from the lowest to the highest example" program instead of a rule. So clustered examples that share a rule (37, 57 or 4, 16, 12) lead people to fill in the rule's non-members between the examples (but not beyond them) more often than the same rule's examples spread widely across 1-100.
- **min_max_bounded_range**: People take the smallest and largest example shown as the limits of a possible program, so "numbers from the lowest to the highest example" comes to mind as a magnitude explanation much more readily than any other range. Otherwise they learn the concept in the usual Bayesian way: equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a range, and magnitude ranges, the size principle, an uncertain stray rate, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers. So when examples that share a rule sit close together (37, 57 or 4, 16, 12), people often fill in the rule's non-members between them (44, 7, 13) but do not stretch that magnitude reading past the outermost examples, whereas the min-to-max range of a widely spread set is too large to compete with the rule.
- **tightness_cued_rule_range_expectation**: Refinement of `variable_rule_range_expectation`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, strays sometimes familiar numbers), and their expectation that the program follows a rule rather than produces a magnitude range still varies from one judgement to the next. The one change, taken from `cluster_cued_range_salience`, is that the average of that expectation depends on how the examples look: the more tightly the examples cluster on the number line, the more people expect a "numbers around here" program instead of a rule. So when examples that share a rule sit close together (31, 3, 1, 15 or 37, 57), people fill in the rule's non-members inside the examples' span more often than for the same rule spread widely across 1-100.
- **previous_set_concept_intrusion**: People judge each target by Bayesian concept learning over the examples in front of them (equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, magnitude ranges of 1-100 with the lowest-to-highest-example range especially salient, the size principle, an uncertain stray rate with sometimes-familiar strays, and a rule-versus-range expectation that varies across judgements), but because different example sets are interleaved in a session, the concept from the most recent other example set they judged sometimes intrudes (proactive interference): on a small, stated share of judgements (about 1%) they check the target against the previous set's concept instead of the current one. So the same (set, target) question is answered differently depending on which set preceded it, with stray "yes" answers to targets that fit the preceding set's concept but not the current one.
