# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## variable_rule_range_expectation  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3324.3

Refinement of `range_limited_rule_familiar_strays`. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules, rules held within a magnitude range, and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers. The one change is that how strongly people expect the program to follow a mathematical rule rather than produce a magnitude range is not fixed: it varies from one judgement to the next around an inferred average, with an inferred spread. So on some judgements a set that fits a rule is read as a range and the person endorses non-members inside the examples' span, and on others the same set is read as a rule. This blurs the commitment to rule or range most where the two are close competitors, such as clustered examples that share only a broad rule like "odd", and least where one explanation wins decisively.

## rule_count_familiar_strays  — rank 7, 1.6 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3325.9

## ratio_scale_exemplar_retrieval  — no comparison row

People hold familiar number concepts (equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100) as stored lists of member numbers. They judge how alike two numbers are by their ratio rather than their difference, as on a logarithmic mental number line: 8 and 10 are as similar as 80 and 100, so similarity falls off steeply among small numbers and gently among large ones, at an inferred rate. A concept is retrieved to the extent that each example resembles its members on this ratio scale (allowing an uncertain rate of stray examples, sometimes familiar numbers), and a target is endorsed by its ratio-similarity to the nearest member of the retrieved concepts. So near-misses next to large examples (e.g. 62 or 66 after 64) are partly endorsed, but near-misses next to small ones (3 or 5 after 4) are not.

## thurstonian_decision_noise  — no comparison row

People learn the hidden concept by the simplest Bayesian concept learning (equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges, the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, strays sometimes familiar numbers), but they do not read out their belief exactly when answering: on every judgement their felt confidence that the target belongs fluctuates randomly around that belief, and they say "yes" when the fluctuating confidence favours membership. This decision noise pulls near-certain judgements slightly towards even odds in proportion to how extreme they are (unlike a lapse's constant floor), while leaving uncertain judgements almost unchanged.
