# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## range_limited_rule_familiar_strays  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3325.3

Refinement of `rule_count_familiar_strays`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers (familiarity = how many rules a number belongs to). The one change is the concept space: people also consider that the program applies a rule only within a magnitude range (e.g. "odd numbers between 9 and 33", "multiples of 4 from 52 to 88"), with an inferred share of their belief in rules going to such range-limited rules. So when the examples fit a rule and sit in part of the number line, people endorse the rule's members inside the examples' span more than its members far outside it, instead of treating every member of the rule alike.

## range_restricted_familiar_strays  — rank 1, 0.4 ± 0.6 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3325.7

Refinement of `rule_count_familiar_strays`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes familiar numbers (judged by how many rules a number belongs to). The one change is that people can also think the program uses a rule only over a stretch of the number line, a run of the rule's consecutive members such as "odd numbers from 5 to 89" or "multiples of 7 from 14 to 42", with how much prior belief these restricted rules get inferred. Because a short run is a small concept, when the examples fit a rule and cluster in magnitude people endorse the rule's members inside the examples' range more than its members far outside it, and they reject non-members inside that range more firmly than a magnitude range alone would.

## rule_count_familiar_strays  — rank 2, 0.6 ± 1.5 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3325.9

Refinement of `familiar_strays_equal_rule_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes "familiar" numbers rather than random ones. The one change is what makes a number familiar. It is not how readily a random member of a random rule lands on it, which favours members of tiny rules such as 27, 64 and 81. It is how many well-known rules the number belongs to, its breadth of mathematical associations. So richly connected numbers (12, 24, 36, 60, 100) are the easiest examples to write off as strays, while a lone member of a tiny rule is no more excusable than other numbers with as many associations. This adds no free parameter.

## familiar_strays_equal_rule_prior  — rank 3, 0.8 ± 1.7 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3326.2

Refinement of `familiar_strays_family_level_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes "familiar" numbers (a member of some well-known rule) rather than random ones. The one change is the rule prior: people have no preference among kinds of rule at all — parity, primes, squares, cubes, powers, multiples, final digit and contained digit rules are all equally plausible a priori — so which rule they adopt is decided only by how many examples it explains and how small it is (the size principle), and the rules they would generate a familiar stray from are the same equally weighted rules.

## two_source_program_concepts  — rank 4, 1.0 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3326.4

People think the hidden program may run two sources at once: a main concept (a mathematical rule or a magnitude range of 1-100, learned with the size principle and an uncertain belief about how many examples come from it) and a secondary source that produced the off-pattern examples, which is either random glitches or a second familiar rule. Examples that break the main concept are therefore not just written off: if they fit a small familiar rule (e.g. 64 among 13, 23, 43), people infer the program also uses that rule and say "yes" to its other members (32, 8), whereas off-pattern examples that fit no small rule are treated as glitches that license no further generalisation.

## two_source_familiar_glitches  — rank 5, 1.2 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3326.6

Refinement of `two_source_program_concepts`: people think the hidden program may run two sources at once, a main concept (a mathematical rule or a magnitude range of 1-100, learned with the size principle and an uncertain belief about how many examples come from it) and a secondary source behind the off-pattern examples, which is either a second familiar rule they generalise from or glitches that license no generalisation. The one change, taken from `rule_count_familiar_strays`, is what a glitch looks like: not any number with equal chance, but sometimes (at an inferred rate) a "familiar" number, in proportion to how many well-known rules it belongs to. So richly connected off-pattern numbers (12, 24, 36, 60, 100) are cheaply written off as glitches without endorsing any rule they fit, while an unremarkable off-pattern number that fits a small rule is better explained as that second rule's output, whose other members people then accept.

## bounded_arithmetic_progressions  — rank 6, 1.5 ± 1.5 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3326.9

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, strays sometimes familiar numbers), but they also consider bounded arithmetic progressions as possible programs, "every d-th number from a to b" (e.g. 20, 40, 60, 80 or 61, 64, 67), which together take a small stated share of their prior belief (about 5%), split equally among all such progressions. Because a progression stops at its ends, people should fill in the regular gaps between the examples (60 after 20, 40, 80) more readily than extend the pattern beyond the examples' range (100), and say yes to evenly spaced continuations that no familiar rule contains (47, 59, 71 after 23, 35, 83, 95).

## concept_gated_exemplar_similarity  — rank 7, 3.1 ± 1.8 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3328.5

People judge a target by comparing it with the stored examples themselves. A target counts as similar to an example only to the extent that both fall under a common number concept: a mathematical rule or magnitude range, learned with the size principle and an uncertain belief that some examples are strays, which are sometimes familiar numbers. Within that, similarity falls off smoothly with the target's distance on the number line from the nearest example, at an inferred rate. So members of the inferred rule that lie close to the examples (inside their range) are endorsed more readily than equally valid members far away from every example.

## attention_lapse_guessing  — rank 8, 3.4 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3328.8

People infer the hidden concept by ordinary Bayesian concept learning (equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, strays sometimes being familiar numbers), and they answer with their belief. But on a small, fixed share of trials, about 2% as is typical of attention lapses in long sessions, they do not consult that belief at all and give a guess with its own yes/no bias. So even targets the concept certainly contains get an occasional "no", and targets it certainly excludes get an occasional "yes", at the same floor rate whatever the examples are.

## graded_exemplar_equal_rule_prior  — rank 9, 3.7 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3329.0

Refinement of `graded_exemplar_familiar_strays`: people hold familiar number concepts (mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100) as stored member lists, retrieve a concept to the extent that the examples resemble its members on the number line, allow for an uncertain rate of stray examples that are sometimes familiar numbers, and endorse a target by its closeness to the nearest stored member of the retrieved concepts. The one change, taken from `familiar_strays_equal_rule_prior`, is the rule prior: people have no preference among kinds of rule at all (parity, primes, squares, cubes, powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit are equally plausible a priori), so which rule is retrieved is decided only by how closely and how tightly its members match the examples.

## prior_matched_familiar_strays  — rank 10, 4.2 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3329.5

Refinement of `familiar_strays_equal_rule_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over equally plausible mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100, with the size principle, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray, and strays that are sometimes "familiar" numbers (a member of some well-known rule). The one change is where their idea of a stray comes from. A stray is simply the output of some other program drawn from their own prior over programs: with the same probability they give a rule-based program a priori, it is a member of a randomly chosen rule, and otherwise it is any number at all. So how often strays are familiar numbers is not a separate free belief. It is fixed by how strongly people expect rule-based programs to begin with, and someone who favours rules more also writes off familiar-looking outliers more readily.
