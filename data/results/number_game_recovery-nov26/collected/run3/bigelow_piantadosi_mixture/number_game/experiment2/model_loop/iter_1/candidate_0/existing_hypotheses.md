# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## first_example_anchored_reliability  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2054.1

People do Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, a prior over rules set by kind of rule, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray draw from 1-100), but they treat the first example differently from the rest: the first number shown anchors the concept and is trusted to come from it at its own, inferred reliability, while only the later examples are pooled into the uncertain judgement of how reliable the program is on that set. So a stray-looking number shown first constrains the concept far more (or less) than the same number shown later, and the same set in a different order generalises differently.

## uncertain_reliability_noise_concepts  — rank 4, 45.0 ± 10.6 nats behind the best (4.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2099.1

Refinement of `inferred_example_noise_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules (each with its own inferred prior weight) and all intervals of 1-100 with the size principle, allowing that some examples are stray draws from 1-100 — but they do not know the program's reliability, so instead of assuming one fixed stray rate they hold a belief over how reliable the program is and average over it for each example set. ## compressed_number_line_ranges  — rank 6, 48.1 ± 10.7 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2102.1

People do Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, a prior weight per rule, an uncertain belief in how often an example is a stray draw), but they represent magnitude ranges on a compressed mental number line, on which small numbers take up more room than large ones, and they treat a range's examples as spread evenly along that line, not evenly over the integers. So ranges among small numbers feel mentally wide and give weak evidence, which makes generalisation broad, while equally wide ranges among large numbers feel narrow and tighten quickly. Within a range, its lower members are more typical than its upper ones, so people extend a range further upward than downward. How compressed the line is gets inferred, from logarithmic to linear.

## kind_specific_reliability_concepts  — rank 8, 69.9 ± 12.0 nats behind the best (5.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2124.0

People do Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and magnitude ranges of 1-100 (size principle, a family-organised prior over rules, an uncertain belief about how often an example is a stray draw from 1-100). But their belief about how reliable the program is depends on what kind of program they suppose it is: they expect a program running a mathematical rule to produce strays at a different rate from one producing numbers in a magnitude range, rather than holding one reliability belief whichever kind of concept is true. So a single off-pattern example counts differently against a rule than against a range, which shifts how readily people give up a tight rule for a broad range (or the reverse) when a set contains an outlier.

## complement_stray_reliability_concepts  — rank 9, 80.5 ± 13.1 nats behind the best (6.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2134.6

Refinement of `uncertain_reliability_noise_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules (each with its own inferred prior weight) and all intervals of 1-100 with the size principle, and they are just as unsure how reliable the program is on each set — but they think of a stray example as a mistake, a number that does *not* belong to the concept, rather than a draw that could land anywhere in 1-100. The one change is this stray distribution, drawn from the concept's complement: an example that a broad concept (e.g. "even", "contains a 3") misses is better explained as a stray than under a uniform stray generator, whereas a tight concept gains nothing. So people keep broad rules that miss one example more readily, and examples that a concept already contains are always taken as genuine evidence for it.

## familiar_number_strays  — rank 10, 125.2 ± 18.3 nats behind the best (6.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2179.3

Refinement of `family_pooled_uncertain_reliability`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules (with the family-structured, partially pooled rule prior) and all intervals of 1-100, with the size principle and an uncertain belief about how reliable the program is on each set. The one change is what people think a stray example is: some strays are random numbers from 1-100, but a share of them (how large is inferred) are "familiar" numbers, i.e. a member of some well-known rule picked at random, so how likely a number is to turn up as a stray depends on how many small, familiar rules produce it, whichever rules the learner currently favours. Thus examples that many small rules produce (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 64, 81, 100) are easily written off as strays and constrain the concept less, while unremarkable examples that few rules produce (e.g. 58, 87, 94) are hard to dismiss and the concept has to cover them.

## strong_weak_sampling_mixture  — rank 11, 135.1 ± 18.0 nats behind the best (7.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2189.1

Refinement of `family_pooled_uncertain_reliability`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules (plausibility organised by rule family) and all intervals of 1-100, with an uncertain belief in how often examples are stray draws. The one change is the sampling assumption: people are unsure whether the examples were sampled at random from the concept (so a small concept that fits gains strongly, the size principle) or were just numbers the program happened to produce that are consistent with it (so a concept's size carries no evidence), and they weigh both possibilities, with how much they trust random sampling inferred. This predicts broader, less size-sensitive generalisation than the incumbent, except where the examples fit a tight concept so well that the random-sampling explanation dominates.

## weak_sampling_concepts  — rank 12, 205.2 ± 22.7 nats behind the best (9.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2259.2

People treat the examples only as numbers the program is allowed to produce, not as random samples drawn from its concept (weak sampling): a candidate rule or magnitude range gains plausibility merely by containing the examples (and loses it for each example it misses, which they may write off as a stray), but not for being small, so a concept's plausibility rests on its prior — one plausibility per kind of rule (parity, primes, squares and cubes, powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit) shared by the rules of that kind, and a preference for narrower ranges — and does not tighten as more consistent examples arrive. This predicts that adding examples consistent with both a broad and a tight concept (e.g. 16, 8, 64 under "even" and "powers of 2") leaves generalisation about as broad as after the first example, unlike the size principle.
