# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## kind_uncertainty_shifted_criterion  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3663.5

Refinement of `uncertainty_shifted_criterion`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as its Bayesian concept learner does and shift their yes/no criterion with how unsure they are, but the one change is what that uncertainty is about. It is not their spread of belief over individual concepts (which rule, which range width) but their uncertainty about what kind of program they face — a parity rule, a prime rule, a power rule, a multiples rule, a digit rule, or a magnitude range — so examples that leave it open whether the program follows an arithmetic rule or a stretch of the number line move the answer toward "no" (or "yes"), while examples that clearly point to one kind do not, however many concepts of that kind remain open.

## uncertainty_shifted_criterion  — rank 1, 0.1 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (0.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3663.6

People infer the hidden concept exactly as the best model's Bayesian concept learner does, but their yes/no decision criterion depends on how sure they are which concept the program uses: the more their belief is spread over many competing concepts (rules and range widths), the more they shift their answer away from the inferred membership probability toward "no" (uncertainty aversion) or, if the inferred shift is negative, toward "yes". So two targets with the same membership probability get different answers depending on whether the examples point clearly to one concept or leave many open.

## beta_share_width_exception_concepts  — rank 2, 0.5 ± 1.1 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3663.9

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges, with the size principle. The one change is the shape of their prior about how wide a range is: they think of a range as a portion of the bounded 1–100 line and expect it to cover a typical share of it (a beta prior over that share, with its mean and sharpness inferred), rather than holding an unbounded gamma expectation over raw width. So ranges covering nearly the whole line are as implausible to them as very narrow ones, and when the examples are spread widely (e.g. 43, 4, 91, 9) people fall back on a rule instead of an "almost any number" range. As a result they say "yes" less often to numbers that only such a near-total range would include.

## rule_vs_range_doubt_criterion  — rank 3, 0.6 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.1

Refinement of `kind_uncertainty_shifted_criterion`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as its Bayesian concept learner does and shift their yes/no criterion with how unsure they are about what kind of program they face. The one change is how coarse that kind judgment is: the only question that moves their criterion is whether the program follows an arithmetic rule at all or picks out a stretch of the number line, so examples that leave rule-versus-range open shift answers toward "no" (or "yes"), while doubt among kinds of rule (say multiples versus final digit) does not shift them, unlike in the incumbent.

## mixed_sampling_uncertainty_criterion  — rank 4, 0.7 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.2

Refinement of `uncertainty_shifted_criterion` with one component taken from `mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts`: people infer the hidden concept by the same Bayesian concept learning (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, occasional stray examples) and shift their yes/no criterion toward "no" (or "yes") the more their belief is spread over competing concepts. The one change is their sampling assumption: they take only an inferred share of the examples as random draws from the concept (the size principle) and the rest as mere members of it, so broad rules and wide ranges stay in play after examples that also fit a narrower concept — which both broadens generalisation to the broad concept's other members and raises the uncertainty that drives the criterion shift for exactly those sets.

## specificity_biased_rule_prior  — rank 5, 1.0 ± 1.2 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.5

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but within each kind of rule they do not find all rules equally plausible beforehand: a rule's prior plausibility scales with how specific it is (how few numbers it contains), to an inferred degree, so "multiples of 9" is a priori favoured over "multiples of 3" and "multiples of 8" over "multiples of 4" (or disfavoured, if the degree is negative). The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives in example sets that fit a sparse rule nested inside a broader rule of the same kind, especially with only two examples (e.g. 81, 9; 8, 32; 6, 66): there this model confines "yes" to multiples of the larger base and rejects the other multiples of its divisor far more firmly than the size principle alone does.

## range_misperception_uncertainty_criterion  — rank 6, 1.1 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.5

Refinement of `range_size_misperception_concepts`, with one component taken from `uncertainty_shifted_criterion`. People do Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, and they misjudge how many numbers a range holds (its felt size grows as an inferred power of its true width), so wide ranges are penalised too little or too much by the size principle. The one change is the yes/no criterion: the more their belief is spread over competing concepts, the further people shift away from the inferred membership probability, toward "no" (uncertainty aversion) or, if the inferred shift is negative, toward "yes". Because a compressed sense of range size keeps many range widths open for three- and four-example sets, this shift is strongest for exactly the sets where magnitude generalisation already reaches beyond the examples' span.

## mixed_sampling_kind_uncertainty  — rank 7, 1.1 ± 0.8 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.6

Refinement of `kind_uncertainty_shifted_criterion` with one component taken from `mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts`: people infer the hidden concept by the same Bayesian concept learning (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, occasional stray examples) and shift their yes/no criterion with how unsure they are what kind of program they face (which family of rule, or a magnitude range). The one change is their sampling assumption: they take only an inferred share of the examples as random draws from the concept (the size principle) and the rest as mere members of it, so broad rules and wide ranges of other kinds stay in play after examples that also fit a narrow concept — broadening generalisation to the broad concept's members and raising exactly the kind-level doubt that moves the criterion for those sets.

## person_specific_response_bias  — rank 8, 1.1 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.6

People infer how likely the target is to belong to the hidden concept as the simplest Bayesian concept learner does (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but each person brings a stable bias toward answering "yes" or "no" that holds across their whole session: their decision criterion is shifted, by a person-specific amount drawn from a population distribution, so the log-odds of saying "yes" is the inferred membership log-odds plus that person's own bias. So, for the same examples and target, a yes-prone person accepts more targets of every kind (including clear non-members), and a no-prone person rejects more (including shown examples), rather than everyone answering with the same probability.

## two_cluster_kind_doubt_criterion  — rank 9, 1.2 ± 0.9 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.6

Refinement of `two_cluster_range_concepts`, with one component taken from the incumbent `kind_uncertainty_shifted_criterion`. People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind), single magnitude ranges, and "two separate groups" concepts (two ranges with a gap between them), all judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line and with the size principle. The one change is the yes/no criterion: the more the examples leave open what kind of program it is, including whether the numbers form one stretch of the line, two separate groups or an arithmetic rule, the further people shift their answer from the inferred membership probability toward "no" (or toward "yes" if the inferred shift is negative). So widely split sets such as 13, 91, which leave one range, two clusters and a rule all in play, get a shifted criterion that the single-range incumbent cannot produce.

## single_kind_commitment_inference  — rank 10, 1.3 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.8

People reason about the hidden program in two steps, as in single-category inference (Murphy & Ross): they first work out what kind of program it is — a parity rule, a prime rule, a square/cube rule, a power rule, a multiples rule, a final-digit rule, a contained-digit rule, an exception rule, or a magnitude range — and then judge the target mostly within the kind they favour, rather than weighing every kind by its full posterior plausibility. So when the examples leave two kinds open (e.g. "multiples of 4" versus "numbers between 20 and 60"), the less favoured kind is discounted to an inferred degree, and targets that only it would contain get fewer "yes" answers than a fully Bayesian learner predicts (or more, if people hedge across kinds more evenly than the posterior warrants).

## two_cluster_range_concepts  — rank 11, 1.4 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.8

People do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle, but their magnitude concepts are not limited to one unbroken stretch of the line: when the examples fall into two separated groups, they can take the program to produce "small numbers and large numbers" — two separate ranges with a gap between them — as a kind of concept with its own inferred plausibility. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lies in targets in the empty middle of widely split sets (e.g. 50 after 13, 91 or 43, 4, 91, 9; 60 after 20, 32, 92): this model says "no" to them while still saying "yes" to numbers near either group, whereas a single-range learner must either cover the gap or fall back on a rule.

## range_size_misperception_concepts  — rank 12, 1.4 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3664.9

People do Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle, but they misjudge how many numbers a range holds: a range's felt size grows as an inferred power of its true width, so (if the power is below one) wide ranges seem to hold fewer numbers than they do and are penalised too little by the size principle — or, above one, too much. Because this distortion acts on the evidence each example gives rather than on the prior, it shows up most with more examples: with a compressed sense of range size, adding examples inside a span narrows magnitude generalisation less than the normative learner predicts, so "yes" extends further beyond the examples' span for three- and four-example sets.

## mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts  — rank 13, 1.5 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3665.0

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with occasional stray examples. The one change is their assumption about how the examples were generated: they believe only some genuine examples (an inferred share) were drawn at random from the concept, which is what drives the size principle, while the rest were merely numbers the concept happens to contain, chosen without regard to how big the concept is (mixed strong/weak sampling). So the size principle is diluted most for big concepts, and after examples that fit both a narrow and a broad concept (multiples of 9 vs multiples of 3, a tight range vs a wide one) people keep the broad concept in play and say "yes" to its other members more than pure random sampling predicts.

## mixed_sampling_prior_leaking_judgment  — rank 14, 2.6 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3666.1

Refinement of `mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts` with one component taken from `beta_share_width_prior_leaking_judgment`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as the incumbent's learner does (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, occasional stray examples, and only an inferred share of examples taken as random draws that drive the size principle). The one change is in the yes/no judgment: it is pulled by how plausible the target was before any examples were shown, so the log-odds of saying "yes" is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership. A priori typical numbers (mid-range numbers that most moderately wide ranges contain, members of many rules) therefore get more "yes" than the examples warrant, or less if the share is negative, on top of the broad-concept generalisation that mixed sampling produces.

## sample_size_shifted_criterion  — rank 15, 2.8 ± 1.7 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3666.2

People infer the hidden concept as a Bayesian concept learner does (rules organised by kind with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but how readily they act on that inference depends on how much evidence they were shown: their yes/no criterion shifts by an inferred amount with each additional example (a sample-size heuristic). So for the same membership probability, people answer "yes" differently after one example than after four, regardless of how sure the examples make them about which concept, or which kind of concept, the program uses.

## boundary_examples_anchor_ranges  — rank 16, 3.5 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3667.0

When people consider whether the examples come from a magnitude range, they judge the range mainly by the smallest and largest examples, which mark its edges. The examples in between tell them nothing new about where the edges are, so they count for less (to an inferred degree) as evidence that the range is narrow, while every example counts fully when they test an arithmetic rule. As a result, with three or four examples people keep entertaining wider ranges, and a range explanation stays more competitive with a rule, than an ideal learner who weighs every example equally would.

## digit_parity_concepts  — rank 17, 5.6 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3669.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle, but they also read numbers by their written digits and hold a kind of concept the current models lack: "numbers written only with odd digits" (1, 3, 5, ..., 11, 13, ..., 99) and "numbers written only with even digits" (2, 4, 6, 8, 20, 22, ..., 88), with this kind's plausibility inferred. So after examples such as 31, 3, 1, 15 or 9, 33, 73, 99 people say "yes" to odd numbers like 71, 77 or 95 but "no" to odd numbers with an even tens digit such as 63 or 85, rather than spreading "yes" evenly over all odd numbers.

## individual_rule_vs_range_tendency  — rank 18, 323.9 ± 34.1 nats behind the best (9.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3987.3

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle. The one change is that people differ, stably across their session, in how readily they take the hidden program to follow an arithmetic rule rather than a magnitude range: each person has their own prior rule-versus-range odds, drawn from a population distribution whose mean and spread are inferred. For the same examples, a "rule-minded" person confines "yes" to members of the fitting rule while a "magnitude-minded" person spreads "yes" over nearby numbers, so their answers to the same (set, target) pair disagree systematically rather than at random.

## conjunctive_shared_feature_exemplars  — rank 19, 901.4 ± 44.3 nats behind the best (20.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4564.9

People do not infer a hidden concept; they compare the target with each stored example using a shared-feature (additive-clustering) similarity: two numbers are similar to the extent that they share familiar number properties (parity, multiples, powers, squares, primes, final digit, digits contained), each property weighted by how salient its kind is and how distinctive it is (properties held by few numbers count more), plus their closeness in magnitude. The judgment is conjunctive: the target is accepted to the extent that it is similar to every example (the geometric mean of its normalised similarities to the examples), so a target must resemble all the examples, not just one, to be called "yes".
