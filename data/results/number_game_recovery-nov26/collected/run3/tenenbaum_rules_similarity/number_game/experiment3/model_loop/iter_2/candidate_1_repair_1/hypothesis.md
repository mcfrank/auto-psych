People do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, using the size principle, but they neglect base rates: when weighing the concepts against each other they give the prior plausibility of each concept less weight than the examples' evidence (the prior counts only to an inferred fraction of its full weight, as in partial base-rate neglect). So concepts that are a priori unlikely — one rule among many of its kind, an unusually narrow or wide range, a rare kind of rule — are discounted less than an ideal learner would discount them, and once examples fit such a concept, people generalise from it more readily than the normative learner does.
