# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2283.9

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with occasional stray examples. The one change is their assumption about how the examples were generated: they believe only some genuine examples (an inferred share) were drawn at random from the concept, which is what drives the size principle, while the rest were merely numbers the concept happens to contain, chosen without regard to how big the concept is (mixed strong/weak sampling). So the size principle is diluted most for big concepts, and after examples that fit both a narrow and a broad concept (multiples of 9 vs multiples of 3, a tight range vs a wide one) people keep the broad concept in play and say "yes" to its other members more than pure random sampling predicts.

## range_size_misperception_concepts  — rank 1, 0.9 ± 1.5 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2284.9

People do Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle, but they misjudge how many numbers a range holds: a range's felt size grows as an inferred power of its true width, so (if the power is below one) wide ranges seem to hold fewer numbers than they do and are penalised too little by the size principle — or, above one, too much. Because this distortion acts on the evidence each example gives rather than on the prior, it shows up most with more examples: with a compressed sense of range size, adding examples inside a span narrows magnitude generalisation less than the normative learner predicts, so "yes" extends further beyond the examples' span for three- and four-example sets.

## beta_share_width_exception_concepts  — rank 2, 1.2 ± 1.0 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2285.2

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges, with the size principle. The one change is the shape of their prior about how wide a range is: they think of a range as a portion of the bounded 1–100 line and expect it to cover a typical share of it (a beta prior over that share, with its mean and sharpness inferred), rather than holding an unbounded gamma expectation over raw width. So ranges covering nearly the whole line are as implausible to them as very narrow ones, and when the examples are spread widely (e.g. 43, 4, 91, 9) people fall back on a rule instead of an "almost any number" range. As a result they say "yes" less often to numbers that only such a near-total range would include.

## specificity_biased_rule_prior  — rank 3, 1.6 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2285.6

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but within each kind of rule they do not find all rules equally plausible beforehand: a rule's prior plausibility scales with how specific it is (how few numbers it contains), to an inferred degree, so "multiples of 9" is a priori favoured over "multiples of 3" and "multiples of 8" over "multiples of 4" (or disfavoured, if the degree is negative). The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives in example sets that fit a sparse rule nested inside a broader rule of the same kind, especially with only two examples (e.g. 81, 9; 8, 32; 6, 66): there this model confines "yes" to multiples of the larger base and rejects the other multiples of its divisor far more firmly than the size principle alone does.

## uncertainty_shifted_criterion  — rank 4, 1.8 ± 1.5 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2285.8

People infer the hidden concept exactly as the best model's Bayesian concept learner does, but their yes/no decision criterion depends on how sure they are which concept the program uses: the more their belief is spread over many competing concepts (rules and range widths), the more they shift their answer away from the inferred membership probability toward "no" (uncertainty aversion) or, if the inferred shift is negative, toward "yes". So two targets with the same membership probability get different answers depending on whether the examples point clearly to one concept or leave many open.

## boundary_examples_anchor_ranges  — rank 5, 2.1 ± 1.7 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2286.0

When people consider whether the examples come from a magnitude range, they judge the range mainly by the smallest and largest examples, which mark its edges. The examples in between tell them nothing new about where the edges are, so they count for less (to an inferred degree) as evidence that the range is narrow, while every example counts fully when they test an arithmetic rule. As a result, with three or four examples people keep entertaining wider ranges, and a range explanation stays more competitive with a rule, than an ideal learner who weighs every example equally would.

## individual_rule_vs_range_tendency  — rank 6, 2.3 ± 1.1 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2286.2

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle. The one change is that people differ, stably across their session, in how readily they take the hidden program to follow an arithmetic rule rather than a magnitude range: each person has their own prior rule-versus-range odds, drawn from a population distribution whose mean and spread are inferred. For the same examples, a "rule-minded" person confines "yes" to members of the fitting rule while a "magnitude-minded" person spreads "yes" over nearby numbers, so their answers to the same (set, target) pair disagree systematically rather than at random.

## beta_share_width_prior_leaking_judgment  — rank 7, 2.7 ± 1.8 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2286.6

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts` with one component taken from `prior_leaking_membership_judgment`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as the incumbent's Bayesian learner does (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is pulled by how plausible the target was before any examples were shown. ## range_placement_prior_concepts  — rank 9, 3.7 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2287.6

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges, with the size principle and the same expectation that a range covers a typical share of the 1–100 line. The one change is that people also expect ranges to sit in a typical place on the line: instead of every placement of a range of a given width being equally plausible, they hold a prior (inferred mean position and sharpness) over where a range's centre lies, so ranges among, say, small numbers can be a priori more plausible than equally wide ranges among large numbers. As a result, examples falling in a disfavoured region of the line are explained by a rule more readily, and "yes" to nearby numbers there is lower, than for the same pattern of examples in a favoured region.

## prior_leaking_membership_judgment  — rank 10, 5.1 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2289.0

People infer the hidden concept as a normative Bayesian concept learner does (rules with a family-organised prior and arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges with a preferred width, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is distorted by how plausible the target was before they saw any examples: the log-odds of saying yes is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership. ## dominated_rule_discount_concepts  — rank 11, 5.1 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2289.1

## hundred_chart_block_concepts  — rank 15, 7.7 ± 3.9 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2291.6

People picture the numbers 1–100 laid out on the familiar hundred chart (tens digit as the row, units digit as the column), and besides arithmetic rules and ranges on the number line they entertain rectangular blocks of that chart as concepts, such as "numbers in the 20s to 80s that end in 2 to 6", with this kind's plausibility inferred and its size judged as a share of the chart. With the size principle, examples that cluster in a few columns across several rows (e.g. 22, 62, 26, 82) lead people to say "yes" to other numbers in that block (42, 66) and "no" to numbers outside its columns (28, 63), instead of spreading "yes" over all even numbers or one wide range.

## first_example_trust_concepts  — rank 16, 8.1 ± 3.7 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2292.0

People treat the first example they see differently from the later ones when judging how far each example is a genuine output of the hidden concept: the first example sets the concepts they entertain and is trusted to belong to the concept at its own inferred rate. Each later example is trusted at the usual fixed rate, and one that does not fit can be dismissed as a stray output. Apart from this, they generalise by the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (size principle, rule prior organised by kind with exception rules as their own kind, gamma prior over range width). So if the first example is trusted more, an odd-one-out example is discounted when it comes late in the set (8, 24, 48, 63 still suggests multiples of 8) but forces a broader concept when it comes first.

## exemplar_concept_similarity_pooling  — rank 30, 810.7 ± 41.1 nats behind the best (19.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3094.6

People do not infer one concept that explains all the examples together; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its average similarity to the stored exemplars, where the similarity of two numbers is how readily a familiar number concept (a rule such as "multiples of 9", or a magnitude range of typical width) suggested by the exemplar would also contain the target, with more specific shared concepts counting for more. So a target strongly similar to just one example (e.g. a number close to only one of several widely spread examples, or an odd number after 3, 9, 27, 81) still gets a fair share of "yes" answers, and adding consistent examples narrows generalisation much less than a learner who requires one concept to cover them all.

## generative_next_output_likelihood  — no comparison row

People read "would the program produce this number?" as a question about how likely the program is to output that particular number next, not about whether the number belongs to the hidden concept. They infer the concept as a Bayesian concept learner does (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges covering a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but they say "yes" according to how much more likely the target is as the next output than a number picked at random. So targets that only a broad concept contains (any even number, anywhere in a wide range) get fewer "yes" answers than their membership probability implies, while members of a small, well-fitting concept (powers of 2, a tight range) are accepted readily.

## tversky_prototype_feature_similarity  — no comparison row

People judge the target by its similarity to the prototype of the examples — the set of familiar number properties (parity, being a multiple of some number, being a square, cube or power, being prime, final digit, decade) that all the examples share, plus their place on the number line — using Tversky's ratio-of-features similarity: the salience-weighted properties the target has in common with that prototype, relative to those plus the prototype's properties the target lacks and the target's own properties the prototype lacks, with numerical closeness to the nearest example falling off exponentially and counting as one more weighted property. The more similar the target is to the prototype by this metric, the more likely they say "yes", so a target is rejected both for missing what the examples share and for having salient properties of its own that the examples do not.

## inferred_sampling_regime_concepts  — no comparison row

Refinement of `mixed_strong_weak_sampling_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with occasional stray examples, and they allow both random sampling from the concept (the size principle) and weak sampling (examples merely members of it). The one change is where that uncertainty sits: rather than each example independently being a random draw or not, people are unsure whether the program as a whole draws its outputs at random from its concept, and they infer this from the whole set together with the concept. So a suspicious coincidence (every example fits a small rule, e.g. 2, 8, 16, 64) is itself taken as evidence of random sampling and sharpens generalisation onto the small rule, while sets that no small concept explains well tilt belief towards weak sampling and keep broad rules and wide ranges in play.
