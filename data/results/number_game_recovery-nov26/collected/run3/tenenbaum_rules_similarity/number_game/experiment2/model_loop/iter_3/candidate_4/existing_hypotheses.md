# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## beta_share_width_exception_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2285.2

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges, with the size principle. The one change is the shape of their prior about how wide a range is: they think of a range as a portion of the bounded 1–100 line and expect it to cover a typical share of it (a beta prior over that share, with its mean and sharpness inferred), rather than holding an unbounded gamma expectation over raw width. So ranges covering nearly the whole line are as implausible to them as very narrow ones, and when the examples are spread widely (e.g. 43, 4, 91, 9) people fall back on a rule instead of an "almost any number" range. As a result they say "yes" less often to numbers that only such a near-total range would include.

## specificity_biased_rule_prior  — rank 1, 0.4 ± 0.8 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2285.6

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but within each kind of rule they do not find all rules equally plausible beforehand: a rule's prior plausibility scales with how specific it is (how few numbers it contains), to an inferred degree, so "multiples of 9" is a priori favoured over "multiples of 3" and "multiples of 8" over "multiples of 4" (or disfavoured, if the degree is negative). The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives in example sets that fit a sparse rule nested inside a broader rule of the same kind, especially with only two examples (e.g. 81, 9; 8, 32; 6, 66): there this model confines "yes" to multiples of the larger base and rejects the other multiples of its divisor far more firmly than the size principle alone does.

## uncertainty_shifted_criterion  — rank 2, 0.6 ± 0.8 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2285.8

People infer the hidden concept exactly as the best model's Bayesian concept learner does, but their yes/no decision criterion depends on how sure they are which concept the program uses: the more their belief is spread over many competing concepts (rules and range widths), the more they shift their answer away from the inferred membership probability toward "no" (uncertainty aversion) or, if the inferred shift is negative, toward "yes". So two targets with the same membership probability get different answers depending on whether the examples point clearly to one concept or leave many open.

## beta_share_width_prior_leaking_judgment  — rank 3, 1.5 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2286.6

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts` with one component taken from `prior_leaking_membership_judgment`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as the incumbent's Bayesian learner does (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is pulled by how plausible the target was before any examples were shown. The log-odds of saying yes is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership (under the same share-of-the-line range prior), so a priori typical numbers — mid-range numbers that most moderately wide ranges contain, and numbers belonging to many rules — get more "yes" than the examples alone warrant (or less, if the share is negative).

## beta_share_parity_range_concepts  — rank 4, 1.8 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2286.9

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts` with one component taken from `parity_within_range_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle, but they can also entertain the conjunctive concept "odd (or even) numbers within a range" (e.g. "even numbers above 60") as its own kind of concept, with its own inferred plausibility and the same share-of-the-line width prior as plain ranges. So after 4, 66, 78, 96 people say "yes" to large even numbers (70, 74) but "no" to small ones and to odd numbers between the examples, and after 23, 35, 95, 83 they reject even numbers between the examples, rather than spreading "yes" over every number in the range or over all even (odd) numbers.

## prior_leaking_membership_judgment  — rank 5, 3.8 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2289.0

People infer the hidden concept as a normative Bayesian concept learner does (rules with a family-organised prior and arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges with a preferred width, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is distorted by how plausible the target was before they saw any examples: the log-odds of saying yes is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership. So a priori "typical" numbers (mid-range, even, multiples) get more "yes" than the examples warrant (prior leakage), or, if the share is negative, less (contrast against what was expected anyway).

## dominated_rule_discount_concepts  — rank 6, 3.9 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2289.1

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle. The one change is which rules they seriously entertain given the examples: when every example also fits a strictly narrower rule (all multiples of 9 fit "multiples of 3"; 4, 16, 64 fit both "powers of 4" and "powers of 2"), they settle on the narrower rule and discount the broader, dominated one by an inferred factor beyond what the size principle already does (arbitrary-exception rules never count as the narrower rule). So after 36, 45, 54, 81 people say "yes" to other multiples of 9 and "no" to multiples of 3 that are not multiples of 9 more sharply than the incumbent predicts — unlike a prior favouring specific rules, the discount applies only when a narrower rule actually fits the examples.

## beta_share_without_replacement_concepts  — rank 7, 5.2 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2290.4

Refinement of `without_replacement_sampling_concepts` with one component taken from `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people assume the program never repeats a number, so they treat the examples as drawn from the hidden concept without replacement (each further example is one of the concept's members not yet shown), and they do Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges on that basis. The one change is their prior about how wide a range is: they judge a range as a typical share of the bounded 1–100 line, so near-total "almost any number" ranges are implausible. As a result, when the examples use up a good part of a small rule (e.g. 36, 45, 54, 81 out of the multiples of 9), people favour that most specific rule over the broader one around it (multiples of 3) more strongly than random sampling with replacement allows, saying "yes" to other multiples of 9 and "no" to multiples of 3 that are not multiples of 9.

## inferred_stray_rate_concepts  — rank 8, 5.9 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2291.1

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges with a gamma width preference and the size principle, and they also allow that any example may be a stray number the program produced at random rather than a member of the concept. The one change is that how often they expect such strays is no longer fixed at one example in ten but is a belief inferred from their judgments: the more strays they expect, the more readily they keep concepts that miss an example (and so sometimes reject an example they were shown), and the weaker the size principle becomes.

## gamma_width_exception_family_concepts  — rank 9, 6.0 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2291.2

Refinement of `gamma_width_family_prior_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules and magnitude ranges (size principle, family-organised rule prior, and a gamma prior over range width whose sharpness is inferred along with its typical width). The one change, taken from `arbitrary_exception_rule_prior`, is that rules with an arbitrary exception ("powers of 2 except 32", "powers of 2 plus 37") form their own kind of rule with their own, presumably very low, plausibility, instead of being ordinary power rules. So people expect ranges of a sharply characteristic width and also firmly reject 37 (and accept 32) after seeing powers of 2.

## first_example_trust_concepts  — rank 10, 6.8 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2292.0

People treat the first example they see differently from the later ones when judging how far each example is a genuine output of the hidden concept: the first example sets the concepts they entertain and is trusted to belong to the concept at its own inferred rate. Each later example is trusted at the usual fixed rate, and one that does not fit can be dismissed as a stray output. Apart from this, they generalise by the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (size principle, rule prior organised by kind with exception rules as their own kind, gamma prior over range width). So if the first example is trusted more, an odd-one-out example is discounted when it comes late in the set (8, 24, 48, 63 still suggests multiples of 8) but forces a broader concept when it comes first.

## times_table_familiarity_concepts  — rank 12, 11.1 ± 4.9 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2296.3

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, with the size principle. The one change is how prior belief is shared among the "multiples of m" rules: not equally, but by how familiar each times table is to them, with a separate inferred plausibility for each table that is not tied to how many numbers it contains. So familiar tables (e.g. multiples of 5 or 9) are a readier explanation of examples they fit than unfamiliar (7, 11, 12) or broader (3) tables, and after examples that are all multiples of 9 people confine "yes" to the 9-table more firmly than the size principle alone implies (or, if the 3-table is the more familiar, spread it further).

## without_replacement_sampling_concepts  — rank 13, 13.8 ± 4.3 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2299.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior, arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges of a characteristic width), but they assume the program never repeats a number, so they treat the examples as drawn from the concept without replacement: each further example is one of the concept's members not yet shown. As a result, examples that nearly exhaust a small concept (e.g. 8, 64 or 81, 9) point to that concept, such as cubes or powers of 3, much more strongly than under sampling with replacement, and generalisation narrows faster onto small, nearly exhausted rules.

## gamma_width_doubling_chain_concepts  — rank 14, 18.0 ± 6.8 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2303.2

Refinement of `doubling_chain_concepts`: people do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind), magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts, using the size principle, and they also entertain multiplicative chains — each number double (or triple) the one before, starting from a number other than 1 (e.g. 5, 10, 20, 40, 80) — as a kind of rule with its own plausibility. ## pedagogical_teacher_sampling_concepts  — rank 15, 18.7 ± 6.4 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2303.9

People assume the examples were chosen by a helpful teacher rather than drawn at random from the concept: they reason that a program meaning a given concept would pick examples that point distinctively to that concept (numbers that few other plausible concepts would also produce), and they infer the concept, over the same rules and magnitude ranges as the best model, by asking which concept such a teacher would most likely have chosen these examples for. So when every example also fits a narrower rule (e.g. all multiples of 9), people conclude the teacher meant the narrower rule — a broader one (multiples of 3) would have been shown with examples that are not multiples of 9 — and they reject the broader rule's other members more firmly than random sampling alone implies.

## example_recall_limited_concepts  — rank 16, 19.2 ± 7.2 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2304.4

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts with a preferred width, size principle), but their memory for the examples is limited: each example shown is retained, independently, only with an inferred probability, and they generalise from whichever examples they still hold. So a judgment is sometimes based on only part of the set, which broadens generalisation for longer sets (a forgotten example no longer rules out a broader or different concept) in proportion to how many examples could have been lost.

## gaussian_neighbourhood_magnitude_concepts  — rank 23, 210.3 ± 29.3 nats behind the best (7.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2495.5

People generalise by rules exactly as the best Bayesian concept learner does, but their magnitude-based concepts are not hard-edged ranges: such a concept is a neighbourhood around a central number, membership falls off smoothly with numerical distance from that centre along a Gaussian similarity gradient whose breadth they infer from the examples (narrow neighbourhoods favoured by the size principle), and examples are produced in proportion to that similarity. A target's graded similarity to the inferred neighbourhood is the probability they say "yes", so "yes" fades gradually with distance from the examples' centre instead of being flat inside the examples' range and stopping at an edge.

## exemplar_concept_similarity_pooling  — rank 24, 809.5 ± 41.6 nats behind the best (19.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3094.6

People do not infer one concept that explains all the examples together; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its average similarity to the stored exemplars, where the similarity of two numbers is how readily a familiar number concept (a rule such as "multiples of 9", or a magnitude range of typical width) suggested by the exemplar would also contain the target, with more specific shared concepts counting for more. So a target strongly similar to just one example (e.g. a number close to only one of several widely spread examples, or an odd number after 3, 9, 27, 81) still gets a fair share of "yes" answers, and adding consistent examples narrows generalisation much less than a learner who requires one concept to cover them all.
