# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## beta_share_width_exception_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2285.2

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges, with the size principle. The one change is the shape of their prior about how wide a range is: they think of a range as a portion of the bounded 1–100 line and expect it to cover a typical share of it (a beta prior over that share, with its mean and sharpness inferred), rather than holding an unbounded gamma expectation over raw width. So ranges covering nearly the whole line are as implausible to them as very narrow ones, and when the examples are spread widely (e.g. 43, 4, 91, 9) people fall back on a rule instead of an "almost any number" range. As a result they say "yes" less often to numbers that only such a near-total range would include.

## prior_leaking_membership_judgment  — rank 1, 3.8 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2289.0

People infer the hidden concept as a normative Bayesian concept learner does (rules with a family-organised prior and arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges with a preferred width, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is distorted by how plausible the target was before they saw any examples: the log-odds of saying yes is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership. So a priori "typical" numbers (mid-range, even, multiples) get more "yes" than the examples warrant (prior leakage), or, if the share is negative, less (contrast against what was expected anyway).

## inferred_stray_rate_concepts  — rank 2, 5.9 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2291.1

Refinement of `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges with a gamma width preference and the size principle, and they also allow that any example may be a stray number the program produced at random rather than a member of the concept. The one change is that how often they expect such strays is no longer fixed at one example in ten but is a belief inferred from their judgments: the more strays they expect, the more readily they keep concepts that miss an example (and so sometimes reject an example they were shown), and the weaker the size principle becomes.

## gamma_width_exception_family_concepts  — rank 3, 6.0 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2291.2

Refinement of `gamma_width_family_prior_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules and magnitude ranges (size principle, family-organised rule prior, and a gamma prior over range width whose sharpness is inferred along with its typical width). The one change, taken from `arbitrary_exception_rule_prior`, is that rules with an arbitrary exception ("powers of 2 except 32", "powers of 2 plus 37") form their own kind of rule with their own, presumably very low, plausibility, instead of being ordinary power rules. So people expect ranges of a sharply characteristic width and also firmly reject 37 (and accept 32) after seeing powers of 2.

## first_example_trust_concepts  — rank 4, 6.8 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2292.0

People treat the first example they see differently from the later ones when judging how far each example is a genuine output of the hidden concept: the first example sets the concepts they entertain and is trusted to belong to the concept at its own inferred rate. Each later example is trusted at the usual fixed rate, and one that does not fit can be dismissed as a stray output. Apart from this, they generalise by the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (size principle, rule prior organised by kind with exception rules as their own kind, gamma prior over range width). So if the first example is trusted more, an odd-one-out example is discounted when it comes late in the set (8, 24, 48, 63 still suggests multiples of 8) but forces a broader concept when it comes first.

## gamma_width_parity_range_concepts  — rank 5, 7.6 ± 3.5 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2292.8

Refinement of the incumbent `parity_within_range_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind), magnitude ranges and "odd (or even) numbers within a range", with the size principle. The one change, taken from `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`, is the shape of their expectation about how wide a range (plain or parity-restricted) is: a gamma prior whose sharpness is inferred along with its typical width, instead of the fixed, loose Erlang form — so people may expect ranges of one sharply characteristic width, strongly disfavouring both very narrow and very wide ranges around the examples.

## without_replacement_sampling_concepts  — rank 6, 13.8 ± 4.3 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2299.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior, arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges of a characteristic width), but they assume the program never repeats a number, so they treat the examples as drawn from the concept without replacement: each further example is one of the concept's members not yet shown. As a result, examples that nearly exhaust a small concept (e.g. 8, 64 or 81, 9) point to that concept, such as cubes or powers of 3, much more strongly than under sampling with replacement, and generalisation narrows faster onto small, nearly exhausted rules.

## gamma_width_doubling_chain_concepts  — rank 7, 18.0 ± 6.8 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2303.2

Refinement of `doubling_chain_concepts`: people do Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules (organised by kind, with exception rules as their own kind), magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts, using the size principle, and they also entertain multiplicative chains — each number double (or triple) the one before, starting from a number other than 1 (e.g. 5, 10, 20, 40, 80) — as a kind of rule with its own plausibility. The one change, taken from the best model `gamma_width_exception_family_concepts`, is their expectation about how wide a range is: a gamma prior whose sharpness is inferred along with its typical width, in place of the fixed, loose Erlang form. With ranges judged as the best model judges them, chains are put to a fair test: after a chain-like set such as 5, 10, 20 this model still predicts "yes" to 40 and 80 and "no" to other multiples of 5 such as 15, 25 and 30, where the best model spreads "yes" over all multiples of 5.

## example_recall_limited_concepts  — rank 8, 19.2 ± 7.2 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2304.4

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts with a preferred width, size principle), but their memory for the examples is limited: each example shown is retained, independently, only with an inferred probability, and they generalise from whichever examples they still hold. So a judgment is sometimes based on only part of the set, which broadens generalisation for longer sets (a forgotten example no longer rules out a broader or different concept) in proportion to how many examples could have been lost.

## parity_within_range_concepts  — rank 9, 19.5 ± 7.3 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2304.7

Refinement of the incumbent `arbitrary_exception_rule_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised rule prior, exception rules as their own kind) and magnitude ranges with a preferred width and the size principle, but they can also entertain a conjunctive kind of concept, "odd (or even) numbers within a range" (e.g. "even numbers above 60"), with its own inferred plausibility and the same width preference. The one change is adding this parity-within-a-range kind of concept, so that after 4, 66, 78, 96 people say yes to large even numbers (70, 74) but no to small ones (8, 22), and after 23, 35, 95, 83 they reject even numbers between the examples, rather than spreading "yes" evenly over all even (or all in-range) numbers.

## decade_and_parity_range_concepts  — rank 10, 21.5 ± 7.9 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2306.7

Refinement of `decade_concept_family_prior` (tied with the best): people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception-laden rules as their own kind, and decades such as "numbers in the 60s" as their own kind of rule) and magnitude ranges with a preferred width and the size principle. The one change, taken from `parity_within_range_concepts`, is that people can also entertain "odd (or even) numbers within a range" as a kind of concept with its own inferred plausibility, so examples sharing a tens digit (61, 64, 67) confine "yes" to that decade while examples such as 4, 66, 78, 96 confine it to large even numbers — two ready-made ways of bounding generalisation that the incumbent and the decade model each have only one of.

## rule_noticing_grows_with_examples  — rank 11, 22.0 ± 7.7 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2307.2

People do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules, magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts as the best model, but whether a rule even comes to mind depends on how many examples they have seen: the prior odds of thinking of a rule rather than a magnitude range grow steadily, at an inferred rate, with each additional example. So after one or two examples people generalise mostly by magnitude, and after four examples they look for a rule more readily than the examples' likelihood alone would warrant.

## multiples_within_range_concepts  — rank 12, 22.5 ± 7.6 nats behind the best (3.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2307.7

Refinement of the incumbent `parity_within_range_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules (family-organised prior, exception rules as their own kind), magnitude ranges and "odd (or even) numbers within a range", with the Erlang width preference and the size principle. The one change is that the conjunctive "rule within a range" idea extends beyond parity: people also entertain "multiples of m within a range" (e.g. "multiples of 3 between 10 and 60", "multiples of 9 from 27 to 81") as a further kind of concept with its own inferred plausibility and the same width preference. So after 39, 60, 12, 45 or 63, 27, 81 they accept multiples near the examples (e.g. 36, 54) but reject multiples far outside the examples' span (e.g. 90, 99, or 3), instead of spreading "yes" over all multiples of 3 or over every number in the range.

## doubling_chain_concepts  — rank 13, 27.6 ± 9.0 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2312.8

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (family-organised rule prior with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges and odd/even-within-a-range concepts with a preferred width, and the size principle), but they also hold a kind of concept the best model lacks: multiplicative chains, in which each number is double (or triple) the one before, starting from some number other than 1 (e.g. 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 or 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 or 2, 6, 18, 54). This kind has its own inferred plausibility. The disagreement with the best model shows up on example sets that form such a chain (e.g. 5, 10, 20 or 6, 12, 24): there this model predicts "yes" to the next links (40, 80 or 48, 96) and "no" to other multiples (15, 25, 30 or 18, 30, 36), whereas the best model spreads "yes" over all multiples of 5 (or 6) and nearby numbers.

## gaussian_neighbourhood_magnitude_concepts  — no comparison row

People generalise by rules exactly as the best Bayesian concept learner does, but their magnitude-based concepts are not hard-edged ranges: such a concept is a neighbourhood around a central number, membership falls off smoothly with numerical distance from that centre along a Gaussian similarity gradient whose breadth they infer from the examples (narrow neighbourhoods favoured by the size principle), and examples are produced in proportion to that similarity. A target's graded similarity to the inferred neighbourhood is the probability they say "yes", so "yes" fades gradually with distance from the examples' centre instead of being flat inside the examples' range and stopping at an edge.

## uncertainty_shifted_criterion  — no comparison row

People infer the hidden concept exactly as the best model's Bayesian concept learner does, but their yes/no decision criterion depends on how sure they are which concept the program uses: the more their belief is spread over many competing concepts (rules and range widths), the more they shift their answer away from the inferred membership probability toward "no" (uncertainty aversion) or, if the inferred shift is negative, toward "yes". So two targets with the same membership probability get different answers depending on whether the examples point clearly to one concept or leave many open.

## off_by_one_power_concepts  — no comparison row

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (rules with a family-organised prior and arbitrary-exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges with a beta prior over the share of the 1–100 line they cover, and the size principle), but their space of rules also contains near-misses of the sparse multiplicative rules: "one more than" or "one less than" a power or a square (e.g. 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65 or 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, or 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, 37), forming their own kind of rule with its own inferred, presumably low, plausibility. So after examples such as 31, 3, 1, 15 or 33, 17, 5, 9 people say "yes" to the next shifted power (63, 65) and "no" to other odd numbers or nearby numbers, rather than spreading "yes" over all odd numbers or a range.

## beta_share_width_prior_leaking_judgment  — no comparison row

Refinement of `beta_share_width_exception_concepts` with one component taken from `prior_leaking_membership_judgment`: people infer the hidden concept exactly as the incumbent's Bayesian learner does (family-organised rules with exception rules as their own kind, magnitude ranges judged as a typical share of the 1–100 line, the size principle), but their yes/no judgment is pulled by how plausible the target was before any examples were shown. The log-odds of saying yes is the posterior log-odds that the concept contains the target, shifted by an inferred share of the target's prior log-odds of membership (under the same share-of-the-line range prior), so a priori typical numbers — mid-range numbers that most moderately wide ranges contain, and numbers belonging to many rules — get more "yes" than the examples alone warrant (or less, if the share is negative).
