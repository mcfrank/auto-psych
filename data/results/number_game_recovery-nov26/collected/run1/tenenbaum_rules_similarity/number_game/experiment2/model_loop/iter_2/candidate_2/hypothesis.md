Refinement of `threshold_concepts_erlang_interval`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules, bounded numeric ranges (with an Erlang prior on width) and one-sided thresholds, with a prior mass per concept family, but the "suspicious coincidence" (size principle) is weaker for magnitude concepts than for rules. The one change is that, while examples count fully against broad rules such as "even" versus "powers of 2", their evidence against a wide range is blunted by a learned exponent on the range's size (one recovers the incumbent, zero means a range is judged only by whether it covers the examples), so ranges do not shrink onto the examples as fast as an ideal learner's would. The sharpest disagreement with the best model is for multi-example sets that are clustered in magnitude (e.g. 11, 15 or 42, 62): this model keeps saying "yes" to targets well beyond the examples' extremes, whereas the incumbent narrows onto a tight range around them, while rule-based generalisation (powers of 2, a shared final digit) is unchanged.
