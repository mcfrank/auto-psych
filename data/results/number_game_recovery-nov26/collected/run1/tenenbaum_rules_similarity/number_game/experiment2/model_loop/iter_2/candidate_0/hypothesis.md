People do not infer one program that explains all the examples together; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its average similarity to the stored examples, where two numbers are similar to the degree that they fall together in the familiar number categories people know (parity, multiples, final or contained digits, primes, squares, powers, magnitude ranges and thresholds), with small, distinctive shared categories counting more than broad ones (Shepard's additive-clustering view of number similarity). Because each example generalises on its own and the similarities are averaged rather than multiplied, the "suspicious coincidence" of several examples sharing a tight category never accumulates: a target similar to only one example of a set gets partial "yes" support, and adding examples does not sharpen generalisation the way it does for a Bayesian concept learner.
