# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## without_replacement_size_principle  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2256.8

Refinement of `threshold_concepts_erlang_interval`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules, bounded ranges (with an Erlang prior on width) and one-sided thresholds, with a prior mass per concept family, but they believe the program never repeats itself — each example is a member of the concept not already shown, drawn without replacement. The one change is this without-replacement size principle, which makes the "suspicious coincidence" grow faster than an ideal with-replacement learner's whenever the examples use up a large share of a small concept: sets that nearly exhaust a tiny rule or a narrow range (e.g. 4 of the 7 powers of 2, or 11, 15 filling a five-number band) favour that tight concept even more strongly, so people reject targets just outside it more often than the best model predicts, while sets that are few relative to every candidate concept behave as before.

## centre_weighted_ranges_with_thresholds  — rank 1, 1.9 ± 2.6 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2258.7

Refinement of `threshold_concepts_erlang_interval`, adding one component from `centre_weighted_range_sampling`. People do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules, bounded ranges (with an Erlang prior on width) and one-sided thresholds (their own family), with the size principle and a prior mass per concept family. The one change is that people expect a bounded-range program ("numbers between 30 and 50") to produce numbers clustered toward the middle of its range, while a threshold program ("numbers above 60") still produces its members evenly. How strongly ranges cluster is inferred from the data, and zero recovers the incumbent. An example at a range's edge is then a suspicious coincidence, so ranges centred on the examples that reach past them gain belief relative to thresholds and rules.

## threshold_concepts_erlang_interval  — rank 2, 2.1 ± 2.2 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2258.9

Refinement of `erlang_interval_size_family_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over rules and numeric intervals, with the size principle, a prior mass per concept family (exception rules in their own family) and an Erlang prior over the width of bounded ranges, but they treat one-sided magnitude thresholds ("numbers above 60", "numbers below 20") as a distinct kind of program rather than as just very wide intervals that happen to touch 1 or 100. The one change is that these threshold concepts form their own family with a separately inferred prior mass, shared equally among them and exempt from the width prior, so when all the examples are large (or all small) people extend "yes" all the way to the end of the number line, not just to a band of moderate width around the examples.

## weber_noisy_interval_membership  — rank 14, 7.2 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2264.0

People do Bayesian concept learning over natural rules and numeric ranges as in the best model (size principle, a prior mass per concept family, an Erlang prior on range width), but they check whether a target falls inside a magnitude range using their approximate, noisy sense of number rather than exact arithmetic. The one distortion is this Weber-like perceptual noise, which grows in proportion to a number's size: symbolic rules (even, ends in 3, powers of 2) are checked exactly, but the edges of a range are blurred, so large targets just outside a range are often accepted and large targets just inside are sometimes rejected, while range boundaries among small numbers stay sharp.

## recognition_shortcut_for_examples  — rank 19, 33.1 ± 11.6 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2289.9

People infer the hidden program as in the best current model (Bayesian concept learning over number rules, bounded ranges and one-sided thresholds) and answer most targets by probability matching on that belief, but when the target is one of the numbers they were just shown they do not consult their inferred concept at all: they answer from recognition, saying "yes" at a single fixed rate that does not depend on the set. So the yes rate for a repeated example is the same whether one example or four were shown and whatever concept they suggest, unlike the best model, where it falls as tight concepts that exclude random-looking examples gain belief.

## pedagogical_teacher_sampling  — rank 20, 104.3 ± 17.6 nats behind the best (5.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2361.1

People assume the program's examples were chosen to be helpful, as a teacher would choose them, rather than drawn at random from its concept (rational pedagogy): a teacher holding a concept picks examples in proportion to how strongly each one, on its own, would point a naive learner (one with no preference among kinds of concept) to that concept. People judge each candidate rule, range or threshold by how likely such a teacher would be to have shown exactly these examples, so examples that would point equally well to many other concepts count as weak evidence and a concept is favoured when the examples are the ones most diagnostic of it; they then say "yes" with the probability that the inferred concept contains the target.

## log_number_line_ranges  — rank 21, 305.9 ± 34.7 nats behind the best (8.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2562.7

People do Bayesian concept learning over number rules (with a prior mass per concept family, the exception rules in their own family) and numeric ranges with the size principle, but they represent magnitude on a compressed, logarithmic mental number line: a range is a band on that line, so ranges are a priori equally likely wherever they sit on the log line and their plausibility depends on their ratio width (e.g. 5-10 is as natural as 50-100), not their width in units. As a result, magnitude-based generalisation scales with the examples' size — after small examples people generalise only to nearby small numbers, after large examples to a proportionally much wider band.

## newest_example_size_principle_wsls  — rank 22, 310.3 ± 27.4 nats behind the best (11.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2567.1

People hold a single running guess about the program (a rule or a numeric range, weighed by family plausibility and an Erlang prior on range width) and revise it example by example in the order shown: they keep their current guess with a probability that grows with how well it explains the newest example, and otherwise draw a fresh guess — but when they redraw they judge the "suspicious coincidence" (the size principle) only for the newest example, merely requiring the guess to cover the earlier ones. So evidence for tight concepts does not compound across examples as in an ideal Bayesian learner, and which example drives specificity depends on the order shown (the last examples, plus whatever guess has survived from the first).

## shared_category_exemplar_averaging  — rank 23, 771.8 ± 42.5 nats behind the best (18.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3028.6

People do not infer one program that explains all the examples together; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its average similarity to the stored examples, where two numbers are similar to the degree that they fall together in the familiar number categories people know (parity, multiples, final or contained digits, primes, squares, powers, magnitude ranges and thresholds), with small, distinctive shared categories counting more than broad ones (Shepard's additive-clustering view of number similarity). Because each example generalises on its own and the similarities are averaged rather than multiplied, the "suspicious coincidence" of several examples sharing a tight category never accumulates: a target similar to only one example of a set gets partial "yes" support, and adding examples does not sharpen generalisation the way it does for a Bayesian concept learner.

## average_digit_magnitude_similarity  — rank 24, 790.4 ± 41.3 nats behind the best (19.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3047.2

People do not infer a rule; they judge a target by its average similarity to the examples, where two numbers are similar if they are close in magnitude and share their final digit, with similarity decaying exponentially along the number line and dropping by a fixed factor when the final digits differ. Because the similarity is averaged rather than summed over examples, "yes" depends on the proportion of the examples the target resembles, not on how many examples were shown, so adding dissimilar examples to a set dilutes generalisation to a target near one of them.
