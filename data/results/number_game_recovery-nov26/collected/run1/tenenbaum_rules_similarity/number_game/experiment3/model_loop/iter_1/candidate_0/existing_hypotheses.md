# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## graded_prior_digit_sum_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3522.8

People infer the hidden program as a Bayesian concept learner over number rules and numeric ranges (with family plausibility, the size principle, a preference for moderate range widths, and range outputs clustered toward the range's middle), and their space of candidate programs also includes digit-sum rules — "numbers whose digits add up to s" (e.g. 5, 14, 23, 32, 41, 50) — as a family with its own prior plausibility. Within that family, sums are not equally natural: how plausible a digit-sum rule is a priori changes smoothly with the size of the sum, in a direction and strength inferred from the data. So when the examples share a digit sum, people endorse other numbers with that sum and reject neighbours with a different sum, and they do so more strongly for the favoured sums.

## centre_weighted_range_sampling  — rank 2, 1.3 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3524.0

Refinement of `erlang_interval_size_family_prior`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules and numeric ranges, with the size principle, a prior mass per concept family (exception rules in their own family) and an Erlang prior over range width, but they do not expect a range-based program to produce every number in its range equally often — they expect its outputs to cluster toward the middle of the range, with how strongly they do so inferred from the data (none recovers the parent model). The one change is this centre-weighted sampling from ranges: examples sitting at a range's edge are a "suspicious coincidence" for it, so ranges that are centred on the examples and extend beyond the lowest and highest example gain support, and people say "yes" to targets somewhat past the examples' extremes more often than a uniform-sampling learner predicts.

## first_example_evidence_weight  — rank 6, 8.6 ± 5.5 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3531.3

People infer the hidden program as an ideal Bayesian concept learner would (number rules, bounded ranges with a round-number bias on their edges, and one-sided thresholds, weighed by family plausibility, range width and the size principle, with examples drawn without repetition), but they revise their belief example by example in the order shown and the first example carries its own evidential weight: met before they have any guess about the program, the opening example counts as a looser (or, if the data say so, stronger) piece of evidence than each later example, which counts normally. So the "suspicious coincidence" that the first example points to a tight concept is discounted relative to later examples, which matters most for single-example sets and for which example of a set is shown first.

## without_replacement_size_principle  — rank 7, 8.8 ± 5.2 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3531.6

Refinement of `threshold_concepts_erlang_interval`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules, bounded ranges (with an Erlang prior on width) and one-sided thresholds, with a prior mass per concept family, but they believe the program never repeats itself — each example is a member of the concept not already shown, drawn without replacement. The one change is this without-replacement size principle, which makes the "suspicious coincidence" grow faster than an ideal with-replacement learner's whenever the examples use up a large share of a small concept: sets that nearly exhaust a tiny rule or a narrow range (e.g. 4 of the 7 powers of 2, or 11, 15 filling a five-number band) favour that tight concept even more strongly, so people reject targets just outside it more often than the best model predicts, while sets that are few relative to every candidate concept behave as before.

## round_number_range_boundaries  — rank 8, 9.0 ± 5.3 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3531.7

People infer the hidden program as an ideal Bayesian concept learner would (number rules, bounded ranges and one-sided thresholds, weighed by family plausibility, range width and the size principle, with examples drawn without repetition), but their sense of how plausible a bounded range is is biased by round numbers: multiples of ten act as cognitive reference points, so a range whose lower or upper boundary falls exactly on one (e.g. 20-37 or 40-60) is judged more, or less, likely a priori than an otherwise identical range, with the direction and strength of this round-number bias inferred from the data. This shifts where people stop saying "yes" near multiples of ten — for example whether 40 is included after examples in the low thirties — while leaving rule-based generalisation unchanged.

## graded_belief_criterion_decision  — rank 10, 11.0 ± 4.5 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3533.8

People infer the hidden program as an ideal Bayesian concept learner would (number rules, bounded ranges with a typical width, and one-sided thresholds, with the size principle and examples drawn without repetition), but they do not simply say "yes" with the probability they believe the target belongs. Instead they weigh how strongly they believe it belongs against a personal response criterion, with some response noise: the criterion sets a bias toward "yes" or "no", and the noise sets whether answers are more decisive than the belief (a soft threshold) or more hedged than it, so partly-believed targets are pushed toward or away from the extremes in the same way for every set.

## attention_weighted_set_prototype  — rank 12, 995.9 ± 46.5 nats behind the best (21.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4518.7

People do not infer a hidden rule; they abstract a single prototype of the example set — its typical magnitude and spread on the number line, and the profile of familiar number properties (parity, multiples, final and contained digits, primes, squares, powers) the examples tend to have — and say "yes" to a target in proportion to its similarity to that prototype. Attention to a property grows with how consistently the examples share it, and rarer, more distinctive properties count more, so a target is rejected mainly for lacking properties that all the examples have in common or for lying far from the examples' typical magnitude relative to their spread.
