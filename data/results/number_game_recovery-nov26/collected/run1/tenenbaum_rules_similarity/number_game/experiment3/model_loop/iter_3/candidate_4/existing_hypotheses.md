# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## equal_prior_familiar_rules_digit_sums  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3519.7

Refinement of `graded_prior_digit_sum_concepts`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over number rules, digit-sum rules (with a prior that changes smoothly with the sum) and numeric ranges (Erlang width prior, outputs clustered toward the range's middle, the size principle), but they have no learned preferences among the familiar kinds of arithmetic rule. The one change is to the rule prior: every familiar rule — odd, even, primes, squares, cubes, powers of m, multiples of k, "ends in d" — is equally plausible a priori, with a single inferred share of belief for all of them together (instead of a separate prior mass per family), and ad-hoc rules such as "contains the digit d" or "powers of 2 except 32" are not entertained at all; so how readily a set is read as, say, "multiples of 7" versus "ends in 3" depends only on how well each rule explains the examples.

## sequential_kind_revision  — rank 2, 3.0 ± 3.8 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3522.7

People infer the hidden program as a Bayesian concept learner over number rules (parity, special sets, multiples, final or contained digits, exception rules, digit sums) and numeric ranges of a typical width, but they read the examples one at a time and form an impression of what *kind* of program it is (a rule of some type, or a range) from the first example, which the later examples then revise by a different amount than an ideal learner would: later examples fully decide which program within a kind is meant, but the weight they get in deciding the kind itself is inferred from the data (less than full weight means sticking with the first impression; more than full weight means discounting it). So for multi-example sets, how much belief ends up on ranges versus rules (e.g. "a band around 16" versus "powers of 2" after 16, 8, 64) depends on which example came first, while single-example sets behave as in the ideal learner.

## kind_level_overcommitment  — rank 4, 3.5 ± 3.6 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3523.2

People infer the hidden program as a Bayesian concept learner over number rules (parity, special sets, multiples, final and contained digits, exception rules, digit sums) and numeric ranges of a typical width, with the size principle, but they reason in two stages and mis-weight the first: they first judge what kind of program it is (a parity rule, a multiples rule, a range, ...) and commit to the more convincing kinds more strongly (or, if the data say so, less strongly) than the evidence warrants, then generalise within each kind exactly as an ideal learner would. So when one kind of explanation is ahead (e.g. "a range" for 42, 47), the competing kinds' targets (other multiples, other numbers ending in the same digit) are endorsed less than Bayes predicts, while the choice among programs of the same kind is unaffected.

## first_example_evidence_weight  — rank 14, 11.6 ± 6.0 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3531.3

## attention_weighted_set_prototype  — rank 22, 998.9 ± 46.6 nats behind the best (21.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4518.7

People do not infer a hidden rule; they abstract a single prototype of the example set — its typical magnitude and spread on the number line, and the profile of familiar number properties (parity, multiples, final and contained digits, primes, squares, powers) the examples tend to have — and say "yes" to a target in proportion to its similarity to that prototype.
