# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## rule_plus_exception_neighbourhood  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -1760.3

Refinement of `rule_plus_exception_concepts`: people do the same tempered, exception-tolerant Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (final-digit rules salient), moderate-width ranges on a compressed number line, and "rule plus exception" concepts, but the exception they add to a rule is not a single isolated number — it is a small fuzzy region of the number line around it ("ends in 2, and also numbers around 27"), whose radius (zero, one, two … up to three steps) is a priori more likely the smaller it is, with the fall-off fitted. The one change is this neighbourhood-sized exception: when one example breaks the others' pattern (for instance, the one odd number among even ones), people still endorse it and still extend the rule to distant numbers, but they also endorse the off-pattern example's close neighbours more than the point-exception model predicts, as the critique reports.

## rule_plus_decade_exception  — rank 1, 0.8 ± 4.6 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1761.1

Refinement of `rule_plus_exception_concepts`: people do the same tempered, exception-tolerant Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (final-digit rules salient), moderate-width ranges on a compressed number line, and "rule plus one exception" concepts, but they can also code the exception at the place-value level, as the whole decade the off-pattern number falls in ("ends in 2, and also the twenties"), with a fitted share of the exception prior going to these rule-plus-decade concepts. The one change is this decade-level exception: when one example breaks the others' pattern, people endorse not only that example but also its same-decade neighbours (closing the critique that they generalise around the off-parity example more than the incumbent predicts), while still rejecting its neighbours just across a decade boundary and still extending the rule to distant numbers.

## rule_plus_exception_concepts  — rank 2, 8.6 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1768.9

Refinement of `final_digit_salient_rules`: people do the same tempered, exception-tolerant Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (final-digit rules salient) and moderate-width ranges on a compressed number line, but their concept vocabulary also contains "rule plus one exception" concepts — a rule together with a single extra number it does not contain ("ends in 2, and also 27"), like the classic "powers of 2, or 37" — which share a fitted, reduced part of each rule's prior. The one change is these explicit rule-with-exception concepts: when one example breaks the others' pattern, people treat that example as a genuine member of the program's output rather than a random slip, so they endorse the off-pattern example itself almost unanimously while still rejecting its numerical neighbours and extending the rule to distant numbers.

## example_recognition_digit_salient  — rank 3, 10.2 ± 4.0 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1770.5

Refinement of `final_digit_salient_rules`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (final-digit rules made more salient) and moderate-width ranges on a compressed number line, but when the number asked about is one of the examples they were just shown, they often simply recognise it from memory and say yes outright — it was produced, so the program produces it — instead of judging it from their inferred concept. The one change is this fitted recognition probability for shown examples: people endorse the examples themselves more than an exception-tolerant learner (who suspects any example might be off-concept noise) would, and their tolerance of exceptions then governs only how they generalise to new numbers.

## two_source_example_partition  — rank 4, 10.5 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1770.8

People believe the hidden program may draw its outputs from more than one source, so they spontaneously split the examples into groups: when one example does not fit the others they can attribute it to a second, separate source (with a fitted prior readiness to posit such a split, weighed by how much better two sources explain the examples than one), and they infer a concept for each source — rules or numerical ranges, by the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning with salient final-digit rules on a compressed number line. A target is endorsed if either source would produce it, so people generalise around an off-pattern example in its own right (saying yes to its numerical neighbours and its own rules) while still extending the majority's rule to distant numbers.

## final_digit_salient_rules  — rank 5, 13.4 ± 4.1 nats behind the best (3.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1773.7

Refinement of `erlang_width_compressed_intervals`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules and moderate-width ranges sampled along a compressed number line, but they do not consider all rules equally plausible beforehand — rules that can be read straight off a numeral's final digit (odd, even, "ends in d") are perceptually salient and get a fitted prior boost over the other arithmetic and digit rules. The one change is this final-digit salience in the rule prior: when all examples share a final digit or a parity, people commit to that rule more firmly than to a range, so they endorse distant same-ending and same-parity numbers more and reject opposite-parity neighbours (one step from an example) more than the incumbent predicts.

## arithmetic_progression_residue_rules  — rank 6, 15.8 ± 4.4 nats behind the best (3.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1776.1

People do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (final-digit rules salient) and moderate-width ranges on a compressed number line, but they also notice regular spacing: their concept vocabulary contains "every m-th number" progressions that do not start at zero ("5, 11, 17, … — numbers that leave remainder 5 when divided by 6", for steps 3 to 12), which share a fitted part of the rules' prior. The one change is these equal-step progression concepts: when the examples are evenly spaced off the multiples (e.g. 53, 47, 41 or 34, 26 or 15, 11), people endorse distant numbers on the same step (35, 59, 65; 18, 42, 50) and reject the in-between same-parity numbers far more than the best model, which can only explain such sets by a range or a parity rule.

## example_recognition_family_salience  — rank 7, 16.0 ± 7.3 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1776.3

Refinement of `family_salience_rule_prior`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules (with fitted family salience) and Erlang-width ranges on a compressed number line, but when the target is one of the shown examples they also use what they remember directly — that the program has already produced this very number — so even when their inferred concept leaves it out (as an exception) they still answer yes with a fitted recognition probability. The one change is this episodic recognition of shown examples, which separates "is it in the concept?" from "has the program produced it?": people endorse the examples themselves more than concept membership alone implies (closing the critique that the best model under-predicts yes to example targets), and the learner can treat off-pattern examples as exceptions without that also making it reject those examples.

## family_salience_rule_prior  — rank 8, 17.5 ± 7.5 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1777.8

Refinement of `erlang_width_compressed_intervals`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules and all ranges of 1-100 (members sampled along a compressed number line, Erlang-shaped width prior), but they do not find every kind of rule equally plausible beforehand — whole families of rules differ in salience (parity, final-digit, contained-digit, multiples, powers, and special sets such as primes or squares), with the share of prior belief each family receives fitted and split equally among its members. The one change is this family-salience prior over rules, replacing the flat one: if parity and final-digit rules are especially salient, people extend a shared final digit to distant targets almost unanimously and reject opposite-parity neighbours of same-parity examples more firmly than the incumbent predicts.

## rule_vs_range_size_principle  — rank 9, 129.4 ± 21.5 nats behind the best (6.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1889.7

Refinement of `erlang_width_compressed_intervals`: people do the same exception-tolerant Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and width-weighted ranges on a compressed number line, but they do not apply a single size principle to both kinds of concept. When they weigh how well a symbolic rule ("ends in 2", "even", "multiples of 4") explains the examples, their sensitivity to its size differs, by a fitted factor, from their sensitivity when they weigh a numerical range. The one change is this kind-specific strength of the size principle, which decides whether more examples mainly tighten the range or mainly sharpen the choice between narrow and broad rules (such as "ends in 2" against "even").

## erlang_width_compressed_intervals  — rank 10, 135.4 ± 22.0 nats behind the best (6.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1895.8

Refinement of `log_compressed_interval_sampling`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100, with interval members pictured as sampled along a compressed number line, but they do not consider every numerical range equally plausible beforehand — they expect a program that produces "a range of numbers" to produce a range of moderate width, so tiny ranges hugging one or two examples are a priori unlikely (an Erlang-shaped prior on range width, its typical width fitted). The one change is this width prior on intervals, replacing the flat prior: people fill in the whole span between the examples more, do not build a tight range around a lone exceptional example, and lean on parity or digit rules rather than a tiny range for a target just one step from an example.

## parity_ranges_erlang_span_prior  — rank 11, 136.0 ± 22.1 nats behind the best (6.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1896.3

Refinement of `parity_restricted_compressed_intervals`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules, plain ranges and parity-restricted ranges ("even numbers between 20 and 40"), with range members pictured as sampled along a compressed number line, but they do not consider every range equally plausible beforehand — they expect a program that produces "a range" (plain or parity-restricted) to span a moderate stretch of the number line, so tiny ranges hugging one or two examples are a priori unlikely (an Erlang-shaped prior on the range's span, its typical span fitted, grafted from `erlang_width_compressed_intervals`). The one change is this span prior on all range concepts: people fill in the stretch between the examples with numbers of the examples' parity, reject parity-flipping immediate neighbours, and do not build a tight range around a lone off-pattern example, leaning on parity or digit rules instead.

## recognition_shortcut_for_examples  — rank 12, 229.7 ± 25.6 nats behind the best (9.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1990.1

People answer by a recognition shortcut before any concept inference: when the number asked about is one they were just shown, they recognise it and answer yes directly, with a fixed (fitted) reliability, instead of consulting their inferred concept. Only for numbers they were not shown do they fall back on simple Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic rules and magnitude ranges, so shown examples are endorsed near-uniformly even when they are off-pattern outliers that the inferred concept would treat as exceptions.

## parity_restricted_range_conjunctions  — rank 13, 230.0 ± 27.4 nats behind the best (8.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1990.3

People can build compound concepts that join a numerical range with the examples' parity ("even numbers between 20 and 60"), but considering such a two-part concept carries a cognitive cost, so these conjunctions get only a fitted, reduced share of prior belief alongside the simple rules and plain ranges of exception-tolerant Bayesian concept learning on a compressed number line. Once considered, the size principle strongly favours these small compound concepts, so people fill in the range around the examples but reject immediate neighbours of the wrong parity (and odd-one-out neighbours of an outlier example) far more than a learner who only has plain ranges.

## log_compressed_number_line_intervals  — rank 14, 230.7 ± 25.9 nats behind the best (8.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1991.0

People represent numerical-magnitude concepts ("numbers between 40 and 60") as regions on a logarithmically compressed mental number line, not on the linear 1-100 scale: when they infer which stretch of numbers the program produces, a stretch's size, and how likely the examples are to fall where they do within it, are judged in compressed (Weber-like) units, while arithmetic and digit rules are judged as before. Because the same numerical step is subjectively smaller among large numbers, people extend a magnitude region upward beyond the largest example more readily than downward below the smallest, and generalise across the span between the examples.

## parity_restricted_compressed_intervals  — rank 15, 231.6 ± 27.4 nats behind the best (8.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1991.9

Refinement of `log_compressed_interval_sampling`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules and all ranges of 1-100, with range members pictured as sampled along a compressed mental number line, but their vocabulary of magnitude concepts also contains parity-restricted ranges ("even numbers between 20 and 40", "odd numbers between 51 and 67"), which share a fitted portion of the prior given to ranges. The one change is these conjunctive parity-and-magnitude concepts: when the examples share a parity, people generalise to nearby numbers of the same parity but reject immediate neighbours (which flip parity) and near numbers around an off-pattern example more than a learner with only plain ranges would.

## round_boundary_interval_prior  — rank 16, 247.5 ± 27.9 nats behind the best (8.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2007.8

People infer numerical-range concepts anchored on round numbers: when they consider that the program produces "a range of numbers", they think of ranges whose edges fall on decade boundaries ("from 20 to 50", "the 80s", "up to 100") as far more plausible than ranges with arbitrary edges such as 27-92 (the strength of this round-boundary preference fitted), while otherwise doing the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian learning over arithmetic rules and ranges sampled along a compressed number line. Because the preferred range is snapped outward to the nearest decade edges, people fill in the span between the examples, generalise within the examples' decades but not across a decade boundary just beside an example, and extend above an example ending in a low digit more than below it.

## log_compressed_interval_sampling  — rank 17, 247.6 ± 27.9 nats behind the best (8.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2007.9

Refinement of `exception_tolerant_tempered_mixture`: people do the same exception-tolerant, tempered Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100, but they picture the examples of an interval concept as sampled along a compressed (roughly logarithmic) mental number line, so within a range larger numbers are individually less likely to be drawn and the size principle charges less for extending a range upward than downward. The one change is this compressed-number-line sampling of interval members (degree of compression fitted; no compression gives back essentially the original model), which predicts that people generalise past the largest example more than below the smallest, closing the critique that humans extend upward more than the symmetric models predict.

## graded_profile_with_neighborhoods  — rank 18, 292.9 ± 29.2 nats behind the best (10.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2053.2

Refinement of `graded_feature_profile_matching`: people still judge membership by matching the target against a frequency-weighted profile of the examples' properties (parity, multiples, powers, squares, primes, final digit, contained digits, decade), rarer properties counting more, but the profile also contains each example's own numerical neighbourhood ("numbers close to 27") as one more graded property, with a fitted width, weighted by the same distinctiveness rule and shared in proportion to how many examples fall near it. The one change is this soft neighbourhood property: targets just one to three away from an example gain strong support that decays steeply within a few units, closing the critique that human generalisation falls off over short numerical distances far more sharply than the current models predict.

## weber_scaled_profile_neighborhoods  — rank 19, 379.4 ± 32.7 nats behind the best (11.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2139.8

Refinement of `graded_profile_with_neighborhoods`: people still judge membership by matching the target against a frequency-weighted, distinctiveness-weighted profile of the examples' arithmetic and surface properties plus each example's own soft numerical neighbourhood, but that neighbourhood lives on a logarithmically compressed (Weber-like) mental number line rather than the linear 1-100 scale. The one change is this compressed neighbourhood (grafted from the compressed-number-line models): "close to 8" covers only a unit or two while "close to 80" covers many, the neighbourhood reaches further above an example than below it, and a broader neighbourhood counts as a less distinctive property — so people extend upward beyond the examples more than downward, as the critique of the incumbent reports.

## luce_concept_selection  — rank 20, 491.5 ± 50.5 nats behind the best (9.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2251.9

People do not average their predictions over every concept they find plausible; they commit to the concept that best explains the examples, choosing among the candidate rules and numerical ranges (same exception-tolerant learner over the 45 rules and compressed-line ranges with a moderate-width prior) by a Luce choice whose sharpness on the posterior is fitted, from soft averaging to near-single-hypothesis selection. Because the winning concept takes nearly all the weight, when every example shares a final digit or a parity people extend that rule almost unanimously and reject parity-violating neighbours more firmly than a hedging Bayesian learner would.

## pairwise_exemplar_consequential_regions  — rank 21, 634.6 ± 42.0 nats behind the best (15.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2395.0

People do not infer one concept that explains all the examples together. They keep each example in memory as a separate exemplar and compare the target with each one in turn, judging how likely the two numbers are to fall in a common consequential region — a shared arithmetic or digit rule, or a stretch of the number line of moderate width, where smaller shared regions count for more — and their yes probability is the average of these pairwise similarities across the stored examples. Because every example generalises on its own and no example is ever discounted as an exception, people endorse numbers around an off-pattern example (e.g. neighbours of the one odd number in an even set) far more than a joint concept learner predicts, and more examples do not tighten generalisation.

## spread_adaptive_log_similarity  — rank 22, 876.8 ± 49.2 nats behind the best (17.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2637.1

People judge whether the program would produce a target purely by how close it lies to the examples on a logarithmically compressed mental number line, with an exponential (Shepard-style) generalization gradient around each example whose breadth is set by how spread out the examples are: tightly clustered examples yield a narrow gradient, widely scattered examples a broad one. Endorsement is the probability that at least one example "captures" the target under this gradient, so targets right at or between nearby examples are endorsed, generalization reaches further above an example than below it, and arithmetic or digit regularities play no role.
