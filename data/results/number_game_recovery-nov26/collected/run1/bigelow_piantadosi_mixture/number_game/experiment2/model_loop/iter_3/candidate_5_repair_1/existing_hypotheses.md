# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## digit_coincidence_noticing_guess  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2097.5

Refinement of the incumbent sticky_guess_fitted_sampling_trust (a single working concept guess — a rule or a numerical range — formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, kept with fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, digit rules discounted, fitted trust in the examples). The one change: a rule defined only by how numbers are written ("ends in 6", "contains the digit 3") becomes a salient candidate only once people notice that digit as a coincidence shared by at least two of the examples seen so far; until then — always after a single example, and for a digit only one example has — its plausibility is reduced by a fitted availability factor. So a lone 46 does not readily lead people to say yes to 96, whereas 46 and 86 together do.

## coincidence_gated_all_rules_guess  — rank 1, 2.1 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2099.7

Refinement of digit_coincidence_noticing_guess (a single working concept guess — a rule or a numerical range — formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, digit rules discounted, fitted trust in the examples, and digit rules made salient only once a digit is noticed as shared by at least two examples). The one change: the same coincidence-noticing gate applies to arithmetic rules too — "even", "multiples of 7" or "squares" becomes a salient candidate only once at least two of the examples seen so far share that property, and until then (always after a single example) its plausibility is reduced by its own fitted availability factor. So a lone example suggests a range of nearby numbers rather than a broad rule, people generalise less to far-away numbers from one example, and the first working guess is range-like until a shared property is noticed.

## sticky_guess_fitted_sampling_trust  — rank 2, 2.5 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.0

Refinement of digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range, with a discounted prior on digit-based rules and power-law/Erlang priors on ranges), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering from all examples only when contradicted. The one change (the component taken from inferred_sampling_reliability_concepts): how much people trust that the examples were really drawn from the program's concept, rather than being stray numbers, is fitted instead of fixed at 90% — so the strength of the size principle, and hence how readily people entertain (and answer by) guesses that do not even contain every example, is set by the data.

## coincidence_gated_rule_guess  — rank 3, 2.8 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.3

People do not suspect a rule of any kind — arithmetic ("powers of 2", "multiples of 7", "squares") or written ("ends in 6") — until it is suggested by a coincidence: a rule becomes a salient candidate only once at least two of the examples seen so far satisfy it, and before that its plausibility is reduced by a fitted availability factor, so a lone example is generalised mostly as a numerical range around it. Otherwise they hold a single sticky working guess formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, as in the current best model (whose availability penalty applies only to digit rules). The sharpest disagreement lives in single-example sets and in sets where only one example fits an arithmetic rule: after a lone 16 the best model still endorses far-away 64, 4 or 32 as powers of 2, whereas this model predicts these far rule-consistent targets are rejected until a second example such as 8 makes the rule a noticed coincidence.

## digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess  — rank 4, 2.9 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.4

Refinement of sticky_guess_power_law_ranges: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering from all examples only when contradicted, with range guesses shaped by a power-law endpoint and Erlang width prior. The one change: rules defined only by how a number is written ("ends in 7", "contains the digit 3") are a priori less (or more) plausible than arithmetic rules (odd, multiples, powers, squares, primes), by a single fitted weight on the digit-rule family — so people latch onto and extend "same last digit" guesses to far-away numbers less readily than the incumbent predicts.

## sampled_guess_vote_threshold  — rank 5, 3.0 ± 3.6 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.6

People form guesses about the concept exactly as in the simplest sticky-guess learner (a working guess from the first example, kept while later examples fit it, revised from all examples when contradicted), but they do not answer by reporting one guess: they call to mind a handful of such candidate guesses and say "yes" only if a sufficient share of them contain the target. The effective number of guesses consulted and the share required (a yes/no criterion) are fitted, so the same belief maps onto a sharpened or softened, more liberal or more conservative yes rate; one guess with no criterion recovers plain probability matching.

## target_as_evidence_sticky_guess  — rank 6, 3.1 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.6

Refinement of digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess (Bayesian concept learning over rules and numerical ranges, a single sticky working guess formed from the first example, digit rules discounted). The one distortion: people treat the question itself as weak evidence — they partly assume the experimenter tends to ask about numbers the program actually produces, so the asked-about target is counted, with a fitted fraction of the weight of a real example, as one more sample from the concept. This "leading question" bias inflates "yes" for targets that would belong to a small, specific concept (e.g. a power of 2 after 16 and 8) far more than for targets that only fit broad concepts.

## first_guess_win_stay_concepts  — rank 7, 3.8 ± 3.5 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.3

People do not weigh all examples together at once; they hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range) that they form from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, and as each further example arrives they keep that guess, with a fitted stickiness, as long as the new example fits it, reconsidering the concept from all examples seen so far only when the new example contradicts the guess (or when they do not stick). Because the guess is anchored on what fitted the early examples, a broad concept chosen after the first example survives later examples that would have favoured a narrower one, so the same examples in a different order produce different generalisations (a primacy effect).

## sticky_guess_last_digit_rule_prior  — rank 8, 3.9 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.4

Refinement of sticky_guess_power_law_ranges: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range, with power-law and Erlang priors on ranges), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering only when contradicted. The one change: rules are not all equally natural guesses — the ten "ends in digit d" rules share a single fitted prior weight relative to the other rules, so people latch onto and keep last-digit rules less (or more) readily than arithmetic rules like multiples or powers, which governs how much they endorse far-away numbers merely because they share an example's final digit.

## sticky_guess_power_law_ranges  — rank 9, 4.0 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.5

Refinement of first_guess_win_stay_concepts: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept, with a fitted stickiness, as later examples arrive as long as each new example fits it, reconsidering from all examples so far only when it is contradicted (or they do not stick). The one change, the component taken from power_law_interval_endpoints: when they consider a numerical range they do not treat all locations on the number line alike — the range's endpoints have a prior plausibility that follows a fitted power law over 1-100 (on top of the incumbent's Erlang preference for a typical width), so whether a range guess is formed and kept depends on whether the examples are small or large numbers.

## fuzzy_trace_example_memory  — rank 10, 4.2 ± 4.2 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.8

People do Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and numerical ranges (with the size principle), but their memory of the examples is imprecise: each example is kept verbatim only with a fitted probability, and otherwise only its rough magnitude (within about five of its true value) is retained. Concepts are evaluated against these fuzzy memory traces, so concepts that depend on an example's exact identity (last digit, multiples, powers) lose support with every imperfectly remembered example while numerical ranges, which only need the magnitude, do not — making people generalise less along digit-based rules to far-away targets, especially from longer sets.

## sticky_guess_attentional_lapse  — rank 11, 4.5 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2102.0

Refinement of sticky_guess_fitted_sampling_trust: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range, digit rules discounted, power-law/Erlang priors on ranges, fitted trust in the examples), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering from all examples only when contradicted. The one change (the component taken from lapse_biased_guess_concepts): on a fitted fraction of trials people do not consult their guess at all and simply guess, answering "yes" with a fitted bias — which explains why they reject even the examples themselves about a quarter of the time without forcing the concept learner to distrust its examples, and puts a common floor under "yes" for numbers outside every plausible concept.

## recency_weighted_guess_revision  — rank 12, 6.1 ± 3.7 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2103.6

Refinement of first_guess_win_stay_concepts: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept, with a fitted stickiness, while each new example fits it. The one change is in how they revise when a new example contradicts the guess (or they do not stick): they rebuild the guess around the newest example, which they weigh fully, while their recall of the earlier examples is only partial — earlier examples' evidence is down-weighted by a fitted retention factor — so revisions are recency-driven and the final concept is pulled toward whatever fits the example that forced the change.

## magnitude_compressed_sampling_concepts  — rank 13, 34.0 ± 7.5 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2131.5

Refinement of power_law_endpoints_erlang_width (same 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100, size principle, 10% noisy examples, a prior weight per rule, power-law endpoint prior and Erlang width prior). The one change is in how people think the examples were sampled: they imagine the program drawing numbers uniformly on a compressed, logarithmic-like mental number line rather than uniformly in raw value, with the degree of compression fitted, so within a range or rule smaller numbers are expected to be drawn more often than larger ones. Because a span among large numbers is then psychologically narrower, the size principle penalises extending a range less for large-number examples than for small-number ones, so generalisation beyond the examples widens in proportion to their magnitude (Weber-like scaling).

## power_law_endpoints_erlang_width  — rank 14, 34.5 ± 7.7 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2132.1

Refinement of power_law_interval_endpoints: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100 (size principle, 10% noisy examples, a prior weight per rule) with interval endpoints whose prior plausibility follows a fitted power law over the number line, but the one change is that they also expect a numerical range to have a characteristic width: the interval prior is additionally weighted by an Erlang density on the interval's size with a fitted typical size (the component taken from erlang_interval_prior_concepts). So where a range lies on the number line and how broad it is both shape the prior, which jointly sets how far people generalise beyond the examples' span for small- versus large-number sets.

## round_endpoint_interval_concepts  — rank 15, 38.8 ± 8.1 nats behind the best (4.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2136.3

Refinement of erlang_interval_prior_concepts: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100 (size principle, 10% noisy examples, a free prior weight per rule, an Erlang prior on interval size), but when they imagine the program picking a numerical range they favour ranges with round-number boundaries (starting at 1 or a multiple of ten, ending at a multiple of ten, e.g. "20 to 40" or "1 to 50") over arbitrary ones like "17 to 43". The one change is a fitted prior boost for each round endpoint, so generalisation from clustered examples extends to, and then drops off sharply at, the nearest decade boundaries rather than fading smoothly with width alone.

## category_step_exemplar_similarity  — rank 16, 45.4 ± 10.6 nats behind the best (4.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2142.9

People do not infer a concept; they retrieve familiar number categories stored in memory as ordered lists of their members (odd numbers, powers of 2, numbers ending in 6, ranges bounded by multiples of five, ...), each activated by how similar the examples are to its stored members. They then endorse a target by its exemplar similarity to the nearest example measured along each activated category — how far through the category's member list they must step from an example to reach the target — so a member only a step or two away from an example (32 after 16 in the powers of 2) is endorsed readily while one many steps away (96 after a lone 46 in "ends in 6") is endorsed much less.

## representativeness_next_output_judgment  — rank 17, 196.2 ± 21.5 nats behind the best (9.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2293.7

People do not answer by asking whether the target belongs to the concept they have inferred; they judge by representativeness — how typical the target would be as the program's next output. Using the same concept learning as the sticky single-guess learner (a working guess formed from the first example and kept while later examples fit it), they estimate how much more likely the program is to produce this particular number than an arbitrary number from 1-100, and say "yes" the more strongly that likelihood ratio exceeds a fitted criterion. So a target that belongs only to a broad concept (all even numbers, everything ending in 6) is endorsed much less than one that belongs to a small, specific concept, even when both are certainly members.

## consistency_attention_similarity  — rank 18, 443.0 ± 32.8 nats behind the best (13.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2540.5

People judge a target by its similarity to the examples, but they attend to each dimension of number similarity (magnitude band, last digit, parity, divisibility, being a square, power or prime) in proportion to how surprisingly consistently the examples agree on it: a property all examples share counts more the rarer it is among 1-100 and the more examples share it. The target's psychological distance is the summed attention on the shared properties it lacks plus its number-line distance from the nearest example, and the probability of "yes" falls off along this distance — so a far-away number is endorsed when it shares a rare property the examples all have, and a near number is rejected when it breaks one.

## independent_example_generalization  — rank 19, 702.0 ± 48.4 nats behind the best (14.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2799.6

People never pool the examples into a single inferred concept. Each example on its own triggers Bayesian generalization (over mathematical rules and numerical ranges, with the size principle applied to that one example), and the target is endorsed according to the average of these separate single-example generalizations. So adding examples does not sharpen generalization onto a shared rule or fill the gap between them as a range: a target is endorsed to the extent that it is something that would be generalized to from the individual examples, one at a time.

## one_back_digit_coincidence_guess  — no comparison row

People hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example and kept while later examples fit it, and a rule about how numbers are written ("ends in 6", "contains the digit 3") becomes a salient guess only once they notice its digit as a coincidence. They notice such coincidences by a one-back working-memory comparison, setting each new example against the example shown just before it, so a shared digit is noticed only when two consecutive examples have it. So the order of the examples matters: "46, 86, 13" makes "ends in 6" salient and leads people to say yes to 96, whereas "46, 13, 86" does not, although both contain the same numbers.

## range_strain_triggered_rule_search  — no comparison row

People look for a numerical range first and search for a rule only when a range is a strained explanation: the more widely the examples seen so far are spread over the number line, the more readily they consider rules ("powers of 2", "even", "ends in 6"), whereas a single example or a tight cluster of examples is read as a place on the number line and rules stay largely unconsidered (by a fitted availability factor). Otherwise they hold a single sticky working guess formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, as in the current best model — so clustered examples that share a rule (20, 22, 24) are generalised as a range, while scattered ones (16, 8, 64) prompt the rule.

## compressed_concept_size_sticky_guess  — no comparison row

People learn the concept as in the current best model (a single sticky working guess — a rule or a numerical range — formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, with the size principle favouring small concepts), but with one distortion: they misjudge how many numbers a candidate concept contains, perceiving concept size on a compressed, numerosity-like scale relative to the whole range 1-100 (with the compression fitted). So they feel "powers of 2" or a narrow range is less small, and "even numbers" less large, than it really is, which weakens (or, if expanded, strengthens) the size principle's preference for specific concepts without changing how strongly a non-fitting example counts against a guess.

## coincidence_triggered_guess_rethink  — no comparison row

Refinement of the incumbent digit_coincidence_noticing_guess (a single working concept guess — a rule or a numerical range — formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, kept with fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, digit rules discounted and made salient only once a digit is noticed as shared by at least two examples, fitted trust in the examples). The one change: noticing a coincidence is itself a trigger to rethink. When a new example makes people notice a digit it shares with an earlier example, they drop their current guess with a fitted probability and reconsider the concept from all the examples, even if that guess still fits. So after 46 then 86, a range guess formed from 46 that happens to contain 86 is often given up in favour of "ends in 6", and far numbers like 96 are endorsed more than the purely sticky learner predicts.
