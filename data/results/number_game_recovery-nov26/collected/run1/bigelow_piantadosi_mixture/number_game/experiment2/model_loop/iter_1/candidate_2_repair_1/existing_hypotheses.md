# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## sticky_guess_fitted_sampling_trust  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2100.0

Refinement of digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range, with a discounted prior on digit-based rules and power-law/Erlang priors on ranges), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering from all examples only when contradicted. The one change (the component taken from inferred_sampling_reliability_concepts): how much people trust that the examples were really drawn from the program's concept, rather than being stray numbers, is fitted instead of fixed at 90% — so the strength of the size principle, and hence how readily people entertain (and answer by) guesses that do not even contain every example, is set by the data.

## digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess  — rank 1, 0.4 ± 2.6 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.4

Refinement of sticky_guess_power_law_ranges: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering from all examples only when contradicted, with range guesses shaped by a power-law endpoint and Erlang width prior. The one change: rules defined only by how a number is written ("ends in 7", "contains the digit 3") are a priori less (or more) plausible than arithmetic rules (odd, multiples, powers, squares, primes), by a single fitted weight on the digit-rule family — so people latch onto and extend "same last digit" guesses to far-away numbers less readily than the incumbent predicts.

## target_as_evidence_sticky_guess  — rank 2, 0.6 ± 2.4 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2100.6

Refinement of digit_rule_discount_sticky_guess (Bayesian concept learning over rules and numerical ranges, a single sticky working guess formed from the first example, digit rules discounted). The one distortion: people treat the question itself as weak evidence — they partly assume the experimenter tends to ask about numbers the program actually produces, so the asked-about target is counted, with a fitted fraction of the weight of a real example, as one more sample from the concept. This "leading question" bias inflates "yes" for targets that would belong to a small, specific concept (e.g. a power of 2 after 16 and 8) far more than for targets that only fit broad concepts.

## first_guess_win_stay_concepts  — rank 3, 1.2 ± 3.2 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.3

People do not weigh all examples together at once; they hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range) that they form from the first example by Bayesian concept learning, and as each further example arrives they keep that guess, with a fitted stickiness, as long as the new example fits it, reconsidering the concept from all examples seen so far only when the new example contradicts the guess (or when they do not stick). Because the guess is anchored on what fitted the early examples, a broad concept chosen after the first example survives later examples that would have favoured a narrower one, so the same examples in a different order produce different generalisations (a primacy effect).

## sticky_guess_last_digit_rule_prior  — rank 4, 1.4 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.4

Refinement of sticky_guess_power_law_ranges: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range, with power-law and Erlang priors on ranges), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept with a fitted stickiness while later examples fit it, reconsidering only when contradicted. The one change: rules are not all equally natural guesses — the ten "ends in digit d" rules share a single fitted prior weight relative to the other rules, so people latch onto and keep last-digit rules less (or more) readily than arithmetic rules like multiples or powers, which governs how much they endorse far-away numbers merely because they share an example's final digit.

## sticky_guess_power_law_ranges  — rank 5, 1.4 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (0.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.5

Refinement of first_guess_win_stay_concepts: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept, with a fitted stickiness, as later examples arrive as long as each new example fits it, reconsidering from all examples so far only when it is contradicted (or they do not stick). The one change, the component taken from power_law_interval_endpoints: when they consider a numerical range they do not treat all locations on the number line alike — the range's endpoints have a prior plausibility that follows a fitted power law over 1-100 (on top of the incumbent's Erlang preference for a typical width), so whether a range guess is formed and kept depends on whether the examples are small or large numbers.

## fuzzy_trace_example_memory  — rank 6, 1.7 ± 4.0 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2101.8

People do Bayesian concept learning over mathematical rules and numerical ranges (with the size principle), but their memory of the examples is imprecise: each example is kept verbatim only with a fitted probability, and otherwise only its rough magnitude (within about five of its true value) is retained. Concepts are evaluated against these fuzzy memory traces, so concepts that depend on an example's exact identity (last digit, multiples, powers) lose support with every imperfectly remembered example while numerical ranges, which only need the magnitude, do not — making people generalise less along digit-based rules to far-away targets, especially from longer sets.

## recency_weighted_guess_revision  — rank 7, 3.6 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2103.6

Refinement of first_guess_win_stay_concepts: people hold a single working guess about the concept (a rule or a numerical range), formed from the first example by Bayesian concept learning and kept, with a fitted stickiness, while each new example fits it. The one change is in how they revise when a new example contradicts the guess (or they do not stick): they rebuild the guess around the newest example, which they weigh fully, while their recall of the earlier examples is only partial — earlier examples' evidence is down-weighted by a fitted retention factor — so revisions are recency-driven and the final concept is pulled toward whatever fits the example that forced the change.

## magnitude_compressed_sampling_concepts  — rank 8, 31.5 ± 7.4 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2131.5

Refinement of power_law_endpoints_erlang_width (same 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100, size principle, 10% noisy examples, a prior weight per rule, power-law endpoint prior and Erlang width prior). The one change is in how people think the examples were sampled: they imagine the program drawing numbers uniformly on a compressed, logarithmic-like mental number line rather than uniformly in raw value, with the degree of compression fitted, so within a range or rule smaller numbers are expected to be drawn more often than larger ones. Because a span among large numbers is then psychologically narrower, the size principle penalises extending a range less for large-number examples than for small-number ones, so generalisation beyond the examples widens in proportion to their magnitude (Weber-like scaling).

## power_law_endpoints_erlang_width  — rank 9, 32.0 ± 7.5 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2132.1

Refinement of power_law_interval_endpoints: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100 (size principle, 10% noisy examples, a prior weight per rule) with interval endpoints whose prior plausibility follows a fitted power law over the number line, but the one change is that they also expect a numerical range to have a characteristic width: the interval prior is additionally weighted by an Erlang density on the interval's size with a fitted typical size (the component taken from erlang_interval_prior_concepts). So where a range lies on the number line and how broad it is both shape the prior, which jointly sets how far people generalise beyond the examples' span for small- versus large-number sets.

## round_endpoint_interval_concepts  — rank 10, 36.3 ± 8.0 nats behind the best (4.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2136.3

Refinement of erlang_interval_prior_concepts: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over the 45 rules and all intervals of 1-100 (size principle, 10% noisy examples, a free prior weight per rule, an Erlang prior on interval size), but when they imagine the program picking a numerical range they favour ranges with round-number boundaries (starting at 1 or a multiple of ten, ending at a multiple of ten, e.g. "20 to 40" or "1 to 50") over arbitrary ones like "17 to 43". The one change is a fitted prior boost for each round endpoint, so generalisation from clustered examples extends to, and then drops off sharply at, the nearest decade boundaries rather than fading smoothly with width alone.

## independent_example_generalization  — rank 11, 699.5 ± 48.3 nats behind the best (14.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2799.6

People never pool the examples into a single inferred concept. Each example on its own triggers Bayesian generalization (over mathematical rules and numerical ranges, with the size principle applied to that one example), and the target is endorsed according to the average of these separate single-example generalizations. So adding examples does not sharpen generalization onto a shared rule or fill the gap between them as a range: a target is endorsed to the extent that it is something that would be generalized to from the individual examples, one at a time.

## consistency_attention_similarity  — no comparison row

People judge a target by its similarity to the examples, but they attend to each dimension of number similarity (magnitude band, last digit, parity, divisibility, being a square, power or prime) in proportion to how surprisingly consistently the examples agree on it: a property all examples share counts more the rarer it is among 1-100 and the more examples share it. The target's psychological distance is the summed attention on the shared properties it lacks plus its number-line distance from the nearest example, and the probability of "yes" falls off along this distance — so a far-away number is endorsed when it shares a rare property the examples all have, and a near number is rejected when it breaks one.

## sampled_guess_vote_threshold  — no comparison row

People form guesses about the concept exactly as in the simplest sticky-guess learner (a working guess from the first example, kept while later examples fit it, revised from all examples when contradicted), but they do not answer by reporting one guess: they call to mind a handful of such candidate guesses and say "yes" only if a sufficient share of them contain the target. The effective number of guesses consulted and the share required (a yes/no criterion) are fitted, so the same belief maps onto a sharpened or softened, more liberal or more conservative yes rate; one guess with no criterion recovers plain probability matching.
