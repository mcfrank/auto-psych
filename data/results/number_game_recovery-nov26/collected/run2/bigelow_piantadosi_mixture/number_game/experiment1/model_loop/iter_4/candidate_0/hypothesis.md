People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges as the best model (size-graded rule prior, distinct examples drawn without replacement, 10% strays, occasional guessing), but they believe the program does not pick a concept's members equally often: it favours numbers of a particular magnitude (small numbers more often than large, or the reverse), so an example's evidence for a concept depends on how much of the concept's magnitude-weighted mass it carries, not just on the concept's member count. This single magnitude-biased sampling assumption disagrees most sharply with the best model on sets made of large numbers (e.g. 92, 82, 27, 42 or 83, 38, 58, 68) versus small ones (e.g. 16, 8 or 26, 2): with a small-number bias, large examples are unexpectedly strong evidence for concepts whose members are mostly large (high ranges, "contains 8", "ends in 2"), and small examples are weak evidence for small-number rules such as powers of 2.
