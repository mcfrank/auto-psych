## Hypotheses

- **position_decaying_example_evidence**: People read the examples one after another and build a running guess about the program, but each example revises that guess by a steadily changing amount: the evidence an example carries shrinks (or grows) by a constant factor with each step down the sequence, with the factor inferred, so the total weight of the evidence is not fixed but depends on how many examples came before. Otherwise they do plain Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range of 1-100 (equally plausible ranges, independent draws with some strays, occasional guessing). This predicts that a later example narrows generalization less than a full-weight learner expects when evidence fades (for example, a fourth example after 31, 3, 1), and that the same numbers shown in a different order generalize toward the concepts favoured by whichever examples came first.
- **position_decay_upward_room**: Refinement of `position_decaying_example_evidence`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range of 1-100. Each example's evidence shrinks or grows by a constant factor along the sequence, and the examples are read as independent draws with some strays, with occasional guessing. The one change, the component taken from `upward_room_range_prior`, replaces the flat range prior with a counting habit: people expect the program's numbers to carry on upward past the largest example, so a candidate range's prior plausibility rises with how far it reaches above the examples (strength inferred). This predicts more "yes" to numbers a little above a cluster than to numbers the same distance below it (e.g. 67 versus 57 after 60, 62, 64), whatever order the examples were shown in, while rule and digit-sum generalization and the order-dependent weighting of the examples are kept.
- **span_filling_example_evidence**: Refinement of `position_decaying_example_evidence`: people read the examples one after another and do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range, independent draws with some strays, occasional guessing), with each later example's evidence changing by a constant factor per position. The one change is that how much an example revises their guess also depends on whether it tells them anything new about the extent of the program: an example that falls inside the span of the examples shown before it (e.g. 15 after 31, 3, 1) carries its own inferred evidence weight, either discounted as redundant (so it narrows generalization less than a full-weight learner expects) or taken as extra confirmation that the pattern holds (so it narrows it more). This predicts that the same numbers shown in an order where the last one fills a gap (31, 3, 1, 15) generalize differently from an order where every example extends the span (1, 3, 15, 31), with the difference falling on members of broad rules and wide ranges.
- **offset_progression_concepts**: Alongside the arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and the numeric ranges of the best model, people entertain arithmetic progressions that run with an offset through the whole number line, "numbers that are r more than a multiple of m" (e.g. 2, 7, 12, 17, ... or 1, 4, 7, 10, ...) for steps of 3 to 12. All of these progressions are equally likely a priori, and the family as a whole takes an inferred share of the prior belief given to rules; otherwise learning is as in the best model (size principle with some strays, position-decaying example evidence, occasional guessing). This predicts that examples sharing a remainder (e.g. 4, 16, 28 or 17, 32, 47) draw "yes" to other numbers with that remainder spread across the whole line (40, 52, 88 or 2, 62, 92), beyond what "even numbers", "ends in d" or a range gives.
- **set_size_scaled_evidence_weight**: Refinement of the incumbent `heading_weber_upward_with_replacement`: people do the same Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range of 1-100. They read the examples as independent draws with some strays, sometimes guess, judge a range's breadth Weber-like, stretch ranges toward the heading and expect the numbers to carry on upward past the largest example. The one change is how much weight people give the evidence as a set grows: the weight per example is not fixed but rises or falls as a power of how many examples were shown, with strength and direction inferred. So a single example is judged as before, while bigger sets sharpen belief toward narrow rules and tight ranges by more (or by less) than an ideal learner would. This predicts that adding a fourth example to a set changes how far "yes" spreads by a different amount than the incumbent predicts (e.g. 31, 3, 1 compared with 31, 3, 1, 15).
- **heading_weber_upward_with_replacement**: Refinement of the incumbent `heading_weber_with_upward_counting`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range of 1-100. They allow some strays, sometimes guess, judge a range's breadth Weber-like, let the last step's direction stretch ranges toward where the sequence is heading, and expect the program's numbers to carry on upward past the largest example. The one change is the sampling assumption. People do not assume the program avoids repeating itself, so they read each example as an independent draw from the concept's members (with replacement), not as a draw from the members not yet shown. This predicts that examples that nearly use up a small rule or a short range (e.g. 1, 8, 27 for cubes, or 60, 61, 62, 63 for a four-number stretch) make that concept no more credible than the plain size principle implies, so people say "yes" more often to members of broader rules and ranges after such sets than the incumbent predicts.
- **heading_weber_with_upward_counting**: Refinement of the incumbent `heading_extrapolation_weber_width`. People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range of 1-100. They read the examples as distinct draws with some strays, sometimes guess, judge a range's breadth Weber-like (relative to its magnitude), and let the direction of the last step stretch the ranges toward where the sequence is heading.

The one change, the component taken from `upward_room_range_prior`, is a counting habit that does not depend on order. Whatever order the examples came in, people expect the program's numbers to carry on upward past the largest example, so they credit a candidate range by how far it reaches above the examples (strength inferred). This predicts more "yes" to numbers a little above a cluster than below it even for single examples and for sets whose last step points down (e.g. 67 versus 57 after 64, 62, 60), on top of the order-dependent heading effect.
- **rule_breadth_heading_upward_counting**: Refinement of the incumbent `heading_weber_with_upward_counting`. People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and every numeric range. They read the examples as distinct draws with some strays, sometimes guess, judge a range's breadth relative to its magnitude, stretch ranges toward where the sequence is heading, and expect the numbers to carry on upward past the largest example. The one change, the component taken from `rule_breadth_prior_concepts`, is that the arithmetic and digit rules do not share an equal prior: before seeing any examples, people find a rule more (or less) plausible as a power of how many numbers it contains, with the strength and direction inferred (the data suggest a slight preference for broad rules). This predicts that broad rules such as "even numbers" or "contains a 3" take a different share of the "yes" answers to their members than the incumbent gives them, compared with narrow rules such as "powers of 4", while range generalization is unchanged.
