People judge the target by its similarity to the example set's feature prototype, where the features are number properties (arithmetic and digit properties, digit sums, and lying within a given stretch of the number line), each feature counts by the share of examples that have it, and rarer properties count as more distinctive. Similarity is the proportion of the prototype's distinctive feature mass that the target shares, and the more similar the target, the more likely a "yes" (with occasional guessing). Because feature salience is fixed and there is no inference over hidden programs, adding examples does not sharpen generalization beyond the properties they share, so after 16, 8, 64 people should say "yes" to members of broad shared properties (e.g. even or small numbers) more often than a size-principle learner predicts.
