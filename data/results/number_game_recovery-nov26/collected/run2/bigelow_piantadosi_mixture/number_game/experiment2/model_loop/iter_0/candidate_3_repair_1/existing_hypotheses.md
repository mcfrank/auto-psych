# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## without_replacement_size_principle  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -1931.7

Refinement of `equal_prior_concepts_with_guess_lapse`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every interval of 1-100 (equal rule prior, 10% of examples random, occasional guessing at their own yes rate), but they understand that the program shows distinct examples, so each further example is read as a draw from the concept's members not yet shown (sampling without replacement) rather than from all its members again. The one change is this without-replacement size principle, which adds no free parameter and predicts that several examples sharpen generalization toward small concepts more than the standard model does: a few examples that nearly exhaust a small rule (e.g. 16, 8, 64 for powers of 2) or a short range make that concept far more credible than broader alternatives.

## rule_breadth_prior_concepts  — rank 1, 1.0 ± 2.5 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1932.7

Refinement of `equal_prior_concepts_with_guess_lapse`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every interval of 1-100 (size principle, 10% of examples random), with occasional guessing at their own yes rate — but before seeing any examples they already prefer rules by their breadth, giving each rule a prior weight that rises or falls as a power of how many numbers it contains (a preference for specific, "interesting" rules over broad ones, or the reverse). The one change is replacing the flat prior across rules with this single size-graded rule prior, which predicts that broad rules such as "odd" or "even" win less (or more) of the posterior than an equal-prior learner gives them, e.g. fewer "yes" answers to arbitrary odd numbers after 85, 19, 91.

## breadth_prior_concepts_free_outlier_rate  — rank 2, 1.6 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1933.3

Refinement of `rule_breadth_prior_concepts`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules (with the same breadth-graded rule prior) and every interval of 1-100, with the size principle and occasional guessing at their own yes rate — but how often they believe the program emits a stray random example is a property of the learner, inferred from their answers, rather than fixed at one in ten. The one change is freeing this outlier rate (the component taken from `digit_concepts_inferred_outlier_rate`), which claims that part of what the lapse now absorbs is really people discounting a misfitting example as a stray, so tight rules survive an odd-one-out example (e.g. 85, 19, 91 or 14, 47) instead of being abandoned for broad ones.

## without_replacement_free_outlier_rate  — rank 3, 2.1 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1933.8

Refinement of `concepts_sampled_without_replacement`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules (with the size-graded rule prior) and every interval of 1-100, reading each further example as a draw from the concept's members not yet shown (strong sampling without replacement), with occasional guessing at their own yes rate. The one change (the component taken from `breadth_prior_concepts_free_outlier_rate`) is that how often they believe the program shows a stray random number is a property of the learner, inferred from their answers, rather than fixed at one in ten — so a misfitting example (e.g. 14, 47 or 85, 19, 91) is discounted as a stray at the learner's own rate, letting tight, nearly exhausted concepts survive it instead of yielding to broad rules and wide ranges.

## concepts_sampled_without_replacement  — rank 4, 2.2 ± 1.0 nats behind the best (2.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1933.8

Refinement of `rule_breadth_prior_concepts`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every interval of 1-100, with a size-graded rule prior, 10% of examples random and occasional guessing at their own yes rate. They also take it that the program shows distinct numbers, so each further example is a draw from the concept's members not yet shown (strong sampling without replacement). The one change is this sampling assumption. It sharpens the size principle most for small concepts that are nearly used up by the examples (e.g. 8, 64 or 16, 8, 64 strongly favour "powers of 2", or 1, 8, 27 "cubes"), so people say "yes" less often to members of broader rules and wide ranges than a with-replacement learner predicts.

## counting_by_twos_run_concepts  — rank 5, 2.4 ± 2.7 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1934.1

Refinement of `rule_breadth_prior_concepts`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules (with their size-graded rule prior) and every interval of 1-100, with the size principle, 10% of examples random and occasional guessing. They also entertain one more kind of concept, "counting by twos" runs: a bounded stretch of every other number, such as 60, 62, 64, 66 or 31, 33, 35, i.e. the even or the odd numbers within a limited range. All such runs are equally likely a priori, and the family as a whole takes an inferred share of prior belief relative to the rules. This predicts that examples spaced by two (e.g. 60, 62, 64) draw "yes" to nearby same-parity numbers but not to same-parity numbers far away, a pattern that neither "even numbers" nor a numeric range gives.

## shown_examples_not_repeated  — rank 6, 3.4 ± 1.0 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1935.1

Refinement of `concepts_sampled_without_replacement` (the incumbent): people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every interval of 1-100, with the size-graded rule prior, 10% of examples random, occasional guessing at their own yes rate, and the belief that the program shows distinct numbers, so each further example is a draw from the concept's members not yet shown. The one change follows from that same belief: when the number asked about is one of the examples already shown, people sometimes (at a rate inferred from their answers) read "would the program also produce it?" as asking whether it would come up again as a new example, and since the program does not repeat itself they answer "no". This predicts fewer "yes" answers to targets that are themselves examples than the concept's membership implies (about two-thirds rather than three-quarters), while leaving every other judgment governed by the incumbent's concept learning and making the guessing lapse smaller.

## round_number_salient_sampling  — rank 7, 4.9 ± 2.5 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1936.5

People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules (with a size-graded rule prior) and every interval of 1-100, with occasional guessing, treating the examples as distinct draws from the hidden concept (strong sampling without replacement, with some stray random examples). The one new claim is about how they believe the program picks numbers: not uniformly, but with round numbers (multiples of 5 and 10) more or less likely to be produced than other numbers, with the strength and direction of this round-number bias inferred from their answers. Because a concept's members are then weighted by how readily the program would emit them, this predicts that concepts made mostly of round numbers (e.g. "multiples of 10", "ends in 0" or "ends in 5") gain or lose credibility relative to other rules and ranges. It also predicts that a round example says less about the concept than a non-round one does, or more.

## narrow_range_specificity_bias  — no comparison row

People infer the hidden concept by Bayesian inference over arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges. They read the examples as distinct numbers drawn from the concept, allow that some are stray, and sometimes guess. The one distortion is a specificity bias in how they think about ranges: before seeing any examples, they already judge a narrow stretch of numbers a likelier program than a wide one, so a range's prior plausibility falls (or rises) as a power of its width. The rules themselves keep an equal prior. This predicts tighter "yes" gradients around clustered examples (e.g. 60, 62, 64) and fewer "yes" answers far beyond the examples' span than an unbiased learner gives, while generalization by rules (e.g. powers of 2 after 16, 8, 64) is left unchanged.

## pure_rule_induction_no_ranges  — no comparison row

People treat the task purely as rule induction: they weigh only the arithmetic and digit rules (odd, even, primes, squares, powers, multiples, "ends in d", "contains d") by how well each explains the distinct examples shown (the size principle, sampling without replacement), and when no rule fits they fall back on "the program produces any number at all", with occasional guessing. They never entertain numeric ranges, so this predicts no graded magnitude generalization: a target near the examples (e.g. 61 after 60, 62, 64) is no more likely to get a "yes" than a distant number that fits the same rules.

## position_graded_example_trust  — no comparison row

People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges as the best model (equal rule prior, distinct examples drawn without replacement, occasional guessing), but they read the examples in order and their trust in each one shifts steadily along the sequence: the chance they put on an example being a stray random number rises or falls by a fixed step with each later position (recency or primacy of trust), with the average stray rate unchanged. This predicts that the same misfitting example is discounted more readily when it comes at the distrusted end of the sequence (e.g. 47 after 14 versus 14 after 47), so tight rules survive an odd-one-out example in one position but give way to broad concepts in the other.
