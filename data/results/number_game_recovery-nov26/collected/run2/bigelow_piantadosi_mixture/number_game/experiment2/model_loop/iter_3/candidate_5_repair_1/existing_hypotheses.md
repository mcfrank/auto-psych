# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## digit_sum_open_ended_range_discount  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -1928.2

Refinement of `digit_sum_concepts` (the incumbent): people do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules, the 18 "digits add up to s" concepts and every numeric range of 1-100, reading the examples as distinct draws with some strays, sometimes guessing, and favouring narrow ranges. The one change, the component taken from `open_ended_range_discount`, is that a range running to an end of the number line ("anything up to 30", "anything from 70 on") is a threshold rule rather than a bounded stretch, and people hold such open-ended ranges at their own prior weight relative to bounded ones (inferred; the data suggest lower). This predicts fewer "yes" answers to numbers far below small examples or far above large ones, all the way to 1 or 100, while digit-sum and rule generalization and "yes" to numbers just beyond the examples' span are kept.

## heading_direction_range_extrapolation  — rank 1, 0.6 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1928.7

People read the examples as a sequence and take the direction of the last step (whether the final example went up or down from the one before it) as the direction the program is heading, so the numeric ranges that come to mind as candidate concepts extend further past the examples in that direction and less in the other; otherwise they do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic, digit and digit-sum rules and ranges as the best model. This predicts more "yes" answers to numbers far beyond the examples on the side the sequence was last moving toward (e.g. above 60 after 40, 20, 60) and fewer on the side it moved away from, while rule-based generalization and sets of a single example are unchanged.

## sequence_direction_range_extrapolation  — rank 6, 3.0 ± 1.8 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1931.2

## heterogeneous_range_specificity_bias  — rank 7, 3.2 ± 2.2 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1931.4

Refinement of `narrow_range_specificity_bias` (the incumbent): people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules (equal prior) and every numeric range of 1-100, reading the examples as distinct draws with some strays and sometimes guessing, and each person's prior over ranges rises or falls as a power of the range's width. The one change is that the strength of this specificity bias differs from person to person: the population holds a spread of width biases, with some people strongly preferring narrow ranges and others preferring wide ones, and the spread is inferred from the answers, where the incumbent assumes a single shared bias. This predicts the extra disagreement between respondents on items of moderate confidence, such as targets just beyond a cluster like 60, 62, 64, where narrow-range people say "no" and wide-range people say "yes". It also predicts a population "yes" gradient that is sharp near the examples but has longer tails than any single shared bias gives, while generalization by rules is left unchanged.

## narrow_range_specificity_bias  — rank 8, 3.2 ± 2.5 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1931.4

People infer the hidden concept by Bayesian inference over arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges. They read the examples as distinct numbers drawn from the concept, allow that some are stray, and sometimes guess. The one distortion is a specificity bias in how they think about ranges: before seeing any examples, they already judge a narrow stretch of numbers a likelier program than a wide one, so a range's prior plausibility falls (or rises) as a power of its width. The rules themselves keep an equal prior. This predicts tighter "yes" gradients around clustered examples (e.g. 60, 62, 64) and fewer "yes" answers far beyond the examples' span than an unbiased learner gives, while generalization by rules (e.g. powers of 2 after 16, 8, 64) is left unchanged.

## without_replacement_size_principle  — rank 9, 3.5 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1931.7

Refinement of `equal_prior_concepts_with_guess_lapse`: people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every interval of 1-100 (equal rule prior, 10% of examples random, occasional guessing at their own yes rate), but they understand that the program shows distinct examples, so each further example is read as a draw from the concept's members not yet shown (sampling without replacement) rather than from all its members again. The one change is this without-replacement size principle, which adds no free parameter and predicts that several examples sharpen generalization toward small concepts more than the standard model does: a few examples that nearly exhaust a small rule (e.g. 16, 8, 64 for powers of 2) or a short range make that concept far more credible than broader alternatives.

## position_graded_example_trust  — rank 10, 3.9 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1932.1

People do Bayesian concept learning over the same arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges as the best model (equal rule prior, distinct examples drawn without replacement, occasional guessing), but they read the examples in order and their trust in each one shifts steadily along the sequence: the chance they put on an example being a stray random number rises or falls by a fixed step with each later position (recency or primacy of trust), with the average stray rate unchanged. This predicts that the same misfitting example is discounted more readily when it comes at the distrusted end of the sequence (e.g. 47 after 14 versus 14 after 47), so tight rules survive an odd-one-out example in one position but give way to broad concepts in the other.

## relative_width_range_prior  — rank 11, 4.1 ± 2.4 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1932.3

Refinement of `without_replacement_size_principle` (the incumbent): people do Bayesian concept learning over the same 45 arithmetic and digit rules and every numeric range of 1-100, reading the examples as distinct draws (sampling without replacement), with 10% stray examples and occasional guessing at their own yes rate. The one change is a Weber-like sense of how narrow a range is: before seeing examples, people judge a range more (or less) plausible as a power of its width *relative to its magnitude* (width divided by the range's midpoint), so "60 to 64" counts as a tight, specific range while "2 to 6", equally wide in absolute terms, counts as a broad one. This predicts sharper, more local "yes" gradients around clustered large examples (e.g. 60, 62, 64 or 83, 77) than around clustered small ones (e.g. 3, 7 or 5, 9), a magnitude dependence that neither the flat range prior nor an absolute-width prior produces.

## sampled_concept_majority_vote  — rank 14, 4.7 ± 3.2 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1932.9

People infer the hidden concept exactly as the simplest current model does (Bayesian concept learning over arithmetic and digit rules and numeric ranges, distinct examples, some strays, occasional guessing), but they do not answer by consulting their whole belief: they call to mind a small number of candidate concepts, each drawn in proportion to its posterior plausibility, and answer "yes" when most of those sampled concepts contain the target (a majority vote over a few posterior samples). How many concepts they sample is inferred from their answers; sampling one is pure probability matching, while sampling more pushes moderately likely targets toward a firm "yes" or "no", so "yes" rates should be more extreme than the posterior on items the posterior calls fairly likely or fairly unlikely.

## pure_rule_induction_no_ranges  — rank 23, 454.7 ± 31.8 nats behind the best (14.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2382.9

People treat the task purely as rule induction: they weigh only the arithmetic and digit rules (odd, even, primes, squares, powers, multiples, "ends in d", "contains d") by how well each explains the distinct examples shown (the size principle, sampling without replacement), and when no rule fits they fall back on "the program produces any number at all", with occasional guessing. They never entertain numeric ranges, so this predicts no graded magnitude generalization: a target near the examples (e.g. 61 after 60, 62, 64) is no more likely to get a "yes" than a distant number that fits the same rules.

## set_common_feature_contrast  — rank 24, 630.1 ± 35.9 nats behind the best (17.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2558.3

People judge the target by its feature-contrast similarity to the example set as a whole: they notice the properties every example shares (parity, final digit, a contained digit, being a square, power or multiple of something), and the target counts as similar to the extent it keeps those shared properties and dissimilar to the extent it lacks them, with rarer, more distinctive shared properties (e.g. "power of 2") weighing far more than common ones (e.g. "even"); on magnitude, similarity falls off steadily with how far the target lies outside the stretch the examples span. The more similar on this metric, the more likely a "yes" — there is no weighing of alternative hypotheses, only matching against the set's common features.

## multiplicative_exemplar_resemblance  — rank 25, 1007.1 ± 44.2 nats behind the best (22.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2935.3

People do not infer one concept for the whole set; they store each example and compare the target to every stored example in turn, judging each pairwise resemblance as how likely the two numbers are to share an arithmetic or digit rule (e.g. "powers of 2", "contains a 3") or a numeric range, so sharing a narrow property counts for more than sharing a broad one. These resemblances combine multiplicatively, as a geometric mean over the examples (a conjunctive exemplar rule: the target must resemble every example, and one poor match drags the whole judgment down), and the greater this overall resemblance, the more likely a "yes". A target that matches only some of the examples (e.g. 17 after 16, 8, 64) is therefore strongly rejected, unlike in an additive exemplar model.

## spread_coverage_expectation  — no comparison row

## limited_concept_consideration_set  — no comparison row

People do not weigh every conceivable concept: they bring to mind a limited sample of candidate concepts (arithmetic and digit rules, digit-sum concepts, numeric ranges), each coming to mind in proportion to how plausible it seems before any examples, and then judge the target only by those candidates that came to mind, weighted by how well they explain the examples, as in the best model. Because a simple, a priori plausible rule is almost always among the candidates however plausible it is, the advantage of plausible concepts saturates, so relative to full Bayesian weighing this predicts somewhat more "yes" by low-prior concepts such as a particular narrow range or a digit-sum family and less by broad rules, with the size of the consideration set inferred from the answers.
