# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## individual_range_sampling_looseness  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3512.2

Refinement of `loose_range_sampling_sparse_rules`: people do the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over arithmetic rules (kind plausibilities, sparse familiarities within kinds) and numerical ranges, reading the examples with one sampling strength for rules and a separate, looser one for stretches of the number line. The single change is that how loosely the examples are read as evidence for a tight range is a stable trait of each participant rather than a population constant (each person's range sampling strength is scaled by a personal factor with a fixed spread around the population, inferred from their answers; the rule strength stays shared). Because the examples' range evidence grows with every example, this predicts individual differences that grow with set size: some participants keep to the examples' span and reject far rule members, others favour the fitting rule, consistently across every set each person sees, while for single examples people barely differ.

## loose_range_individual_rule_leaning  — rank 1, 3.3 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3515.4

Refinement of `individual_rule_range_leaning`. People learn the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian weighing of arithmetic rules (sparse rule familiarities within kinds) against numerical ranges, with the same parity-conflict veto, and each participant has a stable personal leaning that raises or lowers the prior odds of every rule against the ranges. The single change, grafted from `loose_range_sampling_sparse_rules`, is that people do not read the examples with one sampling strength for both kinds of explanation: they weigh the evidence that a range is tight on its own, looser scale. So the personal leaning decides between a rule and a less literally sampled range. Participants who lean toward ranges then generalize somewhat beyond the examples' span, instead of stopping sharply at its edges, and as examples accumulate a fitting rule overtakes the tightest covering range faster for everyone.

## loose_range_sampling_sparse_rules  — rank 2, 3.8 ± 4.5 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3516.0

Refinement of `sparse_rule_familiarity_within_kinds`: people do the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over rules (with sparse familiarity within each kind) and numerical ranges. The single change is that people do not apply the size principle equally to both kinds of explanation: they read the examples with one sampling strength when weighing arithmetic rules and with a separate, looser one when weighing stretches of the number line, so the examples count for less as evidence that a range is tight. This predicts that sets no rule fits are generalized somewhat beyond the examples' span, and that as examples accumulate, a fitting rule overtakes the tightest covering range faster than a single shared sampling strength allows.

## pedagogical_example_selection  — rank 3, 9.0 ± 8.8 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3521.2

People infer the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian weighing of arithmetic rules (sparse rule familiarities within kinds) and numerical ranges as the best model, but they do not assume the examples are a random draw from the concept: they assume a helpful teacher picked them, favouring numbers that by themselves would single that concept out (e.g. numbers few other concepts contain, such as 64 for powers of 2 or the numbers near the ends of a range). The examples are therefore read as pointing to the concept that makes them the most telling choices, not just to the smallest concept that contains them. The sharpest disagreement with the best model is in sets made of "telling" numbers, which this account generalizes along the concept those numbers single out, and in sets of numbers that many concepts contain, which it treats as weak evidence and generalizes more broadly than the size principle does.

## without_replacement_example_sampling  — rank 4, 9.5 ± 6.3 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3521.7

People learn the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian weighing of arithmetic rules (each kind with its own plausibility, a few rules standing out as especially familiar or unfamiliar) and stretches of the number line as the best model, but they understand that the examples shown are different numbers drawn from the program one after another without repeats, so each example is judged against the members of a concept not yet shown. This makes a small concept that the examples nearly use up (e.g. 1, 8, 27 as the cubes, or a range only a little wider than the examples) a far more striking explanation than independent draws would make it, so such sets are generalized more narrowly to just the concept's few remaining members, while sets fitting large concepts are judged as before.

## individual_leaning_loose_ranges  — rank 5, 12.3 ± 4.1 nats behind the best (3.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3524.4

Refinement of `loose_range_sampling_sparse_rules`: people do the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over arithmetic rules (sparse familiarities within kinds) and numerical ranges, and they read the examples as more loosely sampled when weighing ranges than when weighing rules. The single change is a component taken from `individual_rule_range_leaning`: participants differ in how readily they reach for a rule rather than a range. Each person has a stable leaning that shifts the prior odds of every rule against the ranges, drawn from a fixed population spread, and an unidentified person is treated as the population average over that spread. The model predicts that the same set is generalized by its rule (yeses to distant rule members) by some participants and by loose proximity (yeses to nearby non-members, even somewhat beyond the examples' span) by others, consistently across every set each person sees.

## individual_rule_range_leaning  — rank 6, 12.4 ± 4.8 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3524.6

Refinement of `parity_veto_sparse_familiar_rules`: people learn the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian weighing of arithmetic rules (sparse rule familiarities within kinds) against numerical ranges, with the same parity-conflict veto, but they differ from one another in how readily they reach for a rule rather than a range — each participant has a stable personal leaning that raises or lowers the prior odds of every rule against the ranges. The single change is this participant-level rule-versus-range leaning (people differ by a fixed spread around the population, each person's leaning inferred from the data), predicting that the same set is generalized by its rule (yeses to distant rule members) by some participants and by proximity (yeses to nearby non-members) by others, consistently across all the sets each person sees.

## parity_veto_sparse_familiar_rules  — rank 7, 13.9 ± 6.0 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3526.1

Refinement of `parity_conflict_decision_veto`: people infer the hidden concept by capacity-limited Bayesian learning over rules (multiples of 5 as its own base-ten kind) and numerical ranges, and a target whose parity matches none of the examples triggers an automatic parity conflict that, on a fixed fraction of such trials, overrides the inferred concept with a "no". The single change is grafting sparse rule familiarity (from `sparse_rule_familiarity_within_kinds`) onto the concept-learning stage: most rules share their kind's plausibility, and only a few stand out as exceptionally familiar (multiples of 11, powers of 10) or unfamiliar (multiples of 7, "contains the digit 1"). The claim is that the decision-stage parity veto survives once rule priors are calibrated this way: opposite-parity targets in all-odd or all-even sets (e.g. odd numbers inside the span of 20, 32, 92) get fewer yeses than the inferred concept implies, while same-parity targets are unaffected.

## sparse_rule_familiarity_within_kinds  — rank 8, 15.4 ± 6.1 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3527.6

Refinement of `rule_familiarity_within_kinds`: people do the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over rules and numerical ranges, with a prior plausibility for each kind of rule and rule-specific familiarities within each kind, but familiarity is sparse: most rules of a kind are about as familiar as one another and simply share the kind's plausibility, while only a few stand out as exceptionally familiar (e.g. multiples of 11, powers of 10) or exceptionally unfamiliar (e.g. multiples of 7, "contains the digit 1"). The single change is replacing the incumbent's evenly spread (normal) familiarities with heavy-tailed ones, which predicts that rules with no standout familiarity are generalized exactly as their kind's other rules are, instead of each being nudged up or down by a moderate amount, while the few exceptional rules keep their large deviations.

## triangular_number_concept  — rank 9, 17.4 ± 6.0 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3529.6

People infer the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over arithmetic rules (each kind of rule with its own plausibility, a few rules standing out as especially familiar or unfamiliar) and numerical ranges as the best model, with the same parity-conflict veto at the answer, but their space of candidate concepts also contains the triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, 36, 45, 55, 66, 78, 91): a well-known figurate sequence, built by adding successive whole numbers, that no multiples, parity, power or digit rule covers. It is its own kind of rule, held a priori with a plausibility inferred from the data. This predicts that sets made of triangular numbers (e.g. 15, 21, 45 or 10, 55, 28) get yeses to distant triangular targets such as 66, 78 or 91, and fewer yeses to non-triangular numbers between the examples, than a learner without this concept would give.

## rule_or_range_kind_commitment  — rank 10, 17.5 ± 6.3 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3529.6

People learn the hidden concept from the examples by the same capacity-limited Bayesian weighing of arithmetic rules and numerical ranges as the best model, but they do not blend the two kinds of explanation when they answer: they first commit to whether the program follows an arithmetic rule or produces a stretch of the number line, by comparing how well each kind as a whole explains the examples more decisively than an ideal learner would, and then judge the target only by the concepts of the kind they chose. This predicts that when rules and ranges both explain the examples moderately well, answers follow whichever kind is ahead more sharply than the blend implies, e.g. fewer yeses to far rule-members when ranges are slightly favored, and fewer yeses to in-span rule-violators when a rule is slightly favored.

## criterion_gated_concept_belief  — rank 11, 83.6 ± 11.7 nats behind the best (7.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3595.8

People infer the hidden concept by the same capacity-limited Bayesian learning over rules (with kind plausibilities and a few exceptionally familiar or unfamiliar rules) and numerical ranges as the simplest current model, but they do not answer by probability matching to their belief. They say "yes" only when their belief that the target belongs to the concept clears an internal criterion of how sure they must be, and this comparison is noisy from trial to trial. This predicts that yes-rates are a steep S-shaped function of belief around the criterion, not a one-for-one copy of it: moderately supported targets on either side of the criterion are answered more unanimously than their belief implies.

## conjunctive_category_exemplar_similarity  — rank 12, 574.8 ± 46.2 nats behind the best (12.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4086.9

People do not infer a hidden concept; they compare the target with each remembered example, judging two numbers similar to the extent that they belong together to the number categories stored in memory (odd, even, primes, squares, powers, multiples, final or contained digit, and stretches of the number line), with small, distinctive shared categories counting more than broad ones. Because the target has to resemble every example, these comparisons combine conjunctively: the more uniformly similar the target is to all the examples (one weak match pulls it down sharply), the more likely people are to say yes.
