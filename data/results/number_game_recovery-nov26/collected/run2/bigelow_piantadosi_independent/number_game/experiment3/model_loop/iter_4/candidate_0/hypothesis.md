People's space of candidate concepts also contains near-misses of the sparse computed sequences: "one more than" or "one less than" a square, a cube, a power, or a prime (e.g. 3, 8, 15, 24, 35 as squares minus one; 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65 as powers of 2 plus one). These form their own kind of rule, held a priori with a plausibility inferred from the data, with the near-misses sharing it equally. Like the other computed rules, people only consider them on the share of judgments where they make the effort to search for computed rules, and they weigh them against ranges and the other rules by the same Bayesian logic. This predicts that sets which sit just off a famous sequence (e.g. 8, 15, 24) get yeses for distant members of the shifted sequence (35, 48, 63) and fewer yeses for in-span numbers that are not in it, where a learner without these concepts falls back on the examples' range.
