People do not infer one concept that covers all the examples together; they store each example as a separate exemplar and judge the target by its average similarity to the stored exemplars. The similarity of the target to one exemplar is Shepard-style generalization through consequential regions: how likely a number concept (an arithmetic or digit rule, or a range of a typical width) that contains that single exemplar also contains the target, with smaller concepts counting more. Because each exemplar is generalized from on its own and the results are summed, a target resembling only some of the examples gets partial "yes", and evidence never tightens jointly across examples as it would under concept inference.
