## Hypotheses

- **new_set_criterion_ratio_ceiling**: I refined the incumbent graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion, grafting the new-set caution of the pruned new_set_cautious_erlang_concepts. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts) and treat the largest example as a soft, ratio-graded ceiling; the one change is that their yes/no criterion also depends on whether the set is new: on the first question after the examples change (or at session start) they demand a different amount of certainty to say "yes" than on a repeated question about the same set, a single log-odds criterion shift whose size and sign are inferred (zero recovers the incumbent). This predicts that, at equal belief, the first query about a new set gets fewer "yes" answers than later queries about that same set, as the posterior-predictive critique of the incumbent suggests.
- **graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion**: I chose above_max_stricter_criterion and refined it. People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and they treat the largest example as a soft ceiling when they turn belief into a yes/no answer. The one change is that the extra certainty they demand is not a fixed step but grows with how far beyond the largest example the target lies, judged as a ratio (Weber-like "how much bigger"), so a target just above the largest example is barely penalised, one twice as large needs much stronger evidence, and the same absolute overshoot counts for more above small examples than above large ones.
- **new_set_criterion_tank_ceiling**: I chose sample_count_scaled_ceiling and refined it. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts) with the largest example as a soft ceiling whose penalty grows with the overshoot ratio and the number of examples shown. The one change, grafted from the pruned new_set_cautious_erlang_concepts but recast as a decision criterion: on the first question after a new set of examples appears, people demand extra certainty before saying "yes" (a log-odds shift of their belief, its size and sign inferred, zero recovering the parent), relaxing to their plain criterion once they have been asked about that set before — so uncertain targets get fewer "yes" answers on a first query than on repeat queries, while near-certain members and non-members barely change.
- **evidence_sufficiency_commitment**: People do the same Bayesian concept learning as graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, the largest example as a ratio-graded soft ceiling), but how firmly they act on that belief depends on how much evidence they were shown: a metacognitive sense of evidence sufficiency scales the log-odds of their belief by a commitment factor that grows (or shrinks) as a power of the number of examples, its exponent inferred and zero recovering the parent. With a positive exponent, after one or two examples people hedge toward 50/50 — saying "yes" to some targets they believe are out and "no" to some they believe are in — and only give crisp yes/no answers once three or four examples are shown.
- **sample_count_scaled_ceiling**: I refined the incumbent graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts) and treat the largest example as a soft ceiling whose extra demanded certainty grows with the ratio by which the target overshoots it. The one change is that this ceiling is inferred like an upper bound from samples ("German tank" reasoning): every example that fell at or below the largest one is further evidence that nothing much larger is produced, so the penalty for overshooting scales with the number of examples shown — a single example sets only a weak ceiling, while four examples all below 20 make 40 strongly implausible.
- **directional_edge_bias_criterion**: I chose above_max_stricter_criterion and refined it. People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and when they turn belief into a yes/no answer the edges of the examples are directional: just past the largest example they demand more certainty to say "yes", and just past the smallest example they demand less. The one change is that the criterion shift is not a fixed step for every target above the largest example but a single signed edge bias, mirrored at both ends and fading over about eight numbers beyond the edge. So at equal belief, people say "yes" more to a target a few numbers below the smallest example than to one a few numbers above the largest, and targets far outside the examples are judged on belief alone.
- **example_retention_limited_memory**: People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, the largest example as a graded soft ceiling), but their working memory for the examples is unreliable: by the time they judge the target, each example has independently been lost with some inferred probability (at least one is always kept), and they generalise only from the examples they still hold, including taking the largest example they remember as their ceiling. Because a forgotten example removes its evidence entirely, this predicts broader, less rule-tight generalisation from larger sets than full-memory inference and partial "yes" to targets that fit only some of the examples.
- **extrapolated_step_ceiling**: I refined the incumbent new_set_criterion_ratio_ceiling. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), with a ratio-graded soft ceiling and a criterion shift on the first question about a new set; the one change is that the ceiling is extrapolated rather than pinned at the largest example: people expect the program could continue past its largest example by an inferred number of the examples' typical proportional steps (how many times larger each sorted example is than the one before), and only targets beyond that extrapolated point make them demand extra certainty (zero steps recovers the incumbent). So widely spread sets (2, 8, 32) license "yes" well above their largest example without penalty, while tightly clustered sets (40, 44, 47) keep their ceiling almost at the largest example.
