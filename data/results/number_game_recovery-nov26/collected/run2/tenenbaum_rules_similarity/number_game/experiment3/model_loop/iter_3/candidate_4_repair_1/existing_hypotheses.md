# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2006.9

I chose above_max_stricter_criterion and refined it. People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and they treat the largest example as a soft ceiling when they turn belief into a yes/no answer. The one change is that the extra certainty they demand is not a fixed step but grows with how far beyond the largest example the target lies, judged as a ratio (Weber-like "how much bigger"), so a target just above the largest example is barely penalised, one twice as large needs much stronger evidence, and the same absolute overshoot counts for more above small examples than above large ones.

## sample_count_scaled_ceiling  — rank 1, 0.5 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2007.5

I refined the incumbent graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts) and treat the largest example as a soft ceiling whose extra demanded certainty grows with the ratio by which the target overshoots it. The one change is that this ceiling is inferred like an upper bound from samples ("German tank" reasoning): every example that fell at or below the largest one is further evidence that nothing much larger is produced, so the penalty for overshooting scales with the number of examples shown — a single example sets only a weak ceiling, while four examples all below 20 make 40 strongly implausible.

## directional_edge_bias_criterion  — rank 2, 0.9 ± 3.7 nats behind the best (0.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2007.8

I chose above_max_stricter_criterion and refined it. People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and when they turn belief into a yes/no answer the edges of the examples are directional: just past the largest example they demand more certainty to say "yes", and just past the smallest example they demand less. The one change is that the criterion shift is not a fixed step for every target above the largest example but a single signed edge bias, mirrored at both ends and fading over about eight numbers beyond the edge. So at equal belief, people say "yes" more to a target a few numbers below the smallest example than to one a few numbers above the largest, and targets far outside the examples are judged on belief alone.

## example_retention_limited_memory  — rank 3, 1.0 ± 0.9 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2008.0

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, the largest example as a graded soft ceiling), but their working memory for the examples is unreliable: by the time they judge the target, each example has independently been lost with some inferred probability (at least one is always kept), and they generalise only from the examples they still hold, including taking the largest example they remember as their ceiling. Because a forgotten example removes its evidence entirely, this predicts broader, less rule-tight generalisation from larger sets than full-memory inference and partial "yes" to targets that fit only some of the examples.

## no_digit_containment_span_concepts  — rank 4, 1.4 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2008.4

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), except that they never entertain "contains the digit d" concepts: a digit that merely appears somewhere in the written numbers (such as a shared tens digit) is not noticed as a pattern. This simpler concept space predicts that near targets get no extra "yes" for sharing an example's decade and that sets like 8, 80, 48 do not generalise to other numbers containing an 8.

## above_max_stricter_criterion  — rank 5, 1.9 ± 1.4 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2008.9

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model, but when they turn their belief into a yes/no answer they demand more certainty for a target larger than every example shown: the largest example acts as a soft ceiling, so going beyond it needs stronger evidence than going below the smallest. This one decision-stage criterion shift (its size inferred, zero recovering the best model) predicts fewer "yes" answers just above the largest example than just below the smallest one, at equal belief that the concept contains the target.

## two_sided_ratio_span_criterion  — rank 6, 2.3 ± 1.3 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2009.2

I refined the incumbent graded_ratio_ceiling_criterion. People do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and when they turn belief into a yes/no answer they judge how far outside the examples' span the target lies as a ratio, both above the largest example and below the smallest. The one change is that the smallest example also acts as a soft, ratio-graded bound, with its own inferred strength and sign (zero recovers the incumbent): the criterion shifts in proportion to how many times smaller the target is than the smallest example, separately from the shift above the largest, so "yes" below the examples can fall off more slowly (or faster) than above them.

## yes_bias_logodds_concepts  — rank 7, 2.4 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2009.3

A refinement of the incumbent no_digit_containment_span_concepts: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), but they do not answer "yes" exactly as often as they believe the concept contains the target: they carry a general response bias, a single shift of the log-odds of their belief (its size and sign inferred, zero recovering the incumbent). This predicts that targets the learner is unsure about (beliefs near 50%) are pushed systematically toward "no" (or "yes"), while targets almost surely in or out of the concept are barely affected.

## prelec_weighted_concept_belief  — rank 8, 2.6 ± 1.8 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2009.5

People do the same normative Bayesian concept learning as no_digit_containment_span_concepts (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), but they distort their belief through a prospect-theory probability-weighting function when answering: small beliefs that the target belongs are overweighted and near-certain beliefs underweighted (an inverse-S Prelec curve whose curvature is inferred, one recovering calibrated answers). Unlike a symmetric confidence scaling of log-odds, this distortion is asymmetric, so near-impossible targets get noticeably more "yes" while near-certain ones lose only a little, and the crossover sits below 50% belief.

## pattern_or_random_guessing  — rank 9, 2.7 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2009.6

Before generalising, people first ask whether the examples follow any pattern at all, or whether the program could just be producing arbitrary numbers. They weigh "no pattern" against the concepts they know (the same arithmetic and final-digit rules, rule stretches and ranges as the current models) by how well each explains the examples, and to the degree they conclude there is no pattern they stop using concepts and guess at their own fixed yes-rate. So short or weakly patterned sets (one or two examples, or examples sharing only a broad property like being even) get noisy, middling answers to every target, including the examples themselves, while sets with a striking pattern (powers of 2, squares) get crisp yes/no answers.

## off_by_one_sequence_concepts  — rank 10, 4.7 ± 2.1 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2011.7

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), but they also entertain "near-miss" sequences: one more or one less than the members of a familiar sparse sequence (squares, cubes, powers of 2 to 10), such as 2, 5, 10, 17, 26 (squares plus one) or 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63 (powers of two minus one). Each such shifted sequence is equally likely a priori, the family taking an inferred share of the rule prior, so sets like 31, 3, 1, 15 or 33, 17, 5, 9 generalise sharply to the other members of the shifted sequence (7, 63 or 65) and not to broad rules such as odd numbers.

## gaussian_cluster_magnitude_concepts  — rank 11, 99.1 ± 15.5 nats behind the best (6.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2106.1

People infer arithmetic and final-digit rules (and stretches of them) by the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model, but when the examples suggest a "region of magnitudes" they do not picture a sharp-edged range: they picture a fuzzy cluster around a central number, from which the program emits numbers more often the closer they are to the centre (with a spread on a scale they have learned to expect), and they judge a target's membership by how typical it is of that cluster, which fades smoothly with its distance from the centre. So, for examples that fit no rule, "yes" is strongest near the middle of the examples, weaker at and just beyond the outermost examples, and fades gradually with no sharp cut-off at the edges of the examples' span.

## digit_grid_block_concepts  — rank 12, 245.8 ± 30.0 nats behind the best (8.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2252.7

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, a preferred typical concept size), but they do not picture "ranges" as stretches of the number line: they picture each number by its two written digits, laid out on a decades-by-final-digits grid, and their magnitude concepts are rectangular blocks of that grid — a run of decades combined with a run of final digits (e.g. "the 40s to 60s ending in 3 to 5", or a whole decade such as "the 30s"). The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives on examples that fit no rule but share a final-digit band across decades: 44, 64 should generalise to 54 (and 34 or 74) far more than to 45 or 47, which sit closer on the number line, and a stretch such as 38-42 that crosses a decade line is a priori an unnatural concept.

## feature_prototype_typicality  — rank 13, 1216.6 ± 58.0 nats behind the best (21.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3223.5

People do not infer a rule; they abstract a prototype of the example set — a summary of how often each familiar number property (odd/even, prime, square, cube, power, multiple of 3-12) and each final digit occurs among the examples, plus the examples' average magnitude and spread — with each property tracked independently and the estimates hedged toward how common the property is among all numbers when there are few examples. They say "yes" to the degree that the target looks more typical of this prototype than of numbers in general (the summed property-by-property and magnitude likelihood ratio), so a target lacking a property every example shares is rejected more firmly the more examples showed it, and a target near the examples' centre is favoured whatever rule it fits.

## last_example_ratio_ceiling  — no comparison row

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the best model (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), and when they turn belief into a yes/no answer they use a magnitude anchor as a soft ceiling whose demanded extra certainty grows with the ratio by which the target overshoots it. The one change is that the anchor is not the largest example but the most recently shown one (recency anchoring): the last example read sets the magnitude reference, so the same set in a different order generalises differently, and targets larger than the last example are penalised even when an earlier example was larger still.

## nearest_example_ratio_similarity  — no comparison row

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best models (arithmetic and final-digit rules plus multiples of 13-50, rule runs confined to a stretch of the number line, ranges of a typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement, no "contains digit d" concepts), but their answer is also pulled by magnitude similarity: the further the target lies from its nearest example on a ratio (Weber-like, logarithmic) scale, the more certainty they demand before saying "yes", with the demand growing in proportion to that log-ratio distance. Unlike a ceiling at the largest example, this similarity gradient is symmetric and applies everywhere — above the largest example, below the smallest, and in wide gaps between examples — so at equal belief a target close in proportion to some example (e.g. 9 after 8) gets more "yes" than one in a large proportional gap (e.g. 30 after 8 and 64).
