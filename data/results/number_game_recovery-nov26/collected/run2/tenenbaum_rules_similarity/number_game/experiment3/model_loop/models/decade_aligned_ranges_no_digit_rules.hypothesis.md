I chose decade_aligned_range_concepts and refined it. People think of ranges of magnitude in round, decade-aligned terms ("the 30s", "1 to 20", "60 to 100"), so a range whose ends fall on decade boundaries (starting at 1, 11, 21, ... or ending at 10, 20, ..., 100) is a priori more plausible than an equally wide range with arbitrary ends, with the strength of this preference inferred (zero recovering the current best model). The one change is the concept learner underneath: the old one also entertained "contains the digit d" rules, which already gave a shared tens digit its own concept and so masked any decade effect; it is replaced by the current best learner (no digit-containment rules, span-restricted rule runs, multiples of 13-50, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), so here the decade structure of magnitude ranges alone predicts that "yes" around examples that fit no rule extends to the edges of their decade and stops sharply at decade lines (e.g. 32 and 37 generalise to 30-39 more than to 28 or 41), without extending to far numbers that merely contain the same digit.
