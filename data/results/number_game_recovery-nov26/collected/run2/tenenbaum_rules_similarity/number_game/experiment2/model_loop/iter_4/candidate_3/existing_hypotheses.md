# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## span_restricted_rule_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -1698.5

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), but they also entertain rules confined to a stretch of the number line — "even numbers from 42 to 92", "numbers ending in 4 from 64 up" — i.e. any unbroken run of consecutive members of a familiar rule, every such run equally likely a priori and the family taking an inferred share of the rule prior. Because such a run is smaller than the whole rule, the size principle favours it when rule-following examples are clustered in magnitude, predicting "yes" to rule members inside or near the examples' span and fewer "yes" answers to members of the same rule far from them (e.g. 52 but not 12 after 72, 92, 82, 42).

## extended_multiples_concepts  — rank 1, 0.2 ± 1.6 nats behind the best (0.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1698.7

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn from the concept without replacement), but their space of arithmetic concepts is larger: besides the familiar multiples of 3-12, they consider "multiples of m" for any m from 13 to 50 (multiples of 15, 20, 25, 45, ...), each such less-familiar multiples rule equally likely a priori and the family as a whole taking an inferred share of the rule prior. Because these rules are small, the size principle makes them dominate when every example is a multiple of a larger number (e.g. 45, 90 or 25, 75, 100), predicting fewer "yes" answers to targets that are only multiples of the smaller familiar factor (e.g. 27 or 60 after 90, 45).

## without_replacement_example_sampling  — rank 2, 0.8 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1699.3

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples), but they assume the program never produces the same number twice, so they treat the examples as drawn from the concept without replacement: each further example that fits a concept is taken as one of that concept's members not yet shown. This makes several examples far stronger evidence for a concept that has barely more members than were shown (cubes, powers of 3 or 4, squares, a tight range) than the best model's with-replacement size principle allows, so the sharpest disagreement lives on three- and four-example sets that nearly exhaust a small rule or range, where this model predicts fewer "yes" answers to targets outside that small concept.

## noisy_magnitude_range_membership  — rank 3, 1.5 ± 1.9 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1699.9

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), but when they check whether the target falls inside a range of magnitudes they consult an imprecise, analog sense of how big the target is, whose noise grows in proportion to the number's size (scalar variability, as in the approximate number system), while arithmetic and digit rules are still checked exactly on the numeral. This resource limit predicts that "yes" fades gradually rather than stopping sharply at the edges of a range, and fades over a wider band around large examples than around small ones, without regard to decade boundaries.

## large_number_biased_sampling  — rank 4, 1.6 ± 2.0 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1700.1

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken without replacement), but they believe the program does not pick its outputs evenly from its concept: it produces larger members more often than smaller ones (output chance growing as a power of the number, the power inferred, zero recovering the best model). Because small numbers then add little to how many outputs a concept "spends" elsewhere, a range stretched downward toward small numbers costs little under the size principle while one stretched upward costs much, so the sharpest disagreement with the best model lives just outside sets of examples that fit no rule, where this model predicts "yes" to targets 1-15 below the smallest example far more than to targets 1-15 above the largest — through the sampling assumption alone, with no new concepts added.

## range_specific_evidence_weighting  — rank 5, 2.0 ± 3.2 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1700.5

A refinement of without_replacement_example_sampling: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken without replacement), but they weigh the examples' evidence differently for the two kinds of concept — the evidence for an arithmetic or digit rule is used at full strength, while the evidence for a range of magnitudes is weighted by its own inferred power (below one: range generalisation is a looser, similarity-like judgement that tightens slowly as examples accumulate; above one: ranges are pinned down faster). The one change is this range-specific evidence weight, which predicts that for examples fitting no rule "yes" spreads around the examples more (or less) than calibrated inference implies, while generalisation within rule-like sets is unchanged.

## conservative_evidence_weighting  — rank 6, 2.1 ± 3.4 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1700.6

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn from the concept without replacement), but they are conservative updaters: they under-weight (or over-weight) the evidence of the examples relative to their prior beliefs about which concepts are plausible, as if the examples' likelihood were raised to a single power below (or above) one. This predicts that generalisation tightens toward the smallest consistent concept more slowly (or faster) as examples accumulate than calibrated inference implies, while the relative plausibility of concepts before any evidence is unchanged.

## strong_weak_sampling_mixture  — rank 7, 2.7 ± 3.0 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1701.2

A refinement of without_replacement_example_sampling: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken from the concept without replacement), but they are unsure how the program chose its examples — whether it picked them from the concept itself (strong sampling, which makes small concepts much more likely) or picked numbers at random and showed the ones that happened to fit (weak sampling, where a concept's size does not matter). The one change is this inferred mixture of strong and weak sampling, which weakens the size principle — so people keep generalising to broader concepts such as "even numbers" after several examples of a narrow one — without making them any more willing to keep a concept that excludes one of the examples.

## guessing_lapse_without_replacement  — rank 8, 2.9 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1701.4

A refinement of without_replacement_example_sampling: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken as drawn from the concept without replacement), but on some share of trials they do not consult their inference at all and simply guess, saying "yes" at their own fixed rate. The one change is this response-stage guessing lapse (grafted from bayesian_concepts_with_guessing_lapse), which claims that "no" answers to the shown examples and "yes" answers to far-off targets come partly from inattentive guessing rather than only from believing that some examples were random, so the inferred share of random examples need not carry both and generalisation from the examples can be sharper.

## first_example_trust_concepts  — rank 9, 2.9 ± 3.6 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1701.4

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, examples drawn without replacement, an inferred share of random examples), but they trust the first example more than the later ones: the first number shown is taken as the one most surely produced by the concept, while later examples are more readily dismissed as random outputs. The one change is that the chance an example is random is smaller for the first example (by an inferred factor) than for later ones, so concepts that exclude the first example are penalised more than concepts that exclude a later example, and the same set in a different order generalises differently.

## count_from_one_ceiling_concepts  — rank 10, 3.0 ± 2.3 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1701.4

People picture the program as a counter that produces numbers from 1 up to some ceiling, so besides the arithmetic and digit rules and ranges of a typical width, they entertain "every number from 1 up to k" (anchored at the start of the number line, any ceiling equally likely a priori, the family taking an inferred share of the prior) and infer the ceiling from the examples by the size principle, as in estimating a maximum from samples. Because such concepts always contain every number below the examples but none above the largest, this predicts that people extend "yes" downward toward small numbers more readily than upward beyond the largest example, an asymmetry that symmetric ranges and rules cannot produce.

## serial_position_attention_gradient  — rank 11, 3.8 ± 2.8 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1702.2

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), but their attention changes as they read the examples one by one, so each example's evidence is weighted by its serial position along a single inferred gradient — falling across the list (primacy: the first examples, read most carefully, count most) or rising (recency) — with the total amount of evidence held fixed. This process-level claim predicts that the same set shown in a different order generalises differently: concepts that fit the attended-to end of the list but exclude an example at the neglected end keep substantial weight.

## digit_rule_evidence_weighting  — rank 12, 4.1 ± 5.1 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1702.5

A refinement of extended_multiples_concepts: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken without replacement), but they weigh the examples' evidence for rules about how a number is written (ends in digit d, contains digit d) by its own inferred power, while evidence for arithmetic rules and ranges is used at full strength. The one change is this digit-rule-specific evidence weight: below one, a shared written digit (such as examples all in the 30s, which all contain a 3) is a weaker coincidence to people than the size principle implies, so "yes" to nearby targets depends less on whether they share the examples' decade than the incumbent predicts.

## odd_multiples_conjunction_concepts  — rank 13, 5.8 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1704.2

A refinement of extended_multiples_concepts: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules plus multiples of 13-50, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples drawn without replacement), but they also form conjunctive "odd multiples of m" concepts (odd multiples of 3: 3, 9, 15, 21, ...; of 7, 9, 11, 15, ...) by combining parity with divisibility. The one change is this added family, each member equally likely a priori and the family taking an inferred share of the rule prior; because these concepts are about half the size of "multiples of m", the size principle makes them win when every example is an odd multiple (e.g. 69, 9, 39, 21 or 63, 27, 99, 33), predicting fewer "yes" answers to even multiples of m (12, 30, 54) than the incumbent does.

## log_width_without_replacement  — rank 14, 7.9 ± 4.0 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -1706.3

I chose log_scale_interval_width_prior and refined it. People judge how wide a "range" concept is on a compressed, logarithmic mental number line, so their prior expects a typical range to be narrow among small numbers (e.g. 3-6) and wide among large ones (e.g. 60-100); the one change is the concept learner underneath, which is replaced by the current best one (45 independently weighted rules, an inferred share of random examples, and examples taken as drawn from the concept without replacement) in place of the old fixed-10%-noise, with-replacement learner. This predicts that, for examples that fit no rule, "yes" extends much further around large examples than around small ones, and further below a cluster of large examples than the linear-width prior of the best model allows.

## stray_count_per_set_noise  — rank 15, 18.9 ± 7.0 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1717.3

I chose the incumbent, without_replacement_example_sampling, and refined how it believes random examples arise: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, in-concept examples drawn without replacement), but they think of a stray example as an occasional slip of the program per set of examples, not as an independent per-example coin flip — the chance that a set contains one, two or more random numbers falls off geometrically with their count (rate inferred) and does not grow with how many examples were shown, and any given count of strays is equally likely to be any of the examples. This predicts that with one or two examples a concept excluding an example is kept about as readily as with four, so the odd example out of a long, otherwise regular set (such as 25 in 91, 25, 61, 31) is judged a stray less readily than independent per-example noise implies, while short sets are discounted more readily.

## single_outlier_concept_learning  — rank 16, 69.8 ± 27.4 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1768.2

A refinement of without_replacement_example_sampling: people do the same Bayesian concept learning (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, examples taken from the concept without replacement), but they do not think each example has its own independent chance of being random. They think a set holds at most one stray example: with some inferred probability one of the examples, any of them equally, is a random number, and otherwise every example came from the concept. The one change is this at-most-one-outlier view of noise, which predicts that people forgive a single example that breaks an otherwise clean pattern even in a four-example set, but completely reject any concept that leaves out two or more of the examples, where the incumbent still gives such concepts some weight.

## predicted_output_threshold  — rank 17, 145.0 ± 18.9 nats behind the best (7.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -1843.5

People do the same Bayesian concept learning as the current best model (45 independently weighted rules, ranges of 1-100 with a preferred typical width, an inferred share of random examples, examples taken as drawn without replacement), but their decision rule is not "is the target in the concept?": they ask how probable it is that the program would output this very number, and answer "yes" when that predicted output probability clears an internal threshold (with noise in the comparison). Because a member of a small concept is a far more probable output than a member of a big one, this predicts that, at the same belief that the target belongs to the concept, targets supported by narrow concepts (powers, cubes, tight ranges) get more "yes" than targets supported only by broad ones (even numbers, wide ranges).

## most_specific_concept_only  — rank 18, 573.4 ± 37.1 nats behind the best (15.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2271.9

People do not weigh many candidate concepts; they adopt the single most specific concept that covers all the examples — the smallest of the arithmetic or digit rules (odd, primes, squares, powers, multiples, final digit, contained digit) or the tightest range spanning the examples — and answer "yes" when the target belongs to that concept and "no" otherwise, apart from a constant rate of response noise. This minimal-generalization strategy predicts no graded size-principle averaging: a single example generalizes to almost nothing, and "yes" falls off sharply at the edge of the one concept chosen.

## distinctive_common_feature_similarity  — rank 19, 822.1 ± 42.1 nats behind the best (19.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2520.5

People judge the target by its similarity to the example set as a whole, using a feature-based (Tversky-style common-features) metric: each arithmetic or digit property the target shares with the examples (parity, multiples, powers, squares, final digit, contained digit, and so on) adds to similarity in proportion to how consistently the examples share it and how distinctive (rare among 1-100) the property is, and closeness on the number line to the span of the examples adds a magnitude-band match that decays with distance. The probability of "yes" rises smoothly with this summed similarity; there is no inference over which single concept produced the examples, so a target that shares a distinctive property with most but not all examples still gets substantial "yes".
