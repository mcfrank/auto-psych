# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## round_number_kinship_exemplar  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -2705.1

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, multiples and factor-kin of an example, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but they also treat round numbers as a familiar class of their own: a target that is a multiple of ten resembles every example ending in 0 or 5 by a fixed amount, at any distance and whatever its final digit. So round targets are endorsed well above what magnitude and digit similarity allow when the examples include numbers ending in 0 or 5 (100 after "5", 50 after "45, 90"), while targets ending in 5, and round targets after examples with no 0 or 5 ending, get no such boost (30 after "63, 27, 99, 33").

## times_table_averaging_exemplar  — rank 1, 3.2 ± 10.2 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2708.3

Refinement of `times_table_multiple_route` (multiples route only for times-table examples 2-12), with one component grafted from `similarity_averaging_exemplar`: people pool their evidence across examples by (partly) averaging rather than summing it. A target's support — its similarity to each example (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences) plus the times-table multiples route — is diluted as more examples are shown, not accumulated. So a far target is not carried by adding up several weak resemblances in multi-example sets like "25, 82" or "50, 2", while lone examples like "7" or "5" keep generalizing broadly to their times-table multiples (14, 49, 98; 30, 100) and multiples of large examples (94 after "47") get no boost.

## one_digit_table_factor_kin_route  — rank 2, 10.3 ± 7.9 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2715.4

Refinement of the incumbent `factor_kin_multiple_route`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples: magnitude, final digit, parity and divisibility by the set's common divisor, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence. A target that shares a factor of 3 or more with an example still gets a small similarity boost at any distance. The one change is to the multiples route. It is retrieved one-digit times-table knowledge, so only one-digit examples (2 to 9) give their multiples a distance-free boost (14, 49 or 98 after "7"; 64 after "4"). Far multiples of two-digit examples (94 after "47", 62 after "31", 75 after "25") get no multiples boost and are judged by ordinary similarity and factor-kinship alone.

## one_digit_arithmetic_route  — rank 3, 10.5 ± 7.9 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2715.6

Refinement of the incumbent `factor_kin_multiple_route`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), with two distance-free arithmetic routes: being a multiple of an example, and sharing a factor of 3 or more with it. The one change is that both arithmetic routes are retrieved times-table knowledge about one-digit numbers, so an example of 10 or more gives both routes a much weaker boost by one common factor: after "7" or "6" people endorse 49, 98 or 9, but after "47" or "15" far targets like 94, 12 or 27 get little arithmetic support and are judged mostly by ordinary similarity.

## problem_size_multiple_retrieval  — rank 4, 12.1 ± 9.9 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2717.2

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus a weaker route for factor-kin), and being a multiple of an example gives a distance-free route to a yes only to the extent that the multiplication fact linking them ("7 × 2 = 14") is retrieved from memory. Retrieval of arithmetic facts gets harder as the problem gets bigger (the problem-size effect), so the route weakens steadily with the sum of the two factors: 14 after "7" or 8 after "4" are readily endorsed, 98 after "7" less so, and 94 after "47" or 62 after "31" gain almost nothing, even though each is a small multiple.

Refinement of `multiple_route_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, divisibility by the set's common divisor, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence). Being a multiple of an example is still a distance-free route to similarity. The one change is that this arithmetic route is broader than exact multiples. ## times_table_multiple_route_2  — rank 7, 15.7 ± 8.8 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2720.8

Refinement of the incumbent `multiple_route_exemplar`: people still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences), and being a multiple of an example is still a separate route to a yes that works at any distance. The one change is that this route comes from memorised times tables. So it is strong for one-digit examples (14, 49 or 98 after "7"; 64 after "4") and much weaker for two-digit examples, whose multiples are not familiar facts (94 after "47", 62 after "31"). ## multiple_route_exemplar  — rank 8, 16.2 ± 8.5 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -2721.3

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (closeness in magnitude, same final digit, same parity, and whether it divides by the set's common divisor), sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence. On top of that there is a second, separate way for a target to resemble an example: being one of its multiples (14, 49 or 98 after "7"; 64 after "4"). This multiples route adds a fixed amount of similarity no matter how far apart the two numbers are, instead of only scaling down closeness. So multiples of an example that are far from it and share none of its digits are still often endorsed, especially after single examples and small-number examples, where magnitude similarity alone predicts almost certain rejection.

## common_divisor_membership_route  — rank 9, 22.2 ± 8.0 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2727.3

Refinement of the incumbent `common_divisor_sharpened_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, and a penalty for not being a multiple of the set's common divisor), sharpened on every dimension when the examples share a suspicious coincidence. The one change is that the set's common divisor of 3 or more (for a single example, the example itself) is also read as a rule, "multiples of that number", which gives the targets it covers a second, distance-free route to a yes: being a member adds a fixed amount to the target's summed similarity, rather than only sparing it a penalty. So multiples of the set's divisor that are far from every example and share none of their digits are often endorsed (e.g. 30 after "5", 14 or 70 after "28, 98", 39 after "63, 27, 99, 33"), while multiples of a single example in a set without that common divisor get no boost.

## set_size_diluted_multiple_route  — rank 10, 29.4 ± 12.0 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2734.5

Refinement of `multiple_route_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity and divisibility by the set's common divisor, sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), and being a multiple of an example is still a separate route to similarity that works at any distance. The one change is that this route is something people take mostly from a lone example: each further example shown weakens it by a constant factor, because with more examples people judge from what the examples have in common rather than from one example's multiples. ## similarity_averaging_exemplar  — rank 11, 30.0 ± 13.9 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2735.1

Refinement of the incumbent `common_divisor_sharpened_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, and the set's common divisor), sharpened on every dimension when the examples share a suspicious coincidence. The one change is how evidence from several examples is pooled: people do not add up the similarity to every example, they (partly) average it, so each further example dilutes rather than adds to a target's support, and a target is judged by how well it fits the examples on average rather than how many it resembles. This predicts that adding examples does not make dissimilar targets more acceptable, that a far target in a sparse pair like "25, 82" is not carried by summing two weak similarities, and that single examples generalize relatively more broadly than multi-example sets, with the degree of averaging (from pure summing to pure averaging) inferred from the data.

## times_table_kinship_exemplar  — rank 12, 32.7 ± 12.0 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2737.8

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, divisibility by the set's common divisor, sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but the distance-free arithmetic route is not "being a multiple of the example": it is belonging to the same familiar times table, i.e. sharing a factor of 3 or more with an example, with the route's strength falling off as the smallest such shared factor gets larger (3, 4, 5, 7 tables are well known; a 47 table is not). ## divisor_family_exemplar  — rank 13, 33.6 ± 14.3 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2738.7

Refinement of `common_divisor_sharpened_exemplar`: people still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, factor), sharpened by how suspicious the examples' shared coincidence is. The one change is to the space of arithmetic concepts they consider: they entertain "multiples of d" for every divisor d of 3 or more that all the examples share, including the divisors of a single lone example, not only the largest common divisor of a multi-example set. ## common_divisor_sharpened_exemplar  — rank 15, 39.0 ± 13.9 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2744.1

Refinement of the incumbent `coincidence_sharpened_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, and a factor dimension), and they still sharpen this generalization on every dimension when the examples share a suspicious coincidence. The one change is to the factor dimension. People do not check pairwise whether the target shares any factor with each example. ## common_divisor_rule_exemplar  — rank 16, 50.3 ± 14.4 nats behind the best (3.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2755.5

Refinement of the incumbent `coincidence_sharpened_exemplar`: people still generalize by summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity and a shared-factor dimension), and they still sharpen it on every dimension when the examples share a suspicious property. The one change is to the shared-factor dimension. When the examples have a common divisor (e.g. 3 in "63, 27, 99, 33", 8 in "8, 32", 10 in "10, 80"), people read the set as "multiples of that number". A target that is not a multiple of it loses similarity to every example, even if it shares some other factor with one of them. Single examples and sets whose only common divisor is 1 keep the old per-example coprimality test. This predicts that far targets with no matching final digit are endorsed when they divide by the set's common divisor (36 after "3, 63") and rejected when they do not.

## coincidence_sharpened_exemplar  — rank 17, 56.5 ± 16.2 nats behind the best (3.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2761.6

People generalize from the examples by summed similarity (magnitude, final digit, parity, shared factors), but how sharply similarity falls off is set by how suspicious a coincidence the examples are: the less likely it is that the examples would share their most specific common property (all the same final digit, all multiples of 5, all the same parity) by chance, the more specific they take the program to be. So they tighten their generalization on every dimension at once. This disagrees most with the best model on coherent sets, e.g. "78, 48, 88" or "15, 85, 45" (near non-matching targets rejected far more), "90, 45" (multiples of 5 favoured more sharply), and on how much sets differ in overall endorsement, while single examples and incoherent sets are generalized as before.

## accumulating_regularity_exemplar  — rank 18, 72.2 ± 19.3 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2777.3

Refinement of the pruned `shared_regularity_exemplar`: people still judge a target by its summed similarity to the remembered examples (magnitude, final digit, parity), and they still notice the most specific arithmetic or digit regularity that all the examples share (e.g. multiples of 3, powers of 2, ending in 8), favouring targets that conform to it and rejecting those that break it. The one change is that rule evidence now builds up with every example, as the size principle says it should: each further example that fits a surprising regularity makes it less likely to be a coincidence. So the push toward rule members and away from rule breakers grows with the number of examples beyond the first, and is no longer a fixed amount per set. Four multiples of 10 therefore support "multiples of 10" (e.g. 20 accepted, 47 rejected) much more strongly than two multiples of 3 support "multiples of 3", while a single example implies no rule.

## running_guess_coincidence_exemplar  — rank 19, 87.7 ± 19.5 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2792.8

People read the examples one at a time and keep a running guess about the program's rule (a shared final digit, multiples of 5, a shared parity): each new example that fits the current guess makes it more convincing, but an example that breaks it makes them drop the guess and start again from that example, forgetting the earlier evidence. They then generalize by summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, shared factors), sharpened on every dimension by how suspicious a coincidence the guess they end up with is. So order matters: "83, 38, 58, 68" ends on a three-example run of final digit 8 and gives narrow generalization, while the same numbers with the odd one last ("38, 58, 68, 83") would not.

## digit_run_rule_attention_exemplar  — rank 20, 100.4 ± 22.0 nats behind the best (4.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2805.5

Refinement of the incumbent `shared_factor_parity_exemplar`. People still judge a target by its summed similarity to the remembered examples in a space of magnitude, final digit, parity and shared factor, with attention to magnitude and final digit tuned by how variable the examples are. The one change, adapted from `digit_run_strict_criterion_exemplar` but placed inside the similarity space rather than the response criterion: when three or more examples all end in the same digit, people read the final digit as the program's rule, and their attention to it jumps. A target with a different final digit then loses much more similarity to every example, so after sets like "46, 76, 66" or "15, 85, 45" magnitude-near targets with the wrong digit are rejected far more often than graded digit attention allows. Targets that match the digit are not penalized.

## rule_uncertainty_guessing  — rank 21, 138.4 ± 20.1 nats behind the best (6.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2843.5

People judge a target by its plain summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, and the set's common divisor), but whether they actually use that judgment is a decision-level choice that depends on how clear the program's rule looks. When the examples share no suspicious property (a common final digit, multiples of 5, a common divisor, a shared parity), people are unsure what the program does and on many trials just guess, saying yes at their own base rate. The clearer the shared rule, the less they guess. So rule-less sets (e.g. "4, 66, 78, 96") get a broad, flat background of yes answers, while rule-coherent sets (e.g. "80, 70, 90, 100") reject dissimilar targets consistently.

## common_step_lattice_exemplar  — rank 22, 144.1 ± 24.1 nats behind the best (6.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2849.2

Refinement of `shared_factor_parity_exemplar`: people still judge a target by its summed similarity to the remembered examples (magnitude, final digit, parity and shared factors, with attention tuned to how variable the examples are), but they also read the spacing of the examples. When three or more examples are separated by gaps that are all multiples of one common step of at most 20 (e.g. step 10 in "78, 48, 88", step 6 in "63, 27, 99, 33", step 2 in "12, 14, 16"), people expect the program to move in that step, and a target that cannot be reached from the examples in whole steps feels dissimilar to every example by a fixed factor. ## shared_feature_contrast  — rank 23, 154.3 ± 25.3 nats behind the best (6.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2859.4

People do not compare the target to each example; they abstract the features that all of the examples have in common (odd/even, being a multiple of some number, a final digit, a decade, being a square, cube, power of two or prime) and weight each shared feature by how suspicious a coincidence it is (the rarer the feature among 1-100 and the more examples share it, the more it counts). They say yes to a target in proportion to how much weighted shared-feature evidence it satisfies, minus how much it violates, so sets with many specific common features (e.g. "8, 32", "3, 63", "78, 48, 88") produce sharp, rule-like acceptance of feature-satisfying targets anywhere on the number line and rejection of nearby violators, while sets with few common features are accepted only weakly.

## capacity_limited_attention_exemplar  — rank 24, 174.0 ± 27.1 nats behind the best (6.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2879.1

People judge a target by its similarity to the remembered examples on magnitude, final digit, parity and shared factors, but their attention is a fixed, limited budget split across these dimensions: whatever the examples are consistent on (a shared final digit, a shared parity, a tight magnitude range) pulls attention onto that dimension and necessarily away from the others. So after a set that shares a final digit, people reject wrong-digit targets and also become more tolerant of magnitude distance for right-digit targets, because the attention spent on the digit is taken from magnitude, and the reverse holds after scattered-digit sets.

## log_odds_distorted_concept_learning  — rank 29, 196.0 ± 28.6 nats behind the best (6.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2901.1

People infer the hidden program as a Bayesian learner would: they weigh candidate rules (parity, multiples of any number, powers, squares, cubes, primes, a shared final digit) and magnitude ranges by how well each explains the examples, favouring small concepts that make the examples a suspicious coincidence (the size principle), and they derive how probable it is that the target belongs. The one distortion is in how that probability is used: people perceive it in a compressed, log-odds-linear way (as in classic probability-weighting), so near-certain inclusions and exclusions are pulled strongly toward the middle. Targets the posterior rates as almost certain or almost impossible are therefore endorsed or rejected far less consistently than the normative account says.

## frequency_weighted_multiple_concepts  — rank 30, 206.6 ± 28.7 nats behind the best (7.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2911.8

Refinement of `log_odds_distorted_concept_learning`. People still infer the hidden program as a Bayesian learner would: they weigh candidate rules (parity, primes, squares, cubes, powers, multiples of a number, a shared final digit) and magnitude ranges by the size principle, and they perceive the resulting probability that the target belongs in a compressed, log-odds way. The one change is in which arithmetic rules come to mind: "multiples of k" is a familiar idea for small times tables, and it becomes less available the larger k is (its prior falls off as a power of k). So after "47" the tiny rule "multiples of 47" hardly competes, and 94 gets little support, while after "7" the rule "multiples of 7" carries 14, 49 and 98.

## times_table_rule_sampling  — rank 31, 272.7 ± 32.8 nats behind the best (8.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -2977.8

People do not compare the target with each example by similarity; on each trial they come up with one guess about the program and answer by whether the target obeys it. The guess is a familiar property that every example has (odd or even, a times-table "multiples of 3 to 12", a shared final digit, squares, cubes or powers of two), or failing that just "numbers in the region the examples cover". Each kind of property comes to mind with its own ease, with smaller times tables easier, and properties become more likely than the region guess as more examples fit them. So after a lone "15" people often say yes to 12 or 9 (the 3- and 5-tables) but after "47" they rarely say yes to 94, and targets inside the examples' span are accepted as fully as the examples themselves.
