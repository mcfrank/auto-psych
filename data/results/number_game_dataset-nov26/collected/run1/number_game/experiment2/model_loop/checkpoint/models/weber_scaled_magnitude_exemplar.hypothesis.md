People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, multiples, factor-kin and round-number kinship, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but the magnitude part of that similarity is read off a compressed mental number line that obeys Weber's law: how different two numbers feel depends on their gap relative to their size, not on the raw gap. So a gap of 10 makes a target feel much less like a small example (5 against 15) than like a large one (85 against 95), and generalization by magnitude is narrow around small examples and broad around large ones, with the degree of compression inferred from the data.
