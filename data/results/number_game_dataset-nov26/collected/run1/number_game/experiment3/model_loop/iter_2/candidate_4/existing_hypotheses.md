# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## coincidence_decisive_exemplar  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4139.9

Refinement of `parity_salience_asymmetry_exemplar` (partly averaged similarity to the examples on magnitude, final digit, asymmetrically salient parity and the set's common divisor, plus distance-free multiples, factor-kin and round-number routes). The one change is in what a suspicious coincidence among the examples (all the same final digit, all multiples of 5, all the same parity) does. It does not make people's similarity gradient narrower. Instead it makes them more confident that a rule is at work, so their yes/no decision becomes more decisive. Similarity still sets which targets are favoured, but after coherent sets ("15, 85, 45", "78, 48, 88", "12, 14, 16") people answer all-or-none: well-fitting targets are almost always accepted and poorly fitting ones almost always rejected. After single examples and incoherent sets, answers stay graded.

## parity_preserving_multiple_route  — rank 1, 4.1 ± 13.7 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4144.0

A multiple of an example feels like the example's kin only if it keeps the example's odd-or-even character. People still judge a target by its similarity to the examples, with a distance-free route for multiples, but that route is sharply weakened when the multiple has switched parity, i.e. an even multiple of an odd example. So far multiples of an odd example that stay odd (85 or 51 after "17", 99 after "33") are endorsed, while its doubles and other even multiples (34 or 68 after "17", 86 after "43", 62 after "31") are mostly rejected. Multiples of even examples (44, 66, 88 after "22") keep the full route.

## lone_example_digit_discount  — rank 2, 15.3 ± 12.5 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4155.2

Refinement of the incumbent `whole_set_round_category`: people still judge a target by its partly averaged similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus distance-free multiples, factor-kin and whole-set round-number routes). The one change: a final digit only looks like part of the rule once the examples can be compared with one another. When there is just a single example, people cannot tell whether its final digit matters, so a digit mismatch costs much less similarity than it does after two or more examples. After a lone "43" or "9", near targets with a different digit (40, 47; 6, 12) are therefore endorsed more, and far targets that only share the final digit (93; 49, 89) less, than the incumbent predicts, while multi-example sets are unchanged.

## log_distance_gradient_exemplar  — rank 3, 32.6 ± 11.3 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4172.5

Refinement of `whole_set_round_category`: people judge a target by its similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, with distance-free multiples, factor-kin and whole-set round routes, partly averaged over examples). The one change is the shape of the magnitude gradient: how different a target feels from an example grows with the logarithm of their numeric gap, not in proportion to it, so similarity falls off steeply over the first few steps away from an example and then levels off. The sharpest disagreement with the current best model is at the two ends of the gradient: near neighbours that do not share an example's final digit (e.g. 12 after "15", 52 after "48, 63, 53") lose more than a linear gradient allows, while targets 30 versus 60 away from the examples (e.g. 4 or 94 after "24, 35", 1 or 97 after "53, 47, 41") are rejected about equally.

## power_mean_pooled_exemplar  — rank 4, 37.8 ± 11.1 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4177.7

Refinement of the incumbent `whole_set_round_category` (similarity to the examples on magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus distance-free multiples, factor-kin and whole-set round-number routes, pooled across examples). The one change is how support is pooled across the examples: people do not take a simple (partly normalised) sum but a power mean whose exponent is inferred from the data, and when that exponent is below one the pooled support is dragged toward the example the target fits worst, so a target has to fit the set as a whole. A near target that is close to one example of a 3-4-example set but unlike the others (e.g. 20 after "16, 52, 88") therefore gets much less support than it would from a single example ("16"). Single-example sets are judged exactly as before, which addresses the critique that larger sets dilute near-target support more than the incumbent predicts.

## whole_set_round_category  — rank 5, 41.7 ± 11.3 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4181.6

People judge a target by its similarity to the stored examples (closeness in magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus distance-free routes for multiples and factor-kin, with support partly averaged over examples), but the familiar category "round numbers" is only brought to mind when the whole example set fits it: a multiple-of-ten target gains distance-free similarity to the examples only when every example ends in 0 or 5 (e.g. 30 or 100 after "5", 50 after "45, 90"). A lone 5- or 0-ending example inside a mixed set (e.g. "15, 11" or "6, 25") gives round targets no boost, unlike in the current best model, where each round-ish example lends kinship to round targets on its own.

## parity_salience_asymmetry_exemplar  — rank 6, 42.7 ± 10.2 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4182.6

Refinement of the incumbent `round_number_averaging_exemplar` (partly averaged similarity to the examples on magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus distance-free multiples, factor-kin and round-number routes). The one change is that "all even" and "all odd" are not equally salient shared properties: how much a parity mismatch cuts a target's similarity depends on whether the set is mostly even or mostly odd, with the strength and direction of this asymmetry inferred from the data. So an odd target after an all-even set such as "12, 14, 16" and an even target after an all-odd set such as "63, 27, 99, 33" are penalized by different amounts, where the incumbent penalizes both by the same amount.

## set_round_kinship_averaging  — rank 7, 42.8 ± 11.0 nats behind the best (3.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4182.7

Refinement of the incumbent `round_number_averaging_exemplar` (averaged similarity on magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus distance-free multiples, factor-kin and round-number routes). The one change: round-number kinship is read from the set as a whole, not from any single example — a round target gains distance-free support from 0/5-ending examples only to the extent that the set as a whole looks round, so the route weakens steeply with the fraction of examples that do not end in 0 or 5. Lone round-ish examples and all-round sets ("5", "45, 90") still carry round targets (100, 50), but a single 5-ending example inside a mixed set ("15, 11", "6, 25") gives round targets like 40 or 80 almost no boost.

## recency_gated_averaging_route  — rank 8, 43.7 ± 13.0 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4183.6

Refinement of `recency_gated_arithmetic_route` (rank 1, tied with the best), with one component grafted from `similarity_averaging_exemplar`. People judge a target by its similarity to each example: magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, all sharpened by suspicious coincidences. They also use distance-free arithmetic routes (multiples, factor-kin and round-number kinship), which need the example held in working memory and so fade with each later example. The one change is that people pool this support across examples by partly averaging it rather than summing it, so each further example dilutes a target's support. Near and far targets in 3-4-example sets therefore get less support than in single-example sets. A round target in a mixed set like "15, 11" is weakly carried, because the lone 5-ending example is both diluted and faded.

## progression_lattice_exemplar  — rank 9, 44.4 ± 13.1 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4184.3

People also entertain an arithmetic-progression concept: they read the examples' common spacing (the largest step, between 2 and 20, that divides every gap between them) and take the program to produce numbers reached from the examples in whole steps of it. A target on that step lattice gets a fixed boost toward "yes" at any distance from the examples (e.g. 50 after "80, 70, 90, 100", 35 after "53, 47, 41"). Off-lattice targets are judged only by the incumbent's similarity and arithmetic routes, which are partly averaged across examples.

## round_number_averaging_exemplar  — rank 10, 52.7 ± 11.6 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4192.6

Refinement of the incumbent `round_number_kinship_exemplar`, with one component grafted from `similarity_averaging_exemplar` (as used in `times_table_averaging_exemplar`): people pool a target's support across the examples by partly averaging rather than summing it. That support is the target's similarity to each example (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences) plus the distance-free routes for multiples, factor-kin and round-number kinship. So each further example dilutes the support instead of adding to it. A far target in a multi-example set (e.g. "25, 82", "50, 2") is not carried by several weak resemblances added together, while lone examples ("7", "5") keep generalizing broadly to their multiples and round kin (14, 49, 98; 30, 100).

## biased_lapse_exemplar  — rank 11, 64.6 ± 12.6 nats behind the best (5.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4204.4

People judge each target by its similarity to the examples exactly as in the current best account, but the decision rule has a lapse: on a fixed fraction of trials they do not consult the examples at all and simply answer from a standing bias toward yes or no. So targets that similarity marks as clear members (an example itself, a close same-digit neighbour) are still sometimes rejected, and targets it marks as clear non-members are still sometimes accepted, at a floor and ceiling set by the lapse rate and its yes-bias rather than by the examples.

## recency_gated_arithmetic_route  — rank 12, 73.5 ± 18.6 nats behind the best (4.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4213.3

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but the distance-free arithmetic routes (being a multiple or a factor-kin of an example, and round-number kinship) need the example to be actively held in mind while the target is checked, and examples fade from working memory as later ones arrive. So an example's arithmetic support falls off steadily with how many examples were shown after it: the multiples of the last-shown example (21 after "3, 7", 36 after "5, 9") are endorsed much more than equally far multiples of an earlier example (99 after "3, 33" is not carried by the 3), while ordinary magnitude and digit similarity is unaffected by order.

## round_target_familiarity_bias  — rank 13, 104.0 ± 20.3 nats behind the best (5.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4243.9

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, multiples and factor-kin of an example, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but the extra appeal of round numbers is a property of the target itself, not a resemblance to round-ish examples: multiples of ten feel like natural outputs of any number-producing program, so people are more willing to say yes to them by a fixed amount whatever the examples are. This disagrees with `round_number_kinship_exemplar` on round targets after sets with no example ending in 0 or 5 (e.g. 30 or 60 after "63, 27, 99, 33" are boosted here, not there), and on round targets after 0/5 sets, which here get no extra boost from having more round-ish examples.

## round_number_kinship_exemplar  — rank 14, 115.6 ± 19.5 nats behind the best (5.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4255.5

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, multiples and factor-kin of an example, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but they also treat round numbers as a familiar class of their own: a target that is a multiple of ten resembles every example ending in 0 or 5 by a fixed amount, at any distance and whatever its final digit. So round targets are endorsed well above what magnitude and digit similarity allow when the examples include numbers ending in 0 or 5 (100 after "5", 50 after "45, 90"), while targets ending in 5, and round targets after examples with no 0 or 5 ending, get no such boost (30 after "63, 27, 99, 33").

## weber_scaled_magnitude_exemplar  — rank 15, 119.2 ± 19.5 nats behind the best (6.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4259.1

People judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, multiples, factor-kin and round-number kinship, all sharpened when the examples share a suspicious coincidence), but the magnitude part of that similarity is read off a compressed mental number line that obeys Weber's law: how different two numbers feel depends on their gap relative to their size, not on the raw gap. So a gap of 10 makes a target feel much less like a small example (5 against 15) than like a large one (85 against 95), and generalization by magnitude is narrow around small examples and broad around large ones, with the degree of compression inferred from the data.

## times_table_averaging_exemplar  — rank 16, 158.6 ± 24.5 nats behind the best (6.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4298.5

Refinement of `times_table_multiple_route` (multiples route only for times-table examples 2-12), with one component grafted from `similarity_averaging_exemplar`: people pool their evidence across examples by (partly) averaging rather than summing it. A target's support — its similarity to each example (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences) plus the times-table multiples route — is diluted as more examples are shown, not accumulated. So a far target is not carried by adding up several weak resemblances in multi-example sets like "25, 82" or "50, 2", while lone examples like "7" or "5" keep generalizing broadly to their times-table multiples (14, 49, 98; 30, 100) and multiples of large examples (94 after "47") get no boost.

## times_table_round_kinship_exemplar  — rank 17, 159.2 ± 25.3 nats behind the best (6.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4299.1

Refinement of the incumbent `round_number_kinship_exemplar`: people still judge a target by its summed similarity to the examples (magnitude, final digit, parity, the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences), with distance-free routes for factor-kin of an example and for round targets after examples ending in 0 or 5. The one change is that the multiples route is retrieved times-table knowledge, so it exists only for examples that are themselves times-table numbers (2 to 12): after "7" people endorse 14, 49 and 98, but far multiples of two-digit examples (94 after "47", 62 after "31", 75 after "25") get no multiples boost and are judged by ordinary similarity, factor-kinship and roundness alone.

## times_table_round_averaging  — rank 18, 172.2 ± 23.3 nats behind the best (7.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4312.1

Refinement of `times_table_averaging_exemplar` (similarity to the examples on magnitude, final digit, parity and the set's common divisor, sharpened by suspicious coincidences, plus a distance-free multiples route only for times-table examples 2-12, with support partly averaged rather than summed over examples). The one change, grafted from `round_number_kinship_exemplar`: round numbers are a familiar class of their own, so a target that is a multiple of ten gains a fixed, distance-free amount of similarity to every example ending in 0 or 5. This predicts that round targets are endorsed well above magnitude and digit similarity after sets containing 0/5-ending examples (100 after "5", 50 after "45, 90"), while far multiples of large two-digit examples (94 after "47", 62 after "31") still get no multiples boost, unlike in the current best model.

## featural_contrast_exemplar  — rank 19, 446.1 ± 41.1 nats behind the best (10.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4586.0

People do not compare numbers on a few geometric dimensions; they represent each number as a bundle of familiar properties (odd or even, multiple of 3 to 12, square, cube, power of two, prime, final digit, decade) and judge a target by Tversky-style featural contrast with each example. Properties the target shares with an example raise its similarity, while properties only one of the two has lower it, each property weighted by how rare it is among 1-100. So 64 after "16" or 99 after "33" (many shared, rare properties) is endorsed at any distance, while a prime such as 43 or 17 lends almost nothing to its double. Pooled featural similarity, not set-level rule reading, drives the yes/no choice.
