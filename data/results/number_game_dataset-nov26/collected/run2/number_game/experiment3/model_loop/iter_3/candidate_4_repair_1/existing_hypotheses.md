# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## parity_bounded_ambiguity_doubt  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4727.2

Refinement of `rule_ambiguity_doubt_all_sizes` by one grafted component, taken from `parity_preserving_random_doubt` and applied at every set size. When the examples fit so many candidate rules that people give up on rule-based judgment (whether one example or several are shown), they do not answer completely flatly: they keep the parity that all the examples share as the one default constraint on a loosely patterned program. Their fallback "yes" goes to targets of that parity at a flat rate and much less often to opposite-parity targets. So rule-ambiguous even sets such as 64, 4 or 96, 48 are endorsed broadly for even targets but not for odd ones, while rule-based judgment (the salience-weighted share of the shared profile with the interval similarity) is unchanged.

## rule_ambiguity_doubt_all_sizes  — rank 1, 2.9 ± 2.9 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4730.1

Refinement of the incumbent `rule_ambiguity_single_doubt`. The one change is that rule-ambiguity doubt is not limited to lone examples. When several examples are shown, people also sometimes give up on rule-based judgment and answer at a flat rate, and they do so more often the more candidate rules all the examples fit together. Examples like 64 and 4 (even, squares, powers of 2, multiples of 4) leave the program's rule ambiguous, so their targets are endorsed broadly and uniformly; examples sharing few categories point to one rule and are judged by the salience-weighted share of their shared profile, as before. Multi-example doubt has its own base rate and its own dependence on how many rules the examples share, and the judged share and the lone-example doubt are the incumbent's unchanged, so sets whose examples share many properties are endorsed more broadly than the profile share alone predicts.

## working_memory_three_example_limit  — rank 2, 6.9 ± 8.0 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4734.2

People generalize from the examples as the current best account says, by the salience-weighted share of the profile the examples have in common (with doubt about a lone example, and the interval between the examples). The one change is a working-memory limit: people can hold only three examples in mind at once. So when four examples are shown, on an inferred share of trials one of them has dropped out of memory, and people judge the target against the profile the remaining three share. That profile is richer, because it includes coincidences the dropped example would have ruled out, so on four-example sets targets that fit the full set's profile are endorsed less than a reasoner using all four examples would endorse them, while sets of one to three examples are judged exactly as before.

## rule_ambiguity_parity_kept_doubt  — rank 3, 8.2 ± 6.3 nats behind the best (1.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4735.4

Refinement of the incumbent `rule_ambiguity_single_doubt` by one grafted component, taken from `parity_preserving_random_doubt`: when a lone example leaves people doubting that the program follows any rule (more often the more candidate rules the example fits), they do not answer completely flatly but keep the example's parity as the one default constraint on a loosely patterned program, so their doubt-mode "yes" goes to targets of the example's parity at the inferred flat rate and to opposite-parity targets at a discounted rate. Everything else is the incumbent unchanged, so lone examples differ in breadth both by how ambiguous they are and by whether they are asked about opposite-parity targets (the odd round lone examples 15, 55, 85, 95), the source of the n1_set_mean_variance discrepancy.

## power_chain_generation  — rank 4, 9.3 ± 5.1 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4736.5

When several examples are shown, people try to reconstruct how the program generated them step by step, and one operation they readily try is repeated multiplication: if every example lies on one chain of powers of a common base (4 and 64; 9 and 81; 2, 8, 16), they read the program as "keep multiplying by the base" and take the other links of that chain (16 for 4, 64; 27 and 3 for 9, 81) as numbers it produces, giving that power pattern extra weight, by an inferred boost over its ordinary salience, that a shared final digit does not overshadow, because it describes how the numbers were made rather than how they look. Otherwise the judgment is that of the current best account (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, the in-span interval similarity, and the richness-dependent doubt on lone examples), so the missing links of a power chain are endorsed far more than the share of the examples' surface profile alone predicts.

## rule_ambiguity_single_doubt  — rank 5, 11.0 ± 6.9 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4738.2

Refinement of the incumbent `single_example_random_program_doubt` by one change to its doubt mechanism: how often people set a lone example aside and answer at a flat rate depends on how many candidate rules that example fits. A number that belongs to many salient categories at once (64: even, a square, a cube, a power of 2, 4 and 8, a multiple of 4 and 8, ...) gives no way to tell which of them the program follows, so people more often give up on rule-based judgment and answer flatly; a number with few salient properties (43) points to a definite rule and is judged by its profile more often. Everything else is the incumbent unchanged (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, with the interval similarity on multi-example sets), so the breadth of generalization differs from one lone example to another, which the incumbent's single global doubt rate cannot produce (the n1_set_mean_variance critique).

## single_exception_tolerance  — rank 6, 11.1 ± 7.8 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4738.3

People allow that one of the examples may be an odd one out (a slip or noise in the program's output), so when three or four examples are shown, a property that every example but one has (two of 48, 63, 53 end in 3) is still entertained as the program's possible rule, at an inferred fraction of the weight it would carry if all examples shared it. Targets with such an almost-shared property are therefore endorsed more than the strictly shared profile implies; everything else is the current best account unchanged (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile with the in-span interval similarity, and the flat-rate doubt on lone examples that grows with how many rules the example fits).

## parity_preserving_random_doubt  — rank 7, 13.0 ± 7.2 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4740.2

When only one example is shown, people often suspect the program produces numbers more or less at random, but even then they do not set the lone example aside entirely: they keep its parity as the one default constraint on what such a loosely patterned program emits ("it gives odd numbers"), answering "yes" at a flat rate to targets of the example's parity and much less often to targets of the opposite parity. Otherwise (the remaining one-example trials and every multi-example trial) they judge by the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile with the in-span interval similarity, as the current best account does. The sharpest disagreement with the best model lives at opposite-parity targets of one-example sets, which this hypothesis predicts are endorsed far less, so lone examples asked about opposite-parity targets have markedly lower mean endorsement than the best model's flat doubt allows.

## pattern_graded_random_doubt  — rank 8, 18.5 ± 7.6 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4745.8

Refinement of the incumbent `single_example_random_program_doubt` by one change to its doubt mechanism. When only one example is shown, how often people suspect that the program produces numbers at random, and so answer at a flat rate whatever the target, is not fixed: it depends on how patterned that lone example looks, meaning the total salience of the properties it carries (a round multiple of 5 or 10, a power or square, many divisors, as opposed to a nondescript number). The direction and strength of this dependence are inferred. On the remaining one-example trials and on every multi-example trial people judge exactly as the incumbent does, so lone examples differ from one another in how broadly and how flatly they are generalized, which addresses the n1_set_mean_variance critique.

## representativeness_gated_random_doubt  — rank 9, 21.1 ± 8.9 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4748.3

People judge whether a lone example came from a rule-following or a random program by how representative it looks of random output (a representativeness heuristic): a conspicuous number — a perfect square or power, a round multiple of ten, a repdigit, 1 or 100 — looks deliberately chosen and convinces them the program follows a rule, while a nondescript number like 43 or 67 looks like what a random generator would spit out and leaves them answering at a flat rate regardless of the target. So one-example sets differ in breadth of generalization according to how patterned the example looks: conspicuous lone examples get sharp, property-based answers (yes to matching targets, no to the rest), nondescript ones get flat answers; multi-example sets are judged by the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile as in the current best account.

## single_example_random_program_doubt  — rank 10, 21.2 ± 8.6 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4748.4

Refinement of the incumbent `span_interval_similarity` by one change. When the program has shown only a single example, people often cannot rule out that it produces numbers more or less at random: on an inferred share of one-example trials they set the example's properties aside and answer at a flat rate that is the same for every target (so some of these answers are a flat "yes" and some a flat "no"), while on the remaining one-example trials and on every multi-example trial they judge exactly as the incumbent does (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, with the interval similarity). So one-example sets are endorsed more often, and their endorsement depends less on the target's distance and properties than the overlap alone predicts.

## round_example_reduces_random_doubt  — rank 11, 21.9 ± 8.5 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4749.1

When only a single example is shown, whether people suspect the program produces numbers at random depends on how random-looking that example is: a round number (a multiple of 5 or 10) looks deliberately chosen rather than randomly drawn, so people doubt much less that a rule lies behind it and judge targets strictly by their match to its properties, whereas a nondescript lone example invites the random-program doubt that makes people answer at a flat rate whatever the target. So lone round examples are endorsed much less broadly than lone nondescript ones, while multi-example judgments (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile with the in-span interval similarity) are unchanged.

## size_discounted_anything_concept  — rank 12, 36.1 ± 9.2 nats behind the best (3.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4763.3

People's space of candidate concepts includes "the program can produce any number at all", a concept every target fits, which they weigh by the size principle: because it is the broadest concept, each additional example that happens to fit a narrower pattern makes it less credible, by an inferred factor per example. So after one example the anything-goes concept carries much of the judgment and people say "yes" broadly with little regard to the target's distance or properties, while with three or four examples it has faded and "yes" follows the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile (with the in-span interval concept) as in the current best account.

## range_band_single_leniency  — rank 13, 38.3 ± 11.5 nats behind the best (3.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4765.5

Refinement of `range_tightened_magnitude_band` (chosen: statistically tied with the best and already separating broad single-example from tight multi-example magnitude generalization) by one grafted component, taken from `progression_band_single_leniency`: when only a single example is shown, people know they cannot yet tell which of its properties the program cares about, so they adopt a laxer decision criterion and lean toward "yes" for every target by an inferred amount. Everything else is the refined model unchanged (salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile with a magnitude band that is broad for one example and tighter outside the span of several, progression concept, seed multiples, final-digit overshadowing, lingering first- and last-example digits), so one-example sets are endorsed more, and more uniformly across distance from the example, than the profile match alone implies — the single_vs_multi_yes_gap and single_example_distance_gradient discrepancies the best model fails — with no change for two- to four-example sets.

## representativeness_discounted_anything  — rank 14, 43.0 ± 11.0 nats behind the best (3.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4770.2

Refinement of `size_discounted_anything_concept` (rank 1, the narrowest loser) by one change: how credible the "the program can produce any number" concept is depends on whether the examples look like something a random program would produce (the representativeness heuristic). A round example — a multiple of 5 or 10 — looks deliberately chosen rather than random, so each round example discounts the anything-goes concept by an extra inferred factor on top of the size-principle discount per additional example; everything else is the refined model unchanged. So a lone round example (85, 30, 100, 55) is generalized much more narrowly, by its shared property profile, than a lone nondescript example (43, 73, 16), which makes breadth of generalization differ between one-example sets (the n1_set_mean_variance critique) and sharpens property-based (parity) generalization from round lone examples.

## evidence_scaled_decisiveness  — rank 15, 49.6 ± 13.4 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4776.8

People infer the program's concept as the current best account says (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared property profile, including the stretch between the examples), but the decision rule that turns that belief into a yes/no answer depends on how much evidence they have: the fewer examples shown, the noisier and less decisive their answers, drifting toward a coin flip. So after a single example poorly matching targets are endorsed more and targets are less sharply differentiated, while with three or four examples the same degree of match is acted on more decisively.

## span_interval_similarity  — rank 16, 50.7 ± 14.8 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4777.9

When two or more examples are shown, people also treat the stretch of the number line between the smallest and largest example as a candidate concept in its own right ("numbers in this range"), and a target earns credit for it by its similarity to that interval: full credit anywhere inside it, decaying exponentially (Shepard's generalization gradient, at an inferred and fairly steep rate) with the target's distance outside it. This interval similarity enters, with its own salience, the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile that drives "yes" in the current best account, so targets lying between the examples are endorsed more than equally nondescript targets outside them, even when they share no digit, parity or seed-multiple with the examples.

## range_tightened_magnitude_band  — rank 17, 53.9 ± 15.0 nats behind the best (3.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4781.1

Refinement of `progression_band_first_digit_primacy` (the incumbent) by one change to its graded magnitude band: the band's width depends on whether the examples mark out a range. A single example gives no range, so magnitude credit falls off broadly around it, but once two or more examples are shown people take the span they cover as the program's range, and magnitude credit falls off more steeply (by an inferred fraction of the single-example width) below the smallest and above the largest example. Everything else is the incumbent unchanged, so in multi-example sets targets inside the examples' range that share no other property are endorsed more than targets outside it (the inrange_nonshared_rate critique), while single-example sets still generalize broadly.

## span_sharpened_magnitude_band  — rank 18, 55.1 ± 15.0 nats behind the best (3.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4782.3

Refinement of the incumbent `progression_band_first_digit_primacy` by one change to its graded magnitude band: how sharply magnitude credit falls off outside the examples depends on whether the examples mark out a span. A lone example gives no edges, so people generalize by magnitude broadly; once two or more examples are shown, their smallest and largest values become real boundaries, and credit falls off much more steeply (its own inferred width) beyond them. Everything else is the incumbent unchanged, so in multi-example sets, targets inside the examples' range that share nothing else with them are endorsed more than equally nondescript targets outside it, closing the inrange_nonshared_rate critique, while single-example generalization stays broad.

## incremental_band_narrowing  — rank 19, 56.9 ± 15.9 nats behind the best (3.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4784.1

People read the examples one at a time and keep a running guess about how widely the program's numbers spread in magnitude. After a single example the guess is broad, but each additional example revises it and tightens how far beyond the examples' range they are willing to generalize. So targets outside the range lose endorsement faster with distance the more examples have been shown, while targets inside the range keep full magnitude credit, and the rest of the judgment (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared properties) is unchanged.

## salience_scaled_span_decisiveness  — rank 20, 62.3 ± 14.4 nats behind the best (4.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4789.5

Refinement of the incumbent `span_interval_similarity` by one change, grafted from `salience_scaled_decisiveness_overlap`: how decisively people act on a target's match to the examples' shared profile grows with how much the examples visibly have in common. When the examples share many or highly salient properties (a common final digit, a seed and its multiples, an arithmetic progression), a well-matching target is endorsed and a mismatching one rejected far more sharply than when the examples share only a few weak properties, where answers stay close to the baseline whatever the target; the judged share itself (including the in-span interval similarity) is the incumbent's unchanged, so items are differentiated more sharply on strongly patterned sets (the item_mean_variance critique).

## first_impression_anchoring  — rank 24, 133.7 ± 21.0 nats behind the best (6.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4860.9

People read the examples in order and form their first guess about the program from the first example alone, entertaining every salient property it has (its parity, the numbers it is a multiple of, its digits, its final digit). Later examples should rule out the first example's properties they lack, but people adjust insufficiently from this first impression: contradicted first-example properties keep a fraction of their weight, so targets that share properties with the first-shown example — not only its final digit — are endorsed more than the examples' common profile alone implies, and the same examples in a different order are generalized differently.

## evidence_tuned_attention_exemplar  — rank 25, 341.7 ± 33.3 nats behind the best (10.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -5068.9

People do not infer a concept; they store each example shown and judge a target by its average similarity to those stored examples, where similarity falls off exponentially with the attention-weighted number of the example's properties (parity, primality, squares/cubes, powers, multiples, base-ten multiples, final digit, contained digits) the target lacks, plus their distance in magnitude. Attention to a property is learned from the examples themselves: it grows with how many of the stored examples carry that property, shrunk toward zero by a prior pseudo-count of uninformative observations, so a single example draws only weak attention to any of its features (broad, nearly distance-flat generalization), while each further example sharpens attention to the features they have in common.

## sequence_momentum_extrapolation  — no comparison row

People read the examples in the order shown as the program's running output, and take the direction it was moving (the last example larger or smaller than the first) as where it is heading next. So when several examples are shown, they extend magnitude generalization further beyond the examples on the side the sequence was moving toward than on the side it came from: an ascending set like 12, 47 is generalized to larger targets above it more readily than to equally distant targets below it, and a descending set the other way round. Lone examples have no direction, and the rest of the judgment (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, with the in-span interval and the parity-keeping rule-ambiguity doubt) is that of the current best account.

## log_number_line_similarity  — no comparison row

People judge how close a target is to the examples in magnitude on a compressed, logarithmic mental number line, where closeness is a matter of ratio rather than difference: 3 is far from 20, while 90 is close to 60. Everything else is the current best account unchanged (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, with rule-ambiguity doubt that keeps the shared parity), but both magnitude similarities in it, the bell-shaped band around the examples' range and the exponential similarity to the interval between them, now fall off with the log-ratio distance from the nearest edge. So small targets below the examples are rejected much faster than large targets above them at the same numeric distance, and generalization upward from large examples is broad.

## uncertainty_gated_default_answer  — no comparison row

People form their belief about whether the program produces the target exactly as the interval-similarity account says (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared property profile, including the stretch between the examples), but the decision rule depends on how sure that belief is: the closer the belief is to a toss-up, the more often they stop deliberating and fall back on a default answer, given at an inferred default "yes" rate, whereas clear-cut beliefs (a target that plainly fits or plainly breaks the pattern) are acted on directly. So targets of intermediate fit, which abound after a single example, are answered at a nearly uniform default rate regardless of their exact fit, while clearly fitting or clearly violating targets keep their sharp answers, unlike a fixed lapse that flattens every answer equally.

## parity_doubt_three_example_memory  — no comparison row

Refinement of the incumbent `parity_bounded_ambiguity_doubt` by one grafted component, taken from `working_memory_three_example_limit`: people can hold only three examples in mind, so when four are shown, on an inferred share of trials one example has dropped out of memory and the whole judgment — the salience-weighted share of the shared profile, how many rules the examples fit (and so how often people give up and answer flatly), and the parity kept in that fallback — is made from the three remembered examples, averaged over which one was lost. Because three examples share more coincidental properties and fit more rules than four, four-example sets are judged against a richer profile, so targets fitting the full set's profile are endorsed less, and sometimes more broadly, than a reasoner using all four would; sets of one to three examples are judged exactly as by the incumbent.
