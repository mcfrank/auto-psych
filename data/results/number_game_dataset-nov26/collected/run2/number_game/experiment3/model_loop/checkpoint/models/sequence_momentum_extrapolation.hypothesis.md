People read the examples in the order shown as the program's running output, and take the direction it was moving (the last example larger or smaller than the first) as where it is heading next. So when several examples are shown, they extend magnitude generalization further beyond the examples on the side the sequence was moving toward than on the side it came from: an ascending set like 12, 47 is generalized to larger targets above it more readily than to equally distant targets below it, and a descending set the other way round. Lone examples have no direction, and the rest of the judgment (the salience-weighted share of the examples' shared profile, with the in-span interval and the parity-keeping rule-ambiguity doubt) is that of the current best account.
