# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## range_specific_evidence_strength  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4152.4

Refinement of `consistent_examples_bounded_rules`. People use the same tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules, size-weighted ranges, their odd/even versions and bounded rules that every example must fit, and they still discount comparison-based rules when shown one example. The one change: people do not weigh the examples as equally strong evidence for every kind of concept. Once they think of a magnitude range, the size principle acts on it with its own strength, which differs from the strength used for mathematical and digit rules by an inferred amount (no difference recovers the incumbent). This separates how tightly the imagined range fits the examples from how sharply people choose among rules, so it should change how much people endorse in-range relative to out-of-range targets for pairs of examples and for sets that no rule fits fully, while judgments driven by a rule that fits fully stay nearly the same.

## consistent_examples_bounded_rules  — rank 1, 11.6 ± 3.7 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4163.9

Refinement of `comparison_dependent_concepts` (the same tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules, with digit rules and bounded rules discounted when only one example is shown). The one change takes that model's own premise further. A rule confined to the examples' span ("ends in 3 between 48 and 63") is built by comparing the examples and seeing that they share the rule, so people entertain it only when every example fits the rule. A bounded rule that one of the examples breaks is never considered, rather than kept with that example written off as a stray, and no parameter is added. People should then drop a rule that most examples share but one breaks, and fall back on the examples' range or on whole rules. In-range numbers lacking the majority pattern (48,63,53 → 49, 51) should be endorsed more than the incumbent predicts, while sets that a rule fits fully are unchanged.

## stray_rate_inferred_concepts  — rank 2, 23.5 ± 5.2 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4175.8

Refinement of `comparison_dependent_concepts`. It keeps the same tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules, with digit and bounded rules discounted for a lone example. The one change: people do not assume a fixed 10% of the examples are random strays. How likely they think a stray example is gets inferred. If people think the program's examples are rarely strays, any rule that one example breaks (whole, bounded or parity-restricted) loses nearly all belief, and that belief moves to the ranges that cover every example. In-range numbers should then be endorsed more than either earlier model predicts for sets no rule fully fits and for pairs of examples, and less outside the range.

## central_tendency_feature_prototype  — rank 3, 270.0 ± 37.0 nats behind the best (7.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4422.3

People do not infer a rule. They boil the examples down to one prototype, which records how often each familiar number property (odd/even, prime, square, cube, power, multiple of k, final digit, digit contained) occurs among the examples and where the examples sit on average on the number line. They say "yes" to a target according to its similarity to that prototype. A property counts toward the comparison more steeply the more consistently the examples share it, and the target loses similarity for the share of that weight that falls on properties it lacks and for its distance from the examples' central magnitude. This predicts that when no property is shared by every example, generalization rests on closeness to the examples' centre, so targets between the examples are endorsed more than equally related targets outside their span, while a property shared by all the examples dominates the judgment.

## most_specific_noticed_rule  — no comparison row

People do not weigh many candidate concepts at once; they search for a single rule and stop at the first one they notice. Each familiar property that every example shares (a parity, multiples of some number, a special number kind such as squares, powers or primes, a final digit, a contained digit) is noticed with a probability that depends on its kind, and among the shared properties they notice people adopt the most specific one and say "yes" to targets that have it. Only when they notice no shared property do they fall back on the stretch of the number line the examples span, endorsing targets inside it and fewer the farther a target lies outside it, so sets with no common rule give little generalization beyond their range.

## specific_rule_preempts_supersets  — no comparison row

Refinement of `range_specific_evidence_strength` (the same tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules, size-weighted ranges with their own evidence strength, odd/even versions and fully consistent bounded rules). The one change is explaining away: when people compare two or more examples and see that they all share a specific property (all end in 3), they treat any broader property this implies (being odd, containing a 3) as a by-product of the specific one rather than as a candidate rule, so every rule that strictly contains another rule all the examples fit loses plausibility by an inferred amount. The disagreement with the best model lives on targets that have only the broader, implied property: after 63,23,43 or 93,43,83,53, odd numbers with another final digit (31, 35, 39) should be endorsed less than the best model predicts, while targets with the specific property, and sets without nested rules, barely change.

## rule_first_range_fallback  — no comparison row

People look for a mathematical or digit rule that every example obeys before they think of a magnitude range, so whether that search succeeds sets how plausible rules and ranges seem. When no rule fits all the examples, prior belief shifts from rules to the range the examples span by an inferred amount; otherwise people learn as the current best model does (tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules, size-weighted ranges with their own evidence strength, odd/even versions and fully consistent bounded rules). This predicts that for sets no rule explains (71,61,26,81; 70,88,7,22) numbers inside the examples' span are endorsed more, and numbers outside it less, than the current best model predicts.
