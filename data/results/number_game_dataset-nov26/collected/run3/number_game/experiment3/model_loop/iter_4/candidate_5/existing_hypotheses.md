# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## magnitude_threshold_concepts  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4130.9

I refine the incumbent `iter2_candidate1`. It keeps the tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over weighted rules, size-weighted ranges with their own evidence strength, odd/even versions and fully consistent bounded rules, along with the lone-example discount and the explaining away of implied rules, which grows with each extra example. The one change: people also treat one-sided magnitude thresholds ("numbers above 40", "numbers below 30") as concepts in their own right. So a range that runs up to the top of 1-100, or down to the bottom, gains plausibility beyond an ordinary range of the same size, with a separate inferred amount for "above" and "below" (none recovers the incumbent). People should then extend generalization from the examples all the way to one end of the number line more than the incumbent predicts, and more toward larger numbers than toward smaller ones.

## belief_criterion_confidence_gain  — rank 13, 43.3 ± 16.3 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4174.2

People infer the program's concept by the same simple tempered Bayesian concept learning as the simplest current model, but they do not answer "yes" with a probability equal to their belief (probability matching): they weigh how strongly they believe the concept contains the target against an internal criterion of how sure they must be, and this comparison is noisy, so their answers are more or less all-or-none than the belief itself by an inferred confidence gain on the log-odds of belief. Unlike a guessing lapse, which pulls every answer toward one fixed rate by the same proportion, this rule moves confident beliefs (examples, clear non-members) much less than moderate ones, so a gain below 1 leaves near-certain judgments close to certainty while it compresses uncertain ones toward the criterion, and a gain above 1 makes them more extreme.

## hundreds_chart_block_concepts  — rank 15, 71.2 ± 25.9 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4202.1

People picture the numbers 1-100 laid out as a ten-by-ten hundreds chart (rows are decades, columns are final digits), and the "magnitude" concepts they entertain are rectangular blocks of that chart rather than stretches of the number line. They weigh these blocks, together with familiar mathematical and digit rules, by how small a concept can still cover the examples (the size principle), and say "yes" to a target with their belief that the concept contains it. So examples sharing a final digit are read as a narrow column block that excludes other final digits even of the same parity, and when no rule fits, people endorse in-range targets only if the target's final digit falls within the span of the examples' final digits.

## most_specific_noticed_rule  — rank 16, 240.3 ± 40.3 nats behind the best (6.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4371.2

People do not weigh many candidate concepts at once; they search for a single rule and stop at the first one they notice. Each familiar property that every example shares (a parity, multiples of some number, a special number kind such as squares, powers or primes, a final digit, a contained digit) is noticed with a probability that depends on its kind, and among the shared properties they notice people adopt the most specific one and say "yes" to targets that have it. Only when they notice no shared property do they fall back on the stretch of the number line the examples span, endorsing targets inside it and fewer the farther a target lies outside it, so sets with no common rule give little generalization beyond their range.

## upward_directed_similarity_gradient  — rank 17, 279.5 ± 37.1 nats behind the best (7.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4410.3

People judge a target by its similarity to the examples on a single explicit metric: the target loses similarity for each number property the examples consistently share (parity, final digit, digits contained, multiples, special kinds such as squares or primes) that it lacks, with a property's attention rising steeply with how consistently the examples share it, and for how far it lies below the smallest example on the number line. The magnitude gradient is one-directional: people treat the program's output as counting upward from its examples, so numbers above the largest example lose no similarity for their magnitude, while numbers below the smallest lose more the farther down they lie, making people generalize upward more than downward.

## central_tendency_feature_prototype  — rank 18, 291.4 ± 38.1 nats behind the best (7.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4422.3

People do not infer a rule. They boil the examples down to one prototype, which records how often each familiar number property (odd/even, prime, square, cube, power, multiple of k, final digit, digit contained) occurs among the examples and where the examples sit on average on the number line. They say "yes" to a target according to its similarity to that prototype. A property counts toward the comparison more steeply the more consistently the examples share it, and the target loses similarity for the share of that weight that falls on properties it lacks and for its distance from the examples' central magnitude. This predicts that when no property is shared by every example, generalization rests on closeness to the examples' centre, so targets between the examples are endorsed more than equally related targets outside their span, while a property shared by all the examples dominates the judgment.
