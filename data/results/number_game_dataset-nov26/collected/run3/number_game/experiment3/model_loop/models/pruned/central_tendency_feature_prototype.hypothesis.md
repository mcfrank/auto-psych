People do not infer a rule. They boil the examples down to one prototype, which records how often each familiar number property (odd/even, prime, square, cube, power, multiple of k, final digit, digit contained) occurs among the examples and where the examples sit on average on the number line. They say "yes" to a target according to its similarity to that prototype. A property counts toward the comparison more steeply the more consistently the examples share it, and the target loses similarity for the share of that weight that falls on properties it lacks and for its distance from the examples' central magnitude. This predicts that when no property is shared by every example, generalization rests on closeness to the examples' centre, so targets between the examples are endorsed more than equally related targets outside their span, while a property shared by all the examples dominates the judgment.
