# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## digit_rules_need_comparison  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3132.2

Refinement of `range_prior_sample_size_neglect` (the same tempered, sample-size-neglecting Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals of 1-100, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules). The one change: a digit pattern ("ends in 6", "contains a 1") is something people notice only by comparing examples, so when a single example is shown, digit-based rules hardly come to mind, and their prior is discounted by an inferred amount (zero recovers the incumbent). Belief then moves to the lone example's arithmetic rules. People should generalize a lone example to its multiples (6 → 12, 18; 11 → 55, 66; 10 → 80) more than the incumbent allows, and endorse numbers that share only its digits (14 → 74, 94) less. Judgments about sets of two or more examples are unchanged.

## range_sized_bounded_rules  — rank 1, 17.5 ± 10.5 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3149.7

Refinement of `range_prior_sample_size_neglect` (tempered Bayesian concept learning with sample-size neglect over 45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals of 1-100, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules). The one change is that the range-size prior people hold applies to every concept defined by a range, so it applies to a rule confined to a range ("multiples of 11 between 5 and 15") as it does to a plain interval. A bounded rule's prior plausibility grows by the same inferred power of how many members it keeps, rather than being equal for every range, and no new parameter is added. Tiny bounded versions of a rule, such as {11} or {25}, then seem implausible. From a lone example people should generalize to the whole rule it belongs to (11 → 55, 66; 25 → 50, 100), while sets of several examples keep a range-bounded reading.

## range_prior_sample_size_neglect  — rank 2, 18.8 ± 9.0 nats behind the best (2.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3151.0

Refinement of `range_size_prior_concepts`: people do the same tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, all intervals of 1-100 (with a prior that grows as a power of a range's size), their odd-only and even-only versions, and range-bounded rules. The one change is taken from `sample_size_neglect_evidence`: people neglect sample size, so each example's evidential weight shrinks as more examples are shown. The total weight of the examples grows less than in proportion to their number (no neglect recovers the incumbent). This predicts that a lone example is weighed heavily enough to pin down the small rule it belongs to (6 → 12, 18; 11 → 55, 66; 25 → 50, 100), so its members are endorsed far more than the incumbent allows, while larger sets sharpen generalization less than an ideal learner's would.

## range_size_prior_concepts  — rank 3, 25.3 ± 10.4 nats behind the best (2.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3157.5

Refinement of `range_bounded_parity_concepts`: people do the same tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, all intervals of 1-100, their odd-only and even-only versions, and range-bounded rules, but they do not think every magnitude range is equally plausible a priori — a tiny range such as "numbers from 10 to 12" seems an unlikely thing for a program to produce, so a range's prior plausibility grows (or shrinks) as an inferred power of its size (power 0 recovers the incumbent). The one change is this size-dependent prior on ranges; because the size principle favours tiny ranges most when there is only one example, a broad-range prior moves belief from tiny ranges around a lone example to the rules it belongs to, so people generalize from a single example (10 → 80, 11 → 55) much more than the incumbent allows, while several examples still pin down a tight concept.

## open_multiples_guessing_lapse  — rank 4, 26.7 ± 14.0 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3158.9

Refinement of `open_ended_multiples_concepts`: people do the same tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals of 1-100, their odd-only and even-only versions, range-bounded rules and the open-ended "multiples of k" family (k from 13 to 50). The one change, taken from `neglect_evidence_guessing_lapse`, is that people do not consult this concept on every trial: at an inferred lapse rate they skip the inference and give a biased guess, answering "yes" at an inferred guessing rate whatever the target is (a lapse rate of 0 recovers the original model). Because the guessing lapse, not weak evidence, accounts for the rejected examples and the floor of yeses, the concept inference itself can be sharper, so members of the examples' rule (including the lone example's own multiples, 25 → 50, 100) are endorsed more and clear non-members less, apart from the guessing floor.

## open_ended_multiples_concepts  — rank 5, 27.6 ± 10.9 nats behind the best (2.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3159.8

People do the same tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning as `range_size_prior_concepts` (45 weighted rules, size-weighted intervals of 1-100, their odd-only and even-only versions, and range-bounded rules), but their space of concepts also includes an open-ended family of "multiples of k" concepts for larger steps k from 13 to 50 (e.g. multiples of 25, of 33, of 14), beyond the few small multiples in the fixed rule list. This family gets an inferred share of prior belief, and within it a step's prior plausibility falls off in inverse proportion to k (larger steps come to mind less easily). Because the size principle strongly favours such a small concept when it contains the examples, people generalize a lone example to its own multiples (25 → 50, 75, 100; 33 → 66, 99; 14 → 28, 42) much more than the fixed rule list allows.

## neglect_evidence_guessing_lapse  — rank 6, 35.6 ± 13.1 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3167.8

Refinement of `sample_size_neglect_evidence`: people do the same tempered Bayesian concept learning with sample-size neglect (45 weighted rules, all intervals, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules, with each extra example adding less evidence), but they do not consult that concept on every trial. On some trials, at an inferred lapse rate, they skip the inference and answer by a biased guess, saying "yes" at an inferred guessing rate whatever the target is. The one change is this guessing lapse (a lapse rate of 0 recovers the original model). It predicts that even targets that are themselves examples, or that clearly belong to the inferred concept, are sometimes rejected (examples get about 0.7–0.9 yes, not 1), and that clear non-members still get a floor of yeses.

## single_example_rule_reading  — rank 7, 38.9 ± 12.8 nats behind the best (3.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3171.1

Refinement of `parity_restricted_concepts` (people do tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, all intervals of 1-100, and their odd-only and even-only versions). The one change: a magnitude range is a concept defined by its endpoints, so a single example cannot suggest one — when only one example is shown, people read it as an instance of a mathematical or digit property, and prior belief shifts from range concepts to rules by an inferred amount (zero shift recovers the original model). This predicts that from a lone example people endorse the members of the rules that contain it broadly (11 → 22, 55, 99; 10 → 60, 90) rather than confining belief to a tight range around the example, while judgments about sets of two or more examples are unchanged.

## sample_size_neglect_evidence  — rank 8, 39.6 ± 9.7 nats behind the best (4.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3171.8

People do the same tempered Bayesian concept learning as the best model (45 weighted rules, all intervals, their odd/even versions and range-bounded rules), with one distortion: they neglect sample size, so the total weight they give the examples grows less than in proportion to how many examples they saw — the first example counts heavily, and each further example adds less. This predicts that a single example already pins down its most salient rule (e.g. 10 → multiples of 10), producing many yeses for that rule's members, while adding more examples sharpens generalization far less than an ideal learner would.

## weak_sampling_hypothesis_elimination  — rank 9, 50.3 ± 19.2 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3182.4

People learn the hidden concept by eliminating hypotheses rather than by weighing how typical the examples are: they keep every rule (one of 45 mathematical or digit rules, each with its own prior plausibility) or range of numbers that is consistent with the examples, treating an example that does not fit only as a possible stray, and they answer "yes" with their total belief in the surviving concepts that contain the target. Because there is no size principle (weak sampling), a small concept gains nothing over a broad one that also fits, so people generalize broadly — especially from a single example — and adding consistent examples does not sharpen belief toward the smallest rule.

## range_bounded_parity_concepts  — rank 10, 51.8 ± 11.7 nats behind the best (4.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3184.0

Refinement of `parity_restricted_concepts`: people do the same tempered-evidence Bayesian concept learning over the 45 weighted rules, all intervals of 1-100, and their odd-only and even-only versions. The one change is a single added component, taken from `bounded_range_rule_learning`: people can also restrict a rule to a range of numbers that covers the examples (e.g. "multiples of 3 between 20 and 100"), and these range-bounded rules get an inferred share of the prior for rules (share 0 recovers `parity_restricted_concepts`). A bounded rule is smaller than the full rule, so the size principle favours it. This predicts that people endorse members of the examples' rule that lie outside the examples' min-max range less than members inside it, and that this gap is larger than the rule/interval mixture allows (63,27,99,33 → 3 below 45).

## arithmetic_relation_exemplar  — rank 11, 77.7 ± 30.3 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3209.9

People do not infer a hidden rule; they judge a target by its similarity to the stored examples, as in an exemplar (context) model, where two numbers are similar to the extent that they stand in familiar arithmetic relations — one is a multiple of the other, they share a common factor of 3 or more, they end in the same digit, they have the same parity — and each relation the pair lacks shrinks their similarity by an inferred factor. The target's average similarity to the examples, against an internal criterion, sets the chance of "yes", so a lone example is generalized to its own multiples (6 → 18, 25 → 100, 11 → 55) because each such target is arithmetically close to it, while numbers sharing none of these relations with any example are rejected.

## pedagogical_sampling_concepts  — rank 12, 87.5 ± 15.1 nats behind the best (5.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3219.7

People assume the examples were chosen by a helpful teacher, not sampled at random: the program picked numbers that would best lead a learner to its concept (pedagogical sampling). So an example counts as evidence for a concept, whether a mathematical rule or a range, in proportion to how strongly that example on its own would make a naive learner think of that concept, compared with the other numbers the teacher could have shown; people then answer "yes" with their resulting belief that the concept contains the target. This predicts that concepts for which the shown examples would be poor, unrepresentative teaching choices lose belief even when they contain the examples, unlike under the size principle, where any concept that contains the examples gains in proportion to how small it is.

## extrapolation_verification_failure  — rank 13, 129.5 ± 24.0 nats behind the best (5.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3261.7

People infer the hidden concept by the same tempered, serial-position-weighted Bayesian concept learning as the best model (45 weighted rules and all intervals, with sorted lists shifting belief toward ranges), but to answer they must mentally verify that the target follows from the examples, and this extrapolation becomes unreliable beyond the span of numbers they have actually seen: the farther the target lies outside the examples' min–max range, the more likely the check fails and they answer "no", at an inferred rate per unit of distance. This predicts that members of a broad arithmetic rule (e.g. multiples of 3 after 63, 27, 99, 33) are endorsed less outside the examples' range than inside it, more steeply than belief in the concept alone implies, while inside-range judgments are unchanged.

## normalized_attention_similarity  — rank 14, 155.4 ± 32.3 nats behind the best (4.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3287.6

People judge a target by its similarity to the examples over number properties (parity, primality, squares, cubes, powers, multiples, final digit, digits contained), where the examples set a fixed budget of attention that is shared among the properties according to how consistently the examples have them. The target's dissimilarity is the fraction of that attention falling on properties it lacks, and the chance of "yes" falls off exponentially with it (Shepard-style generalization). Because the budget is fixed, a lone example spreads attention over its many properties, so a target sharing most of them (10 → 80, 6 → 18) is still judged very similar and broadly endorsed, while several examples that agree on one property (all ending in 2) concentrate attention there and sharply reject targets lacking it.
