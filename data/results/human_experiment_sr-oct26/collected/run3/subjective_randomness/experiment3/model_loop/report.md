## Hypotheses

- **mild_symmetric_trick_coin_streak_weary**: Refinement of the incumbent `streak_weary_chance_pseudoflip` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order, streak-weary picture of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — with pseudo-flip-diluted per-flip evidence, heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change is to the trick coin people imagine: not one leaning towards heads with any degree of rigging equally likely, but a symmetric coin that is only mildly rigged, with how mildly fitted. A modest heads/tails imbalance is then weak evidence against chance, so people weigh balance less against switching. This addresses the critique that in length-8 pairs where the more-switching sequence is also the more lopsided one, people choose the more-switching sequence more often than the incumbent predicts.
- **accelerating_streak_weary_chance**: Refinement of the incumbent `streak_weary_chance_pseudoflip` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order, streak-weary picture of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — with pseudo-flip-diluted per-flip evidence, heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change is in how their gambler's fallacy grows with streak length: the expectation that chance must switch does not rise steadily with each extra flip of a run but accelerates, so a run of three barely seems overdue while a run of five or six feels sharply improbable for a fair coin, and the first break of a long streak makes a sequence look far more random than the incumbent's linear belief allows. This addresses the critique that among long pairs where both sequences have at most two switches, people choose the more-switching sequence more often than the incumbent predicts.
- **lopsided_trick_coin_probit**: Refinement of `thurstonian_probit_decision` (people pick the sequence whose noisy felt randomness is larger — a probit comparison — where felt randomness is how much better their own second-order picture of a fair coin explains the sequence than the regular generators they suspect: a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips, weighed per flip with pseudo-flip dilution, heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change is in how lopsided people imagine a trick coin to be: instead of a fixed prior over its heads rate, how concentrated that prior is is fitted, so if people picture trick coins as grossly rigged, only an extreme heads/tails imbalance is explained away as rigging, and a moderately lopsided but frequently switching sequence still looks random. This addresses the critique that in long pairs where the more-switching sequence is also the more lopsided one, people choose the more-switching sequence more often than the best model predicts — they weigh heads/tails balance against switching less than it assumes.
- **streak_weary_chance_pseudoflip**: Refinement of the incumbent `short_sequence_pseudoflip_dilution` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order picture of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity, a side habit, and the per-flip evidence diluted by imagined pseudo-flips for short sequences). The single change, taken from `gamblers_run_second_order_side_habit`, is to their picture of chance: people hold a gambler's fallacy that a fair coin becomes more likely to switch the longer the current streak has lasted, so a long unbroken streak is improbable under chance far beyond its switch count and the first one or two breaks of a long streak make a sequence look much more random — addressing the FDR-surviving critique that among long, low-switch pairs people choose the sequence with more streak breaks more often than the incumbent predicts.
- **thurstonian_probit_decision**: The choice is made by a Thurstonian comparison: each sequence's felt randomness is read with Gaussian internal noise, and the person picks whichever noisy impression is larger. Choice probability therefore follows a normal (probit) curve of the evidence difference instead of a logistic one, so its tails are thinner: on clear-cut pairs, such as a long pure streak against a sequence with one or two breaks, choices become close to deterministic, while near-ties stay graded. The evidence itself is unchanged from the current best account (the person's own second-order picture of a fair coin weighed against a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips, per flip with pseudo-flip dilution, with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). This addresses the FDR-surviving critique that people pick the more-switching sequence in long low-switch pairs more decisively than the logistic model predicts.
- **short_sequence_pseudoflip_dilution**: Refinement of the incumbent `heavy_tailed_signed_sensitivity_motif` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — weighing the evidence per flip, with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change is in how the evidence is weighed per flip: people do not judge a very short sequence on its few flips alone but read its impression as if diluted by a handful of imagined, unremarkable flips (a fitted pseudo-length added to the real length), so the per-flip weighting no longer inflates the evidence of 2- and 3-flip sequences — people still prefer the more-switching sequence in very short pairs, but less decisively than a pure per-flip scaling implies, addressing the critique that in pairs of length 2–3 people choose the more-switching sequence less often than the incumbent predicts.
- **gamblers_run_signed_sensitivity_motif**: Refinement of `heavy_tailed_signed_sensitivity_motif` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — the evidence weighed per flip, with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change, grafted from `gamblers_run_second_order_side_habit`, is that people's picture of a fair coin also holds a run-length gambler's fallacy: the longer the current run has lasted beyond two flips, the more they expect chance to switch, so a long streak looks improbable under chance roughly quadratically in its length and the first one or two breaks of a long streak make a sequence look much more random than further switches do. This addresses the FDR-surviving critique that, among long pairs where both sequences have at most two switches, people choose the more-switching sequence more often than the best model predicts.
- **heavy_tailed_signed_sensitivity_motif**: Refinement of `attentive_lapse_heads_rigged_motif` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — the evidence weighed per flip, with each person's side habit deciding near-ties, and a person-specific share of trials answered at random). The single change is in how people depart from the shared judgment: instead of occasionally guessing at random, which can lower a person's agreement with the majority only down to chance, each person weighs the regularity evidence with their own sensitivity drawn from a heavy-tailed population that can cross zero — most people near the group's typical sensitivity, a few nearly indifferent, and a few reversed, systematically picking the sequence that looks less random to everyone else. This addresses the FDR-surviving critique that participants' agreement with the majority varies across people more than the lapse model produces, since some participants agree with the majority well below chance.
