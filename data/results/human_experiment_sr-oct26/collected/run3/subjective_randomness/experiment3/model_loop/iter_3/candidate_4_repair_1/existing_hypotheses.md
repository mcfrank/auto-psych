# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## short_sequence_pseudoflip_dilution  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3566.8

Refinement of the incumbent `heavy_tailed_signed_sensitivity_motif` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — weighing the evidence per flip, with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). The single change is in how the evidence is weighed per flip: people do not judge a very short sequence on its few flips alone but read its impression as if diluted by a handful of imagined, unremarkable flips (a fitted pseudo-length added to the real length), so the per-flip weighting no longer inflates the evidence of 2- and 3-flip sequences — people still prefer the more-switching sequence in very short pairs, but less decisively than a pure per-flip scaling implies, addressing the critique that in pairs of length 2–3 people choose the more-switching sequence less often than the incumbent predicts.

## heavy_tailed_signed_sensitivity_motif  — rank 1, 1.4 ± 0.5 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3568.2

Refinement of `attentive_lapse_heads_rigged_motif` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — the evidence weighed per flip, with each person's side habit deciding near-ties, and a person-specific share of trials answered at random). The single change is in how people depart from the shared judgment: instead of occasionally guessing at random, which can lower a person's agreement with the majority only down to chance, each person weighs the regularity evidence with their own sensitivity drawn from a heavy-tailed population that can cross zero — most people near the group's typical sensitivity, a few nearly indifferent, and a few reversed, systematically picking the sequence that looks less random to everyone else. This addresses the FDR-surviving critique that participants' agreement with the majority varies across people more than the lapse model produces, since some participants agree with the majority well below chance.

## person_length_scale_reversed_gamblers  — rank 2, 6.6 ± 10.3 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3573.5

Refinement of `reversed_mapping_second_order_gamblers` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order, gambler's-fallacy model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a biased coin, a repeating motif with slips, with their own motif suspicion and switch belief — with a side habit and a person-specific share of trials answered the wrong way round). ## fifo_memory_buffer_judgment  — rank 4, 28.6 ± 8.6 nats behind the best (3.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3595.5

People read a sequence flip by flip into a limited first-in-first-out working-memory buffer: once the buffer is full, each new flip pushes out the oldest, so the randomness judgment is made only on the last few flips they still hold, with a buffer capacity that varies from trial to trial around a fitted typical span. On what they hold, they judge randomness as in the current best account — how much better their own second-order picture of a fair coin explains the remembered flips than the regular generators they suspect — so on trials where the buffer overflows, streaks, imbalances or patterns confined to the start of a long sequence are forgotten and do not count, while the same features at its end count fully.

## blurred_encoding_chance_vs_regular  — rank 9, 42.8 ± 11.0 nats behind the best (3.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3609.6

People do not register a sequence of coin flips perfectly: they read each flip with some fixed chance of mis-registering it, so their felt evidence that a sequence came from chance (their own second-order, gambler's-fallacy picture of a fair coin) rather than from a regular generator (a switch-biased coin, a biased coin, a repeating short motif with slips) is the evidence averaged over the versions of the sequence they might have registered. This blurred encoding strips perfectly regular sequences (pure streaks, perfect alternation, exact motifs) of much of their extreme "designed" evidence relative to near-regular neighbours one flip away, and it dulls the switch-count preference most in very short pairs — so the model disagrees with the current best model most sharply on exactly regular versus one-slip sequences and on length-2/3 pairs.

## hypothesis_sampling_single_suspect  — rank 10, 42.8 ± 18.8 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3609.7

People judge randomness as Bayesian inference — does their own second-order picture of a fair coin explain the sequence better than the regular ways it could have been made (a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin, a short motif repeated with slips) — but with one resource limit: on each trial they do not weigh all those regular explanations together, they bring a single one to mind (sampled with the generators' prior weights, each person with their own readiness to think of a motif) and judge both sequences of the pair only against that one. So a sequence that is regular under just one explanation (for example a short alternation, which only a motif explains, or a lopsided pair of flips, which only a trick coin explains) is condemned only on the trials where that explanation happens to come to mind, making choices on such pairs less consistent than the normative average over explanations predicts — most visibly on very short pairs.

## structural_exemplar_designs  — rank 20, 636.8 ± 61.3 nats behind the best (10.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4203.6

People judge randomness by similarity to a few remembered "designed" sequences — a streak, perfect alternation, and short repeated patterns (such as HHTT…, HHT…, HHHT…) — stored not as exact strings of heads and tails but as their rhythm of repeats and switches, so a remembered design matches a new sequence at whatever point it starts and whichever face it begins with. A sequence's similarity to each remembered design falls off exponentially with the number of its repeat/switch steps that break that design at the best alignment, each kind of design (streak, alternation, repeated motif) has its own fitted memorability, and the sequence whose summed similarity to the designs is smaller looks more random and is chosen, with people differing in how decisively they act on this impression and in a habitual side preference at the response stage.

## encoding_cost_chunk_parse  — rank 21, 745.1 ± 73.8 nats behind the best (10.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4312.0

People judge randomness by how hard a sequence would be to hold in memory: they encode it in the most economical way as a short list of chunks, each chunk either a streak of one face or a stretch of strict alternation, and a sequence that needs a costlier chunk code looks more random. Holding an alternating chunk costs a fitted amount relative to holding a streak chunk, so how a sequence is parsed — and therefore how random it looks — depends on that cost, and each person acts on the felt encoding difficulty with their own decisiveness and side habit.

## tally_gist_typicality  — rank 22, 879.9 ± 95.4 nats behind the best (9.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4446.8

People judge randomness by keeping a running tally of heads minus tails as they read a sequence and summarising its path by two things only: how far the lead ever strays from balance (the largest lead either way) and how many times the tally comes back to a tie. A sequence looks random to the extent that this tally gist is a typical one for their own imagined fair coin (each person with their own belief about how often a fair coin switches), so the route the tally takes matters, not the final count: a sequence that drifts far and rarely returns to a tie, or one whose tally is pinned unusually tightly to balance for that person's coin, looks less random.

## lempel_ziv_novelty_parse  — rank 23, 1098.1 ± 115.1 nats behind the best (9.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4665.0

People judge randomness by how compressible a sequence is when read once from left to right: they parse it into the shortest list of new "phrases", each phrase being the shortest stretch not already seen earlier in the sequence (Lempel-Ziv parsing), and a sequence that keeps producing new phrases looks random while one that keeps repeating what came before (streaks, alternation, repeated motifs) looks designed. The phrase count is judged relative to what is typical for a fair coin at that length, and the sequence with the higher relative phrase count is chosen, with people differing in how decisively they act on it and in a habitual side preference.

## most_surprising_chain_maximum  — no comparison row

People judge how random a sequence looks by its single most striking stretch: the longest unbroken chain of repeats (a streak) or the longest unbroken chain of switches (strict alternation), whichever would be the more surprising to see in a sequence of that length from their own picture of a fair coin, which may believe a coin switches more (or less) than half the time. Only that one most improbable stretch counts — how many switches or how much balance the rest of the sequence has does not — so the first one or two breaks of a long streak, which cut its longest run sharply, make a sequence look much more random, and the sequence whose most striking stretch is less improbable is chosen, with people differing in decisiveness and side habit.

## thurstonian_probit_decision  — no comparison row

The choice is made by a Thurstonian comparison: each sequence's felt randomness is read with Gaussian internal noise, and the person picks whichever noisy impression is larger. Choice probability therefore follows a normal (probit) curve of the evidence difference instead of a logistic one, so its tails are thinner: on clear-cut pairs, such as a long pure streak against a sequence with one or two breaks, choices become close to deterministic, while near-ties stay graded. The evidence itself is unchanged from the current best account (the person's own second-order picture of a fair coin weighed against a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips, per flip with pseudo-flip dilution, with heavy-tailed signed person sensitivity and a side habit). This addresses the FDR-surviving critique that people pick the more-switching sequence in long low-switch pairs more decisively than the logistic model predicts.
