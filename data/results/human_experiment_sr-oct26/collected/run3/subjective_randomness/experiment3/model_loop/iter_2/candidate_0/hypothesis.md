People read a sequence flip by flip into a limited working-memory buffer: once the buffer is full, each new flip pushes out the oldest, so the randomness judgment is made only on the last few flips they still hold (a capacity of roughly four to seven flips that varies from trial to trial around a fitted typical span). On what they hold, they judge randomness as in the current best account — how much better their own second-order picture of a fair coin explains the remembered flips than the regular generators they suspect — so streaks, imbalances or patterns confined to the start of a long sequence are forgotten and barely count, while the same features at its end count fully.
