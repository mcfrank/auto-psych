# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## gamblers_second_order_person_lapse  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3602.5

Refinement of the incumbent `gamblers_run_second_order_side_habit`: people still judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order, gambler's-fallacy model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect (a switch-biased coin, a biased coin, a repeating short motif with slips, with each person's own motif suspicion), the evidence weighed per flip by a fitted power of length and each person's side habit deciding near-ties. The single change is that people differ in how often they disengage: on a person-specific share of trials (drawn from a population, so a few participants are near-random responders while most rarely lapse) a participant does not judge the pair at all and picks Left or Right at random, which caps how consistently that person agrees with the majority even on clear-cut pairs — addressing the critique that participants differ in their rate of agreeing with the majority more than graded person-level sensitivity produces.

## attentive_lapse_heads_rigged_motif  — rank 1, 2.8 ± 3.3 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3605.3

Refinement of `heads_rigged_second_order_side_habit` (people judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect — a switch-biased coin, a heads-favoured trick coin and a repeating motif with slips — the evidence weighed per flip, with each person's side habit deciding near-ties). ## person_lapse_second_order_chance  — rank 2, 3.4 ± 4.2 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3606.0

People judge each sequence exactly as in the simplest current account (their own second-order model of a fair coin, with a person-specific believed switch rate, weighed against a switch-biased coin, a biased coin and a repeating short motif with slips, evidence per flip, person-specific sensitivity), but the decision rule has a person-specific lapse: on some share of trials, which differs from person to person, a participant does not judge at all and picks a side at random. This caps how often anyone agrees with the majority even on clear-cut pairs, so a subgroup of inattentive or near-random responders makes people's agreement with the majority vary more than graded sensitivity alone produces.

Refinement of the incumbent `second_order_motif_side_habit`: people still judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin (a person-specific believed switch rate, shifted right after a switch by a shared amount) explains it better than the regular generators (a switch-biased Markov coin, a biased coin, and a repeating short motif with slips, with each person's own suspicion of motifs), the evidence weighed per flip by a fitted power of length and each person's side habit deciding near-ties. ## switch_gist_own_length_scale  — rank 4, 10.3 ± 6.8 nats behind the best (1.5× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3612.8

Refinement of the incumbent `gamblers_run_second_order_side_habit`: people still judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order, gambler's-fallacy model of a fair coin explains it better than the regular generators they suspect (a switch-biased coin, a biased coin, a repeating short motif with slips, with each person's own motif suspicion), with person-specific sensitivity and side habit. The single change is that the person's quick impression of how often a sequence switches (judged against their own believed switch rate of chance) is weighed on its own length scale, separate from the Bayesian weighing of the regular explanations: the switch-count impression is discounted (or amplified) with sequence length by its own fitted power, so its pull relative to the pattern evidence differs between short and long pairs — addressing the critique that in very short pairs people prefer the more-switching sequence less than the incumbent's single length scaling predicts.

## second_order_chance_motif_suspicion  — rank 7, 11.6 ± 8.1 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3614.1

Refinement of the incumbent `person_specific_motif_suspicion`: people still judge a sequence random to the extent their own model of a fair coin (with a person-specific believed switch rate) explains it better than the regular generators (a switch-biased Markov coin, a biased coin, and a repeating short motif with occasional slips, with each person's own suspicion of motifs), the generators' unknowns averaged out and the evidence weighed per flip by a fitted power of length. ## face_specific_streak_chance_coin  — rank 8, 12.9 ± 8.2 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3615.4

Refinement of `face_biased_second_order_motif`: people still judge a sequence random to the extent their own second-order model of a fair coin (a person-specific believed switch rate, shifted right after a switch by a shared amount) explains it better than the regular generators (a switch-biased Markov coin, a biased coin, and a repeating short motif with slips, with each person's own suspicion of motifs), the evidence weighed per flip by a fitted power of length. The single change is where the coin's label asymmetry lives: instead of believing a fair coin lands on tails more often overall, people believe chance repeats one face more readily than the other (fitted; the data suggest a run of tails is more expected of chance than an equally long run of heads), so a heads-heads repeat is stronger evidence against chance than a tails-tails repeat — locating the FDR-surviving critique (people choose the head-heavier sequence less often than a symmetric model predicts) in streaks of heads rather than in the head count itself.

## structural_exemplar_designs  — rank 12, 601.1 ± 60.2 nats behind the best (10.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4203.6

People judge randomness by similarity to a few remembered "designed" sequences — a streak, perfect alternation, and short repeated patterns (such as HHTT…, HHT…, HHHT…) — stored not as exact strings of heads and tails but as their rhythm of repeats and switches, so a remembered design matches a new sequence at whatever point it starts and whichever face it begins with. A sequence's similarity to each remembered design falls off exponentially with the number of its repeat/switch steps that break that design at the best alignment, each kind of design (streak, alternation, repeated motif) has its own fitted memorability, and the sequence whose summed similarity to the designs is smaller looks more random and is chosen, with people differing in how decisively they act on this impression and in a habitual side preference at the response stage.

## tally_gist_typicality  — rank 13, 844.3 ± 91.2 nats behind the best (9.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4446.8

People judge randomness by keeping a running tally of heads minus tails as they read a sequence and summarising its path by two things only: how far the lead ever strays from balance (the largest lead either way) and how many times the tally comes back to a tie. A sequence looks random to the extent that this tally gist is a typical one for their own imagined fair coin (each person with their own belief about how often a fair coin switches), so the route the tally takes matters, not the final count: a sequence that drifts far and rarely returns to a tie, or one whose tally is pinned unusually tightly to balance for that person's coin, looks less random.
