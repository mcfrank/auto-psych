# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## nearest_decade_digit_common_factor  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3193.5

A refinement of `digit_similarity_with_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with an extra boost for multiples of the examples' common factor, but the one change is that they also compare the target's leading (tens) digit with the examples' and get a familiarity boost from the example whose decade is closest to the target's, a boost that is largest when the target shares that example's decade and decays with each decade further away. This addresses the critique that the model under-predicts "yes" for targets sharing a decade with an example (and, through that, for targets lying among the examples).

## decade_digit_similarity_common_factor  — rank 1, 12.5 ± 4.2 nats behind the best (3.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3206.0

A refinement of `digit_similarity_with_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least) and still boost targets that are multiples of the examples' common factor (3 or more), but the one change is that they also compare the target's leading (tens) digit with each example's, so a target in the same decade as an example is judged similar to it, one in an adjacent decade partly similar, and "yes" also grows with the share of examples it resembles in decade. This addresses the critique that people accept targets sharing a decade with an example (even without a final-digit match) more than the magnitude-blind incumbent predicts.

## digit_similarity_with_common_factor  — rank 2, 14.2 ± 7.8 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3207.7

A refinement of `final_digit_similarity_gradient`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but the one change is that they also notice the largest whole number (3 or more) that divides every example and treat "multiples of that number" as a candidate rule, so a target divisible by the examples' common factor gets an extra, inferred boost toward "yes" whatever its final digit. This addresses the critique that, among parity-matched targets, people accept those divisible by the examples' common divisor (e.g. multiples of 8 after 16, 8, 64) more than the final-digit model predicts.

## decade_digit_range_factor_similarity  — rank 3, 21.8 ± 9.8 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3215.3

A refinement of `common_factor_digit_range_similarity`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with a boost for targets inside the examples' span that fades beyond its edges and an extra push for multiples of the examples' common factor (3 or more). The one change is that people also compare leading digits: the share of examples that lie in the same decade as the target (same tens digit) adds an inferred boost toward "yes", so a target like 34 after 31 is accepted more than its final digit alone predicts, even when it ends in a different digit. This addresses the critique that, among targets sharing no final digit with any example, those in an example's decade are accepted more than the best model predicts.

## common_factor_digit_range_similarity  — rank 4, 28.5 ± 11.1 nats behind the best (2.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3222.0

A refinement of `digit_similarity_with_range_extension`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with an extra boost for targets inside the examples' span that fades beyond its edges, but the one change is that they also notice when several examples are all multiples of a common factor of 3 or more (their greatest common divisor, e.g. 8 for 16, 8, 64) and infer a "multiples of that factor" rule, giving an inferred extra push toward "yes" to targets that are multiples of it. This addresses the critique that, among parity-matched targets of sets sharing a common divisor, people accept divisible targets more than the digit model predicts.

## iter0_candidate0  — rank 5, 46.4 ± 11.9 nats behind the best (3.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3239.9

People generalize like an ideal Bayesian concept learner — weighing candidate concepts (numbers with the same final digit, the same parity, the same decade, multiples of a number, or a numerical interval) by their prior plausibility and by the size principle — but with one resource limit: they cannot integrate the examples jointly, so they generalize from each example on its own and answer "yes" according to the average of these single-example generalizations. Because evidence never accumulates across examples, judgments do not sharpen as examples are added, a target is accepted in proportion to how many examples individually support it, and a shown example is accepted almost always.

## digit_similarity_with_range_extension  — rank 6, 50.3 ± 14.0 nats behind the best (3.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3243.8

A refinement of `final_digit_similarity_gradient`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but they also treat the span from the smallest to the largest example as territory the program covers, so a target inside that span gets an extra, inferred boost toward "yes" that fades smoothly with how far it lies beyond the span's edges (for a single example, with its distance from that example). The one change is this range-membership component grafted from `range_extension_by_spacing`, addressing the critique that the magnitude-blind digit model under-predicts yes for in-range versus out-of-range targets.

## place_value_grid_similarity  — rank 7, 50.9 ± 13.3 nats behind the best (3.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3244.4

People represent each two-digit number by its place values, as a position on a tens-digit by units-digit grid, and judge the target by its similarity to each example on that grid: similarity is highest for the same final digit, partial for a different final digit of the same parity, and lowest for opposite parity, and it is multiplied down by how many decades apart the target and that example lie. They say "yes" in proportion to the target's average place-value similarity to the examples, so a final-digit match earns credit mainly when it falls in the same or a nearby decade as the matching example. This disagrees most sharply with the magnitude-blind final-digit model on digit-matched targets several decades away from every matching example (e.g. 93 after 13), which it predicts are rejected far more often than nearby digit-matched targets (e.g. 23).

## proximity_cued_digit_retrieval  — rank 8, 52.6 ± 12.8 nats behind the best (4.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3246.1

People compare the target's final digit with the final digits of the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but the target acts as a retrieval cue: examples close to it in magnitude come to mind readily, while numerically distant examples are only sometimes recalled for the comparison. So "yes" grows with the final-digit similarity to the examples that are actually retrieved, which makes a digit match with a nearby example count for more than the same match with a far-away one.

## digit_similarity_with_span_falloff  — rank 9, 59.4 ± 13.9 nats behind the best (4.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3252.9

A refinement of `final_digit_similarity_gradient`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but the one change is that they also treat the span from the smallest to the largest example as covered by the program, so their willingness to say "yes" falls off steadily with how far the target lies beyond that span (with no penalty anywhere inside it). This addresses the critique that people accept targets inside the examples' range more than the magnitude-blind digit model predicts.

## digitwise_common_feature_matching  — rank 10, 66.9 ± 13.8 nats behind the best (4.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3260.4

People read each number as a string of two digit features, a tens digit (its decade) and a units digit together with that digit's parity, and judge the target by Tversky-style common-feature matching: every feature the target shares with an example (same decade, same final digit, same parity) adds its own independent increment of support for "yes", averaged over the examples. Because the features add rather than multiply, a target in the same decade as an example gains credit even when its final digit differs (e.g. 17 after 12), and a final-digit match earns credit however many decades away it lies.

## final_digit_similarity_gradient  — rank 11, 75.6 ± 14.4 nats behind the best (5.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3269.1

People ignore magnitude and judge the target by how much its final digit resembles the final digits of the examples: a target ending in the same digit as an example is maximally similar to it, one ending in a different digit of the same parity (odd/even) is partly similar, and one of the opposite parity is least similar. They say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to all the examples shown, so the answer is graded by the share of examples it resembles rather than by any rule that every example must obey, and numerically close targets get no credit for closeness.

## serial_position_digit_similarity  — rank 12, 77.2 ± 14.3 nats behind the best (5.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3270.7

People compare the target's final digit with the final digits of the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but they do not hold every example equally strongly in memory: how much each example counts in the comparison depends on its serial position in the order shown, changing steadily from first to last (recency if later examples are held more strongly, primacy if earlier ones are). So "yes" grows with a position-weighted average of final-digit similarity, and the same numbers shown in a different order change which digit matches drive acceptance.

## recency_faded_magnitude_anchor  — rank 13, 84.3 ± 13.5 nats behind the best (6.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3277.8

People keep the final digit of every example as durable gist, and say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least); but their memory of each example's exact magnitude fades with every later example shown, so the most recent example acts as a magnitude anchor. Targets numerically close to the remembered magnitudes, above all to the last example shown, get an extra push toward "yes", so the same numbers in a different order shift which neighbourhood of the number line is accepted.
