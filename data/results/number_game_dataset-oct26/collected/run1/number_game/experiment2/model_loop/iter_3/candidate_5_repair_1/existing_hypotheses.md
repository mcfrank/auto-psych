# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## final_digit_rule_suppresses_parity  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3169.2

A refinement of `decade_band_digit_common_factor` (final-digit similarity, common-factor boost, decade-band familiarity all kept). The one change: the partial credit people give a target for merely sharing the examples' parity is not fixed but shrinks with how consistently the examples share one final digit — when the examples all end in the same digit, people infer an "ends in that digit" rule and give little or no credit to same-parity targets ending in a different digit, while for examples with varied final digits parity still earns its usual partial credit. This addresses the critique that, in sets whose examples share a final digit, people accept parity-only matches far less than the model predicts.

## shared_digit_rule_preempts_parity  — rank 1, 2.1 ± 3.1 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3171.4

A refinement of `decade_band_digit_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples, with a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor and full decade-band familiarity fading outside the band. The one change is rule abstraction on the final digit: when two or more examples all end in the same digit, people abstract the explicit rule "ends in that digit", and this rule pre-empts the graded parity similarity, so a target of the same parity but a different final digit loses (by an inferred share) the partial credit it would otherwise get. The sharpest disagreement with the incumbent lives in uniform-final-digit sets (e.g. 13, 43, 73): the incumbent accepts same-parity, different-digit targets (e.g. 15) far more than opposite-parity ones, whereas this model predicts they are rejected about as often.

## shared_digit_sharpened_decade_band  — rank 2, 3.3 ± 5.1 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3172.5

A refinement of `diminishing_similarity_decade_band`: people still judge the target by its final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with diminishing returns from each further resembling example, a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor, and full leading-digit familiarity inside the band of decades the examples span, fading outside it. The one change is that when every example ends in the same final digit, people take that shared digit as the program's rule and, by a size-principle-like sharpening, give a different digit of the same parity less and less partial credit with each additional example that confirms the shared digit. This addresses the critique that, in sets whose examples all share one final digit, people accept parity-only matches far less than the model predicts.

## variability_tuned_exemplar_gradient  — rank 3, 4.8 ± 4.9 nats behind the best (1.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3174.0

People judge the target by its similarity to the stored examples, and the breadth of their generalization around those exemplars is set by how variable the exemplars are (the category-variability effect): when the examples all end in the same digit, the similarity gradient on final digit is tight, so a target that merely shares their parity gets almost no credit, whereas when the examples' final digits vary, the gradient is broad and same-parity targets are accepted almost as readily as digit matches. Apart from this variability-tuned digit gradient, the comparison is the current best model's (final-digit similarity averaged over examples, full leading-digit familiarity within the examples' band of decades fading outside it, and a boost for multiples of their common factor).

## decade_band_digit_common_factor  — rank 4, 8.3 ± 4.9 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3177.5

A refinement of `nearest_decade_digit_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with an extra boost for multiples of the examples' common factor. The one change is the metric for magnitude similarity: people treat the whole band of decades running from the lowest example's decade to the highest example's decade as familiar territory. So a target anywhere in that band, including decades between the examples that no example occupies, gets the full leading-digit boost, and the boost decays with each decade the target lies outside the band. This addresses the critique that people accept targets inside the examples' range more than targets outside it (range interpolation), while accepting adjacent-decade near-misses outside the band less than the nearest-decade model predicts.

## accumulated_digit_mismatch_evidence  — rank 5, 9.1 ± 5.4 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3178.4

People treat each example as a separate piece of evidence about the program and accumulate that evidence across examples, rather than merely averaging their resemblance to them: every example whose final digit the target fails to share costs some credibility (a little for a same-parity mismatch, more for an opposite-parity one), and these costs pile up with the number of examples shown (with an inferred degree of accumulation between pure averaging and full summing), so judgments sharpen as more examples agree. Thus a target that breaks a final digit shared by three examples (e.g. 69 after 59, 89, 79) is rejected more firmly than one breaking a single example's digit, while decade-band familiarity and membership in the examples' common-factor multiples still shift the answer as in the incumbent.

## diminishing_similarity_decade_band  — rank 6, 10.9 ± 6.1 nats behind the best (1.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3180.1

A refinement of `decade_band_digit_common_factor`: people still judge the target by its final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor and full leading-digit familiarity anywhere in the band of decades the examples span, fading with each decade outside it. The one change is that support from similar examples has diminishing returns: the first resembling example raises the willingness to say "yes" most, and each further one adds less (a concave, power-law transform of the average similarity with an inferred exponent), rather than every resembling example adding an equal share. So a target resembling just one of several examples, such as a single final-digit match, is accepted more, relative to a target resembling all of them, than the linear-share model predicts.

## log_compressed_decade_band  — rank 7, 15.3 ± 6.6 nats behind the best (2.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3184.5

A refinement of `decade_band_digit_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor and full familiarity anywhere in the band of decades the examples span. The one change is that familiarity fades outside that band with distance on a compressed, logarithmic mental number line rather than by counting decades. So a target the same number of decades above the band (e.g. 90 after 60, 70) stays more familiar than one the same number of decades below it (e.g. 30 after 60, 70), and leaving a band of small numbers costs far more than leaving a band of large ones.

## nearest_decade_digit_factor_span_fill  — rank 8, 17.3 ± 6.0 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3186.5

A refinement of `nearest_decade_digit_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with an extra boost for multiples of the examples' common factor and a familiarity boost from the example whose decade is closest to the target's. The one change, a range-interpolation component taken from `common_factor_digit_range_similarity`, is that people also treat the numerical span from the smallest to the largest example as territory the program covers, so a target lying inside that span gets an extra push toward "yes" that fades smoothly with how far (in raw numerical distance, regardless of decade) the target lies beyond the span's edges. This addresses the critique that the incumbent under-produces interval generalization: targets inside the examples' range are accepted more than targets outside it even when they share no final digit with any example.

## divisor_lattice_size_principle  — rank 9, 21.0 ± 9.5 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3190.2

A refinement of `nearest_decade_digit_common_factor` that changes its space of arithmetic concepts: instead of entertaining only one rule, "multiples of the examples' greatest common factor (3 or more)", people entertain every "multiples of d" concept that all the examples obey (d = 2 and up, every common divisor, e.g. multiples of 2, 4 and 8 after 16, 8, 64), and weigh them by the size principle, so smaller, more specific concepts are favoured more strongly the more examples there are, with an inferred strength. A target's arithmetic boost toward "yes" is the resulting graded belief that it lies in the inferred multiples concept, alongside the unchanged final-digit similarity and nearest-decade familiarity.

## in_range_nearest_decade_digit_factor  — rank 10, 22.1 ± 6.6 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3191.4

A refinement of `nearest_decade_digit_common_factor`: people still say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same final digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), with a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor and a familiarity boost from the example in the nearest decade. The one change is that, when several examples are shown, people also take the stretch of the number line between the smallest and largest example as ground the program covers, so a target lying strictly inside the examples' range gets an extra, inferred boost toward "yes", whatever its digits or decade. ## nearest_decade_digit_common_factor  — rank 11, 24.3 ± 8.3 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3193.5

## decade_digit_range_factor_similarity  — rank 14, 46.1 ± 9.7 nats behind the best (4.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3215.3

## iter0_candidate0  — rank 16, 70.7 ± 14.2 nats behind the best (5.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3239.9

People generalize like an ideal Bayesian concept learner — weighing candidate concepts (numbers with the same final digit, the same parity, the same decade, multiples of a number, or a numerical interval) by their prior plausibility and by the size principle — but with one resource limit: they cannot integrate the examples jointly, so they generalize from each example on its own and answer "yes" according to the average of these single-example generalizations. Because evidence never accumulates across examples, judgments do not sharpen as examples are added, a target is accepted in proportion to how many examples individually support it, and a shown example is accepted almost always.

## digit_similarity_with_range_extension  — rank 17, 74.6 ± 13.4 nats behind the best (5.6× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3243.8

## place_value_grid_similarity  — rank 18, 75.1 ± 15.5 nats behind the best (4.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3244.4

People represent each two-digit number by its place values, as a position on a tens-digit by units-digit grid, and judge the target by its similarity to each example on that grid: similarity is highest for the same final digit, partial for a different final digit of the same parity, and lowest for opposite parity, and it is multiplied down by how many decades apart the target and that example lie. They say "yes" in proportion to the target's average place-value similarity to the examples, so a final-digit match earns credit mainly when it falls in the same or a nearby decade as the matching example. This disagrees most sharply with the magnitude-blind final-digit model on digit-matched targets several decades away from every matching example (e.g. 93 after 13), which it predicts are rejected far more often than nearby digit-matched targets (e.g. 23).

## proximity_cued_digit_retrieval  — rank 19, 76.9 ± 15.1 nats behind the best (5.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3246.1

People compare the target's final digit with the final digits of the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but the target acts as a retrieval cue: examples close to it in magnitude come to mind readily, while numerically distant examples are only sometimes recalled for the comparison. So "yes" grows with the final-digit similarity to the examples that are actually retrieved, which makes a digit match with a nearby example count for more than the same match with a far-away one.

## digitwise_common_feature_matching  — rank 21, 91.2 ± 15.9 nats behind the best (5.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3260.4

People read each number as a string of two digit features, a tens digit (its decade) and a units digit together with that digit's parity, and judge the target by Tversky-style common-feature matching: every feature the target shares with an example (same decade, same final digit, same parity) adds its own independent increment of support for "yes", averaged over the examples. Because the features add rather than multiply, a target in the same decade as an example gains credit even when its final digit differs (e.g. 17 after 12), and a final-digit match earns credit however many decades away it lies.

## final_digit_similarity_gradient  — rank 22, 99.9 ± 16.4 nats behind the best (6.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3269.1

People ignore magnitude and judge the target by how much its final digit resembles the final digits of the examples: a target ending in the same digit as an example is maximally similar to it, one ending in a different digit of the same parity (odd/even) is partly similar, and one of the opposite parity is least similar. They say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to all the examples shown, so the answer is graded by the share of examples it resembles rather than by any rule that every example must obey, and numerically close targets get no credit for closeness.

## serial_position_digit_similarity  — rank 23, 101.5 ± 16.4 nats behind the best (6.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3270.7

People compare the target's final digit with the final digits of the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least), but they do not hold every example equally strongly in memory: how much each example counts in the comparison depends on its serial position in the order shown, changing steadily from first to last (recency if later examples are held more strongly, primacy if earlier ones are). So "yes" grows with a position-weighted average of final-digit similarity, and the same numbers shown in a different order change which digit matches drive acceptance.

## recency_faded_magnitude_anchor  — rank 24, 108.6 ± 15.8 nats behind the best (6.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3277.8

People keep the final digit of every example as durable gist, and say "yes" in proportion to the target's average final-digit similarity to the examples (same digit most similar, same parity partly, opposite parity least); but their memory of each example's exact magnitude fades with every later example shown, so the most recent example acts as a magnitude anchor. Targets numerically close to the remembered magnitudes, above all to the last example shown, get an extra push toward "yes", so the same numbers in a different order shift which neighbourhood of the number line is accepted.

## example_count_diluted_decade_familiarity  — no comparison row

People judge the target as the current best model says (final-digit similarity to the examples with parity credit suppressed when the examples share one final digit, a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor, and familiarity with the band of decades the examples span), but how much weight they put on that magnitude (decade) familiarity depends on how many examples they saw: with a lone example there is no relational structure to extract, so people lean heavily on "numbers near that one" and readily accept other numbers in its decade, while each additional example supplies digit and arithmetic structure that draws attention away from magnitude, so the decade-familiarity boost is diluted as the number of examples grows. This addresses the critique that, after a single example, people accept same-decade targets with a different final digit far more than the model predicts.

## attention_shift_magnitude_to_digit  — no comparison row

People divide attention between a number's magnitude (its decade) and its final digit, and that split is set by how many examples they have read: a single example offers no regularity among digits to notice, so people judge mostly by magnitude and readily accept other numbers in the example's decade, but each further example lets digit regularities be detected and draws attention away from magnitude toward the final digit. Apart from this set-size-driven attention shift, judgments follow the current best model (final-digit similarity with parity credit suppressed by a shared final digit, decade-band familiarity, and a common-factor boost).

## size_principle_decade_band  — no comparison row

People generalize as a Bayesian learner would from the band of decades the examples span (with final-digit similarity, parity credit suppressed by a shared final digit, and a common-factor boost as in the current best model), applying the size principle to that magnitude concept: the narrower the band of decades the examples occupy, the more strongly a target inside it is judged likely, so a single example or a set confined to one decade produces strong within-decade generalization while a band covering many decades confers little. The one distortion is that the size principle is applied with an inferred, partial strength rather than in full, so familiarity falls with the band's width by a weaker-than-normative power.
