# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## shortfall_from_mutual_similarity  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4725.5

A refinement of `mutual_similarity_contrast_standard`: people still hold the target to a standard set by how much the examples resemble one another on the final-digit metric (same digit most alike, same parity partly, opposite parity least). The one change is in how the target is compared with that standard: what counts against a "yes" is the shortfall, how far the target's own similarity to the examples falls below their similarity to one another, and nothing else. A target that resembles the examples at least as much as they resemble each other pays no penalty, partial matches pay only for the gap that is left, and in the incumbent's model a target with no digit match pays the full mutual similarity regardless of its parity credit, which no longer happens. Rule-pre-empted parity credit, the common-factor boost and rule-gated decade-band familiarity are kept unchanged.

## contains_digit_concept  — rank 1, 4.7 ± 7.1 nats behind the best (0.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4730.3

People's space of candidate concepts includes "numbers containing the digit d" — a digit appearing anywhere in a number, tens or units place — and not only "numbers ending in d". So a target that shares some digit with an example only in a different position (e.g. 27 or 71 after 72, or 41 after 14) gets extra support toward "yes", in proportion to the share of examples it shares a digit with this way. Apart from this added contains-a-digit concept, judgments follow the current best model (final-digit similarity measured against the examples' own mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a common-factor boost, and rule-gated decade-band familiarity).

## mutual_similarity_contrast_standard  — rank 2, 10.9 ± 5.4 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4736.4

People judge a target's resemblance to the examples relative to how much the examples resemble one another on the same final-digit metric (same digit most alike, same parity partly, opposite parity least): the examples' mutual similarity sets the standard a target must meet. When the examples closely resemble each other (shared digits or parity), a target that matches none of their final digits falls well short of that standard and is rejected. When the examples share little (mixed digits and parities), even a dissimilar target is about as alike to them as they are to each other, so people accept broadly. Apart from this, judgments follow the current best model: rule-pre-empted parity credit, a common-factor boost, and rule-gated decade-band familiarity.

## attentional_lapse_biased_guess  — rank 3, 12.0 ± 10.1 nats behind the best (1.2× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4737.5

People judge the target with the same simple comparison as the current rule-gated model (final-digit similarity with parity credit pre-empted by a shared final-digit rule, a common-factor boost, and decade-band familiarity that a detected rule pre-empts), but they do not always act on that judgment: on an inferred fraction of trials their attention lapses and they answer by an unconsidered guess that is biased toward "yes" or "no" by an inferred amount, independent of the set and target. This puts a floor under "yes" for targets the comparison rejects and a ceiling under it for targets it strongly accepts, including the examples themselves.

## round_digit_kinship_rule_gated  — rank 4, 12.1 ± 14.2 nats behind the best (0.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4737.7

A refinement of `rule_gated_decade_familiarity` (final-digit similarity averaged over the examples with "ends in that digit" rule pre-empting parity credit, a common-factor boost, and decade-band familiarity that an explicit rule pre-empts, all kept). The one change is to the final-digit similarity metric itself: besides parity, people treat the "round" digits 0 and 5 as kin, so a target ending in 0 is judged partly similar to an example ending in 5 (and vice versa) even though their parities differ, with that kinship credit inferred. So after examples like 5, 65 or 90, 100, 5, round targets such as 80, 50 or 100 are accepted far more than parity-based similarity predicts.

## recognized_example_contrast_standard  — rank 5, 12.1 ± 5.5 nats behind the best (2.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4737.7

A refinement of `mutual_similarity_contrast_standard` (final-digit similarity judged against the examples' own mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a common-factor boost and rule-gated decade-band familiarity, all kept). The one change is episodic recognition: when the target is literally one of the numbers just shown, people recognise it as something the program has already produced and accept it with an extra, inferred boost on top of its similarity, so a shown example is accepted more than an equally similar unseen number (e.g. 64 after 4, 64, 25, 81 more than 84, and 12 after 4, 16, 12 more than other numbers ending in 2), regardless of how many examples were shown.

## rule_gated_decade_familiarity  — rank 6, 15.2 ± 9.6 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4740.7

A refinement of `shared_digit_rule_preempts_parity` (final-digit similarity averaged over the examples, an abstracted "ends in that digit" rule pre-empting parity credit, a common-factor boost and decade-band familiarity all kept). The one change is that magnitude familiarity is a fallback: when people detect an explicit rule linking the examples — they all end in the same digit, or are all multiples of a common factor of 3 or more — the rule pre-empts "numbers near these", and the decade-band familiarity boost is cut by an inferred share; when the set has no detectable rule (a lone example, or examples with no shared digit or factor), people lean on magnitude neighbourhood at full strength. This addresses the critiques that people accept same-decade, different-digit targets after a single example more than predicted, and that generalization breadth differs between sets more than the model produces.

## position_weighted_rule_gated_digits  — rank 7, 17.4 ± 10.0 nats behind the best (1.7× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4742.9

People judge the target as the current best model says (final-digit similarity to the examples with parity credit pre-empted by a shared final digit, a boost for multiples of the examples' common factor, and decade-band familiarity that an explicit rule pre-empts), but they do not hold every example equally in memory when comparing final digits: each example's weight in the digit comparison changes steadily with its serial position, from the first shown to the last (recency if later examples count more, primacy if the first sets the impression), with the direction and strength inferred. So a target sharing the final digit of the most heavily weighted example is accepted more than one matching an equally similar example in a weaker position, and the same numbers in a different order shift which digit matches drive "yes".

## ruleless_set_anything_goes_floor  — rank 8, 18.3 ± 9.8 nats behind the best (1.9× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4743.8

A refinement of `rule_gated_decade_familiarity` (final-digit similarity with rule-pre-empted parity credit, common-factor boost, and decade-band familiarity cut back when an explicit rule is found, all kept). The one change: when two or more examples show no detectable rule (mixed final digits and no common factor of 3 or more), people entertain, with an inferred probability, the hypothesis that the program simply produces any number at all; under that "anything goes" belief they say "yes" regardless of the target, so rule-less multi-example sets get a floor of acceptance that lifts most the targets that resemble the examples least (far, unmatched targets), while lone examples and rule-bearing sets keep the incumbent's judgments. This addresses the critique that people accept far, digit-unmatched targets of rule-less sets more than predicted and that generalization breadth differs between sets more than the model produces.

## rule_gated_mixed_parity_criterion  — rank 9, 20.1 ± 9.4 nats behind the best (2.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4745.6

A refinement of `mixed_parity_loosens_criterion` (final-digit similarity with parity credit suppressed by a shared final digit, a common-factor boost, decade-band familiarity, and a looser "yes" criterion when the examples mix odd and even numbers, all kept). The one change, a component taken from `rule_gated_decade_familiarity`, is that magnitude familiarity is only a fallback: when two or more examples obey an explicit rule (all end in the same digit, or all are multiples of a common factor of 3 or more), the rule pre-empts "numbers near these" and the decade-band boost is cut by an inferred share, whereas rule-less sets and lone examples get it in full. Together with the mixed-parity criterion shift, this predicts that rule-less, mixed-parity sets are accepted broadly — even far from the examples' decades — while rule-bound sets are rejected outside their rule, widening the differences between sets that the critique reports.

## coincidence_graded_rule_preemption  — rank 10, 29.3 ± 10.6 nats behind the best (2.8× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4754.8

A refinement of `rule_gated_decade_familiarity` (final-digit similarity with an "ends in that digit" rule pre-empting parity credit, a common-factor boost, and decade-band familiarity that a detected rule pre-empts, all kept). The one change: rule detection is not all-or-none but graded by how unlikely the examples' shared property would be by coincidence — a shared final digit (1 in 10 per extra example), a common factor g (1 in g per extra example) or merely a shared parity (1 in 2 per extra example), with evidence piling up across examples — and the more suspicious the coincidence, the more firmly people believe in a rule and the more completely it pre-empts "numbers near these" magnitude familiarity. So four examples sharing a digit or a large factor shut off neighbourhood generalization almost entirely, two examples sharing only parity or a factor of 3 barely do, and sets with no shared property at all keep magnitude familiarity in full, spreading generalization breadth across sets more than a binary rule detector does.

## mixed_parity_loosens_criterion  — rank 11, 81.2 ± 15.2 nats behind the best (5.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4806.7

A refinement of `final_digit_rule_suppresses_parity` (final-digit similarity with parity credit suppressed by a shared final digit, common-factor boost and decade-band familiarity all kept). The one change: whether the examples agree in parity sets how broad people think the program is — when the examples mix odd and even numbers, people conclude the program is not bound by any parity or digit rule and lower their criterion, saying "yes" more readily to every target, whereas examples that all share a parity signal a constrained program and keep the criterion strict. This addresses the critique that generalization breadth differs between sets more than the incumbent produces: mixed-parity sets (e.g. 92, 14, 20, 5) are accepted broadly while same-parity sets (e.g. 68, 58) are rejected almost everywhere outside their rule.

## coherence_tuned_attention_gcm  — rank 12, 120.4 ± 19.9 nats behind the best (6.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4845.9

People do not infer a rule; they judge the target by its average similarity to the stored examples, as in an exemplar (Generalized Context) model in which each number is compared with each example on three separately coded aspects — its final digit (same digit most alike, same parity partly), its decade (similarity falling off with decades apart), and whether the two share a divisor of 3 or more. The distinctive claim is that attention across these aspects is tuned by the stored examples themselves: people attend most to whichever aspect the examples cohere on (shared final digits or parity, shared decades, or shared divisors), so a match on that aspect counts heavily and a match on an aspect the examples vary on counts little, while a lone example leaves attention at its default split.
