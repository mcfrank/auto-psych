# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## digit_rule_size_principle_counting  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4689.4

A refinement of `counting_up_from_single_example` (final-digit similarity judged by its shortfall from the examples' mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a privileged round common-factor rule, rule-gated decade-band familiarity and step-costed counting up from single examples, all kept). The one change is a size principle on the "ends in that digit" rule: each further example beyond the second that shares the set's final digit is another suspicious coincidence that firms up belief in the rule, so a target that breaks the shared digit is rejected by an inferred, additional amount per confirming example (e.g. 37 is rejected more after 13, 43, 73, 93 than after 13, 43), whereas the incumbent's mutual-similarity shortfall is the same for two or four uniform examples.

## accumulating_similarity_standard  — rank 1, 2.3 ± 8.4 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4691.7

A refinement of `privileged_named_number_concepts` (final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, the privileged even-number and round-factor rules and rule-gated decade-band familiarity all kept). The one change: the standard a target's final-digit similarity is held to is not a fixed average but is built up as evidence, so each further example that resembles the others makes the standard more binding, and a target falling short of the examples' mutual similarity is penalised more the more examples set that standard (growing with the number of examples by an inferred power). So after 3 or 4 examples that all end in the same digit, targets ending in a different digit are rejected much more firmly than after 2 such examples.

## bayesian_digit_rule_veto  — rank 2, 2.5 ± 7.7 nats behind the best (0.3× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4691.9

A refinement of the incumbent `privileged_named_number_concepts` (shortfall from the examples' mutual final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, the privileged "even numbers" rule, separate round and non-round common-factor boosts, and rule-gated decade-band familiarity, all kept). The one change is a Bayesian size principle on the "ends in digit d" rule: when all the examples share a final digit, people weigh how unlikely that coincidence would be (one in ten for each example after the first) against an inferred prior doubt, and with the resulting belief that the program really is "ends in d" they veto targets ending in any other digit outright. Because that belief saturates as confirming examples accumulate, rule violators are rejected far more after three or four same-digit examples than after two, while targets that keep the digit, and sets without a shared digit, are judged exactly as before.

## digit_rule_size_principle  — rank 3, 5.0 ± 8.2 nats behind the best (0.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4694.4

A refinement of the incumbent `privileged_named_number_concepts` (shortfall from the examples' mutual final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, rule-gated decade-band familiarity, the privileged "even numbers" rule and separate round/non-round common-factor boosts, all kept). The one change is a size principle on the shared-final-digit rule: when every example ends in the same digit, each further consistent example is more evidence that "ends in that digit" is the program's rule rather than a coincidence, so a target ending in a different digit is rejected more firmly the more examples confirm the rule (an inferred extra penalty per confirming example beyond the first). So 69 after 59, 89, 79 is rejected more than 69 after 59, 89, whereas the incumbent's mutual-similarity shortfall treats the two alike.

## privileged_named_number_concepts  — rank 4, 6.8 ± 8.5 nats behind the best (0.8× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4696.2

A refinement of `even_numbers_privileged_rule` (shortfall from the examples' mutual final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, rule-gated decade-band familiarity, and the privileged "even numbers" rule that makes all-even sets reject odd targets extra strongly, all kept). The one change, a component taken from `round_counting_privileged_factor`, is that counting by fives and tens is also a privileged, overlearned concept: when the examples' common factor is a multiple of 5 people embrace "multiples of that factor" with its own, inferred strength, separate from the boost for non-round factors such as 3, 4, 6 or 8. So people favour the culturally named number concepts — evens, and counting by fives and tens — over equally consistent but unnamed ones (odd numbers, multiples of 3, 4 or 8).

## counting_up_from_single_example  — rank 5, 7.8 ± 4.9 nats behind the best (1.6× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4697.2

Besides inducing a rule that fits all the examples, people mentally count up from each example on its own (times-table retrieval: x, 2x, 3x, ...), so a target that this counting reaches from any single example of 3 or more is felt as something the program could produce and gets extra support toward "yes" (e.g. 66 after 33, 3; 48 after 4, 16, 12; 27 after 81, 9), even when it is not a multiple of what all the examples share. Each further counting step costs effort, so this support is strongest for a target that is double an example and fades, at an inferred rate, the more steps it takes to get there; everything else follows the current best model (final-digit similarity judged by its shortfall from the examples' mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a privileged round common-factor rule and rule-gated decade-band familiarity).

## factor_seed_visible_counting  — rank 6, 9.2 ± 6.7 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4698.6

A refinement of the incumbent `round_counting_privileged_factor` (final-digit similarity judged by its shortfall from the examples' mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, separate boosts for round and non-round common-factor rules, and rule-gated decade-band familiarity, all kept). The one change: the "multiples of the examples' common factor" rule is only fully compelling when the factor is itself one of the examples, because then the set looks like a counting sequence whose step is in plain view (e.g. 5, 65 or 3, 63 or 4, 16, 12). When the factor has to be worked out by arithmetic (e.g. 5 behind 75, 95, or 3 behind 66, 93, 51), people often fail to notice the rule, so its boost toward "yes" is cut to an inferred share. So 50, 80 and 100 after 5, 65 are accepted much more than 10, 40 and 50 after 75, 95.

## sequential_digit_streak_confirmation  — rank 7, 12.1 ± 8.6 nats behind the best (1.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4701.5

People read the examples in order and keep a running guess that the program makes numbers "ending in the same digit as the latest example": each new example that ends in the same digit as the one before confirms that guess and makes it firmer, while an example with a different final digit replaces the guess and starts its confidence over. So a target that breaks the final digit of the most recent unbroken run of matching examples is rejected more strongly the longer that run has grown (four same-digit examples more than two, and 72, 13, 43 more than 13, 43, 72), on top of the current best model's judgments (shortfall from the examples' mutual final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, privileged even and round-factor rules, rule-gated decade-band familiarity).

## even_numbers_privileged_rule  — rank 9, 21.9 ± 11.1 nats behind the best (2.0× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -4711.2

"Even numbers" is a privileged, named concept for people but "odd numbers" is not, so when every example shown is even people readily guess the program makes even numbers and reject odd targets more strongly than final-digit similarity alone predicts, whereas an all-odd set brings no "odd numbers" rule to mind and its even targets pay nothing extra. Apart from this, judgments follow the current best model: shortfall from the examples' mutual final-digit similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a common-factor boost and rule-gated decade-band familiarity.

## round_kin_shortfall_standard  — rank 10, 25.6 ± 9.5 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4715.0

A refinement of `shortfall_from_mutual_similarity` (final-digit similarity held to the standard of the examples' own mutual similarity, with only the target's shortfall below that standard counting against "yes", plus rule-pre-empted parity credit, the common-factor boost and rule-gated decade-band familiarity, all kept). The one change, a component taken from `round_digit_kinship_rule_gated`, is to the final-digit similarity metric that both the target and the standard are measured on: people treat the "round" digits 0 and 5 as kin, so a number ending in 0 is partly similar to one ending in 5 despite their opposite parity, with that kinship credit inferred. ## comparison_load_decision_noise  — rank 11, 31.3 ± 11.8 nats behind the best (2.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4720.7

A refinement of `contains_digit_concept` (final-digit similarity judged against the examples' mutual similarity, rule-pre-empted parity credit, a common-factor boost, rule-gated decade-band familiarity and "contains the digit d" support, all kept). The one change is to how decisive the judgment is: comparing the target with each example is a separate mental comparison, and the more examples there are to weigh, the noisier the combined judgment becomes (or, if the inferred direction is reversed, the more examples, the more confident). This addresses the critique that people's judgments are more unanimous per item than the incumbent's lukewarm probabilities, which in the data is concentrated in single-example sets.

## example_count_sharpens_decision  — rank 12, 32.0 ± 10.1 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4721.4

## shortfall_from_mutual_similarity  — rank 13, 36.1 ± 10.3 nats behind the best (3.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4725.5

A refinement of `mutual_similarity_contrast_standard`: people still hold the target to a standard set by how much the examples resemble one another on the final-digit metric (same digit most alike, same parity partly, opposite parity least). The one change is in how the target is compared with that standard: what counts against a "yes" is the shortfall, how far the target's own similarity to the examples falls below their similarity to one another, and nothing else. A target that resembles the examples at least as much as they resemble each other pays no penalty, partial matches pay only for the gap that is left, and in the incumbent's model a target with no digit match pays the full mutual similarity regardless of its parity credit, which no longer happens. ## mutual_similarity_contrast_standard  — rank 15, 47.0 ± 10.5 nats behind the best (4.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4736.4

People judge a target's resemblance to the examples relative to how much the examples resemble one another on the same final-digit metric (same digit most alike, same parity partly, opposite parity least): the examples' mutual similarity sets the standard a target must meet. When the examples closely resemble each other (shared digits or parity), a target that matches none of their final digits falls well short of that standard and is rejected. ## round_counting_sequence_membership  — rank 19, 50.2 ± 11.9 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4739.6

## rule_gated_decade_familiarity  — rank 20, 51.3 ± 12.3 nats behind the best (4.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4740.7

## coincidence_graded_rule_preemption  — rank 24, 65.4 ± 13.3 nats behind the best (4.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4754.8

A refinement of `rule_gated_decade_familiarity` (final-digit similarity with an "ends in that digit" rule pre-empting parity credit, a common-factor boost, and decade-band familiarity that a detected rule pre-empts, all kept). The one change: rule detection is not all-or-none but graded by how unlikely the examples' shared property would be by coincidence — a shared final digit (1 in 10 per extra example), a common factor g (1 in g per extra example) or merely a shared parity (1 in 2 per extra example), with evidence piling up across examples — and the more suspicious the coincidence, the more firmly people believe in a rule and the more completely it pre-empts "numbers near these" magnitude familiarity. ## mixed_parity_loosens_criterion  — rank 25, 117.3 ± 17.9 nats behind the best (6.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4806.7

The one change: whether the examples agree in parity sets how broad people think the program is — when the examples mix odd and even numbers, people conclude the program is not bound by any parity or digit rule and lower their criterion, saying "yes" more readily to every target, whereas examples that all share a parity signal a constrained program and keep the criterion strict. ## coherence_tuned_attention_gcm  — rank 26, 156.5 ± 21.6 nats behind the best (7.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4845.9

People do not infer a rule; they judge the target by its average similarity to the stored examples, as in an exemplar (Generalized Context) model in which each number is compared with each example on three separately coded aspects — its final digit (same digit most alike, same parity partly), its decade (similarity falling off with decades apart), and whether the two share a divisor of 3 or more. The distinctive claim is that attention across these aspects is tuned by the stored examples themselves: people attend most to whichever aspect the examples cohere on (shared final digits or parity, shared decades, or shared divisors), so a match on that aspect counts heavily and a match on an aspect the examples vary on counts little, while a lone example leaves attention at its default split.

## mental_simulation_example_mutation  — rank 27, 240.4 ± 25.7 nats behind the best (9.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4929.8

People answer by mental simulation rather than by comparing the target with the examples or weighing rules: they imagine a handful of further outputs of the program, each made by taking one of the examples and altering it in a typical way (keeping its final digit and changing its decade, keeping its decade and changing its final digit within or across parity, stepping through multiples of the examples' common factor, or just a random number), and they say "yes" if the target turns up among the numbers they imagined. Because acceptance depends on whether the target is generated at least once in a few imagined samples, it saturates for easily generated targets and is near zero for hard-to-generate ones, making judgments more all-or-none per item than a graded similarity response.

## conservative_bayesian_concept_updating  — rank 28, 291.8 ± 27.3 nats behind the best (10.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4981.2

People generalize as an ideal Bayesian concept learner whose candidate concepts are the familiar ways of describing numbers: "ends in digit d", "even" or "odd", "multiples of k", a band of consecutive decades, and "any number at all". Each concept is weighed by its prior plausibility and by how well it explains the examples, with smaller concepts favoured (the size principle). The one distortion is conservatism: people update less than Bayes' rule says, so each example counts as only an inferred fraction of a full random draw from the concept. As a result, broad concepts such as parity, wide decade bands or "any number" keep more belief after several examples than an ideal learner would leave them. Sets whose examples fit no small concept therefore stay broadly accepted, while sets that fit one tightly are judged sharply.

## conjunctive_example_verification  — rank 29, 735.1 ± 47.5 nats behind the best (15.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -5424.5

People decide by a conjunctive verification rule rather than by weighing overall resemblance: they check the target against each example in turn, and say "yes" only if it passes every one of these checks, where each check is passed readily when the target resembles that example (same final digit, same parity, nearby decade, or a multiple of the examples' common factor) and is sometimes failed otherwise. Because every additional example is one more check a rule-violating target can fail, targets that break a final digit shared by the examples are rejected more and more firmly as consistent examples accumulate (a size-principle-like sharpening produced by the decision rule), while targets that fit every example stay accepted.
