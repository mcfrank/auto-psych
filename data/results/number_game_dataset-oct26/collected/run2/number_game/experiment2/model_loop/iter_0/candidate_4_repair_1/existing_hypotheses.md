# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## dual_route_shared_factor_rule_exemplar  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3120.5

Refinement of dimension_specific_coherence_exemplar: people still judge a target by its recency-weighted exponential similarity to the remembered examples (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor), with each dimension's attention stretched by its own sensitivity to how much the examples resemble one another, but when the examples all share a common factor of 3 or more they also abstract the explicit rule "multiples of that factor" and consult it alongside similarity when answering. The single change is this rule as a separate source of evidence at the decision, not a dimension of similarity: it raises "yes" for multiples and lowers it for non-multiples regardless of how far they are in size, in proportion to how unlikely the shared factor would be by coincidence (larger factors and more examples), so far-away multiples of g are endorsed and nearby non-multiples rejected, closing the common-factor rule gap that similarity alone leaves.

## coherence_sharpened_decision_exemplar  — rank 1, 47.6 ± 14.9 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3168.1

Refinement of graded_factor_coherence_tuned_exemplar: people still judge a target by its recency-weighted exponential similarity to the remembered examples (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor), with attention stretched toward the respects in which the examples resemble one another, but how much the examples resemble one another also sets how decisively people turn that similarity into an answer. The single change is that set coherence sharpens the decision rule: after a coherent set (examples alike in size, digit, parity or factors) people answer near-deterministically from similarity, endorsing similar targets and rejecting dissimilar ones almost always, whereas after an incoherent set or a single example their answers stay noisy, which makes set-level yes rates more extreme than the incumbent predicts (the between-set yes-rate spread in the critique) and sharpens the "ends in d" and "multiples of g" gaps for coherent sets.

## coherence_sharpened_set_factor_exemplar  — rank 2, 50.2 ± 15.0 nats behind the best (3.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3170.7

Refinement of coherence_sharpened_decision_exemplar: people still judge a target by its recency-weighted exponential similarity to the remembered examples (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor), with attention stretched toward the respects in which the examples resemble one another and set coherence sharpening how decisively similarity is turned into an answer. The single change (the set-level factor from set_factor_tuned_attention_exemplar) is that when several examples share a common factor of 3 or more, people notice that factor and judge the factor part of similarity by whether the target is a multiple of the examples' shared factor (graded by how much of that factor the target shares), rather than by the factors it shares with each example separately — so after 12, 18, 30 the target 42 is close and 15 or 20 far, sharpening the "multiples of g" gap the critique reports as still too small.

## independent_retrieval_exemplar_search  — rank 3, 56.4 ± 18.1 nats behind the best (3.1× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3176.9

Refinement of graded_factor_coherence_tuned_exemplar: people still compare the target with each remembered example by exponential similarity (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor), with attention stretched toward the respects in which the examples resemble one another. The single change is the decision process. People do not add up familiarity and threshold it. They search memory example by example: each example is recalled (less often the longer ago it was shown) and independently accepts the target with probability equal to its similarity, and they answer "yes" as soon as any recalled example accepts it (otherwise they guess "yes" at a low base rate). So one highly similar example is enough for "yes", and every additional example is one more chance to accept, so yes rates rise with set size.

## factorized_rule_and_range_inference  — no comparison row

People are Bayesian concept learners who think the program's numbers obey both an arithmetic rule (a step pattern such as "even", "multiples of 3" or "ends in 7", or no pattern at all) and a numeric range, each inferred from the examples with the size principle, but — as a resource-saving distortion of joint inference — they work out the rule and the range as two separate, independent questions and say "yes" only to the extent that the target passes both. So a target is endorsed when it fits the inferred step pattern AND lies within the inferred range: far-away numbers sharing the examples' final digit or factor are rejected, as are nearby numbers that break the pattern, and the yes-probability is the product of the two separate judgements.

## shared_property_coincidence_rule  — no comparison row

People read the examples for the arithmetic properties they all have in common (same parity, same final digit, same decade, being multiples of the same number) and treat each shared property as evidence for a rule in proportion to how surprising it would be for that many examples to share it by coincidence. They then say "yes" to a target according to the surprise-weighted balance of shared properties it has versus lacks, discounted only by how far the target falls outside the range the examples span — no similarity to individual examples is computed.

## memoryless_win_stay_lose_shift_rules  — no comparison row

People test one candidate rule at a time while reading the examples in order, in a win-stay, lose-shift fashion: the first example suggests a single rule (a final digit, a multiple of some number, odd or even, or a range of sizes, smaller rules being favoured), they keep that rule as long as each new example fits it, and when an example contradicts it they discard it and pick a fresh rule suggested by that contradicting example alone, without re-checking the earlier ones. They then answer "yes" if the target obeys the rule they ended up holding, so the final judgement is driven by the last examples that forced a switch and by whichever rule survived from the start, and the same examples shown in a different order lead to different generalisations.

## dual_route_factor_and_digit_rule_exemplar  — no comparison row

Refinement of dual_route_shared_factor_rule_exemplar: people still judge a target by its recency-weighted exponential similarity to the remembered examples (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor, each dimension's attention stretched by how much the examples resemble one another), and still consult an abstracted explicit rule alongside similarity at the decision, but the rule route is not limited to shared factors. The single change is that when all the examples end in the same digit, people also abstract the explicit rule "numbers ending in that digit" and consult it as separate evidence, raising "yes" for targets ending in that digit and lowering it for the rest regardless of size distance, in proportion to how unlikely that shared ending would be by coincidence (more examples), closing the "ends in d" gap the critique reports (people apply the final-digit rule more sharply than final-digit similarity predicts).
