# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## sequence_momentum_span_exemplar  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -3100.8

People read the examples as a sequence the program is producing in the order shown and expect it to keep going in the direction it has been moving: beyond the span the examples fill in, a number lying ahead of the last-shown example in the direction of travel from the first example to the last feels much closer in size than an equally distant number lying back behind where the sequence started. So after 20, 40 people generalize to 50-60 more than to 0-10, but after 40, 20 they generalize to 10 more than to 50 — a momentum in the order of presentation, not a fixed upward preference, which produces the critique's recency (closer-to-last) advantage.

## recency_anchored_span_exemplar_2  — rank 1, 2.0 ± 4.7 nats behind the best (0.4× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3102.8

Refinement of set_size_scaled_magnitude_gradient (recency-weighted exponential similarity to the examples on size, final digit, parity and graded shared factor with coherence-stretched attention, the "multiples of g" and "ends in d" rules as separate evidence, a criterion that relaxes over the session, and a size gradient that broadens with the number of examples). The single change, adapting the incumbent's span-filling idea, is that people treat each earlier example as connected to the most recently shown one by a filled-in path the program travelled, so a target's size mismatch with an earlier example is how far it lies outside the stretch between that example and the last one (zero inside it), while its mismatch with the last example is its plain distance from it. Generalization thus fills the gaps between the examples yet stays centred on the latest example, so among targets inside or near a set, those nearer the last-shown example are endorsed more than those nearer the first (the recency gap the critique reports, which an order-blind span cannot produce).

## recency_anchored_span_exemplar  — rank 2, 4.6 ± 4.4 nats behind the best (1.1× dse: statistically tied with the best), ELPD-LOO -3105.5

## independent_retrieval_exemplar_search  — rank 21, 76.0 ± 17.7 nats behind the best (4.3× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3176.9

Refinement of graded_factor_coherence_tuned_exemplar: people still compare the target with each remembered example by exponential similarity (size, final digit, parity, graded shared factor), with attention stretched toward the respects in which the examples resemble one another. The single change is the decision process. People do not add up familiarity and threshold it. They search memory example by example: each example is recalled (less often the longer ago it was shown) and independently accepts the target with probability equal to its similarity, and they answer "yes" as soon as any recalled example accepts it (otherwise they guess "yes" at a low base rate). ## shared_property_coincidence_rule  — rank 22, 207.9 ± 25.4 nats behind the best (8.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3308.8

People read the examples for the arithmetic properties they all have in common (same parity, same final digit, same decade, being multiples of the same number) and treat each shared property as evidence for a rule in proportion to how surprising it would be for that many examples to share it by coincidence. They then say "yes" to a target according to the surprise-weighted balance of shared properties it has versus lacks, discounted only by how far the target falls outside the range the examples span — no similarity to individual examples is computed.

## memoryless_win_stay_lose_shift_rules  — rank 23, 255.0 ± 28.3 nats behind the best (9.0× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3355.8

People test one candidate rule at a time while reading the examples in order, in a win-stay, lose-shift fashion: the first example suggests a single rule (a final digit, a multiple of some number, odd or even, or a range of sizes, smaller rules being favoured), they keep that rule as long as each new example fits it, and when an example contradicts it they discard it and pick a fresh rule suggested by that contradicting example alone, without re-checking the earlier ones. They then answer "yes" if the target obeys the rule they ended up holding, so the final judgement is driven by the last examples that forced a switch and by whichever rule survived from the start, and the same examples shown in a different order lead to different generalisations.

## factorized_rule_and_range_inference  — rank 24, 330.6 ± 31.9 nats behind the best (10.4× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3431.5

People are Bayesian concept learners who think the program's numbers obey both an arithmetic rule (a step pattern such as "even", "multiples of 3" or "ends in 7", or no pattern at all) and a numeric range, each inferred from the examples with the size principle, but — as a resource-saving distortion of joint inference — they work out the rule and the range as two separate, independent questions and say "yes" only to the extent that the target passes both. So a target is endorsed when it fits the inferred step pattern AND lies within the inferred range: far-away numbers sharing the examples' final digit or factor are rejected, as are nearby numbers that break the pattern, and the yes-probability is the product of the two separate judgements.

## log_scale_magnitude_range_inference  — rank 25, 374.6 ± 34.9 nats behind the best (10.7× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -3475.4

People do not look for arithmetic rules or compare the target with each example; they infer the range of magnitudes the program produces, reasoning about size on a compressed, logarithmic mental number line. They favour the narrowest range (in ratio terms) that covers all the examples, more strongly the more examples there are, and say "yes" in proportion to their belief that the target falls inside that range — so generalization extends further above the largest example than below the smallest (equal ratios span more numbers upward), and tightly clustered sets are generalized narrowly while widely spread sets are generalized broadly.
