# Existing hypotheses

Each model below is ONE cognitive hypothesis, with how it stands on the current data by ELPD-LOO (best first). `elpd_diff ± dse` is a model's deficit against the best and the standard error of that difference (with the responses to each stimulus pair counted together, as they are correlated): within about 2·dse the two are statistically tied on this data; beyond it the model has lost. "PSIS-LOO unreliable" means the estimate itself is untrustworthy (too many high-Pareto-k trials), not that the model is bad. Propose a hypothesis that is genuinely different from these, or a refinement of a single one of them — never a combination of several.

## saturating_criterion_relaxation  — rank 0, the best model on this data, ELPD-LOO -4712.9

Refinement of enumerated_list_concept_exemplar (all else unchanged: recency-weighted similarity on size, final digit, parity and graded shared factor with coherence-stretched attention, the "multiples of g", "ends in d" and fixed-list concepts, the filled-in span, sequence momentum and zigzag breadth). The single change is the time course of the relaxing yes-criterion: people loosen their criterion quickly over the first stretch of the session as they settle into a task with no feedback, and then it levels off at a stable, more lenient setting rather than continuing to relax steadily to the end. ## recollection_shortcut_span_exemplar  — rank 10, 23.7 ± 8.1 nats behind the best (2.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4736.6

People answer by two routes at the decision: if they explicitly recollect that the target is one of the numbers the program actually showed, they say "yes" outright on that recollection alone (an all-or-none, high-threshold recognition that succeeds on some fraction of such trials); only when no such recollection occurs do they fall back on weighing graded familiarity (the simplest current span-filling dual-route exemplar evidence) against their criterion. Repeated examples are therefore endorsed far more than numbers just 1-3 away, by a boost that familiarity alone cannot produce (the critique's exact-example gap).

## sequence_momentum_span_exemplar  — rank 11, 23.9 ± 7.5 nats behind the best (3.2× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4736.8

People read the examples as a sequence the program is producing in the order shown and expect it to keep going in the direction it has been moving: beyond the span the examples fill in, a number lying ahead of the last-shown example in the direction of travel from the first example to the last feels much closer in size than an equally distant number lying back behind where the sequence started. So after 20, 40 people generalize to 50-60 more than to 0-10, but after 40, 20 they generalize to 10 more than to 50 — a momentum in the order of presentation, not a fixed upward preference, which produces the critique's recency (closer-to-last) advantage.

## scalar_variability_exemplar_traces  — rank 15, 255.7 ± 30.2 nats behind the best (8.5× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -4968.6

People judge a target by its recency-weighted similarity to the stored examples (closeness in size, shared final digit, shared parity and shared factors), but each example is stored as an approximate magnitude whose fuzziness grows in proportion to its size, as in the scalar variability of the approximate number system. So the size-similarity gradient is sharp around a small example and broad around a large one: a target 10 away from the example 8 feels far, while a target 10 away from 80 feels close, and a target identical to a small example stands out sharply from its neighbours. This predicts fewer "yes" answers beyond the examples when the nearest example is small than when it is large, for the same linear gap.

## arithmetic_operation_generative_program  — rank 16, 301.8 ± 33.8 nats behind the best (8.9× dse: distinguishable from the best — it has lost on this data), ELPD-LOO -5014.7

People imagine the hidden program as a generator that makes each new number from one of the numbers it has already produced (most often its latest) by a single elementary arithmetic operation: repeating it, rescaling it by a ratio (small ratio changes being far likelier than large ones), jumping by tens so the final digit is kept, or moving to another number that shares a factor with it. They answer "yes" in proportion to how probable it is that such a program would produce the target, relative to an arbitrary number. Because rescaling is a matter of ratios, the same linear gap is far less likely among small numbers, and below the examples, than among large numbers and above them.
